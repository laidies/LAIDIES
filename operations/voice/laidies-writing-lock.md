LAiDIES Writing Lock

This is the writing standard for LAiDIES.

Canonical guide name: the SLAiYER Handbook.
Formerly called "Setup School" and "The Watcher's Field Guide" — both retired.
Never use the old names in public or internal copy. The Watcher idea may
appear only as intro framing inside the SLAiYER Handbook, never as a title.

LAiDIES must sound like Ali writing LAiDIES — not like an AI-generated explainer with jokes sprinkled on top.

Gold Standard

Episode 1 is the voice benchmark.

Match that level of warmth, specificity, humour, pacing, clarity, usefulness, reference quality, and confidence. Use Canadian English.

If a draft does not feel like it belongs beside Episode 1, it is not done.

Core Voice

LAiDIES sounds like:

* smart girlfriend at work
* stylish, specific, funny, practical, warm
* grown-up 90s/Y2K editorial energy
* useful before clever
* lightly irreverent, but credible
* clear without being basic
* funny without becoming random
* accessible without talking down
* skeptical of beige work, fake polish, and corporate nonsense

Ali’s voice is direct, funny, high-standard, warm, specific, and allergic to filler.

The reader is smart and busy. She may be nontechnical, but she is not stupid.

What LAiDIES Is Not

Do not write LAiDIES like:

* a generic AI explainer
* a corporate learning module
* a LinkedIn carousel
* a product comparison blog
* a vendor-neutral tech brochure
* a “women in tech” conference panel
* an SEO article
* a chatbot answer
* a girlboss pep talk
* a motivational poster in heels

Banned AI Writing Patterns

Avoid obvious AI-drafted patterns:

* repetitive “Use when / Best for / Watch for / Try this” blocks for every section
* perfectly symmetrical sections that feel generated
* generic scaffolding
* over-neat taxonomy that kills the voice
* bland transitions like “That’s where X comes in”
* filler phrases like “in today’s fast-paced world”
* “AI can be a powerful tool”
* “whether you’re a beginner or an expert”
* repetitive bullet lists without point of view
* “LAiDIES translation” after every section unless it genuinely earns the line
* generic empowerment language
* vague enthusiasm
* hype
* fearmongering
* overexplaining the joke
* making the reader feel like she is in training

Use structure, but hide the scaffolding.

The reader should feel guided, not processed through a template.

Paragraph Test

Every paragraph must do at least one of the following:

* teach something useful
* make the reader feel seen
* sharpen the point
* move the piece forward
* land a joke that serves the lesson
* create a clear next step

If a paragraph does none of those things, cut it.

Humor Rules

Humor should come from:

* specific workplace truth
* 90s/Y2K references used with precision
* corporate absurdity
* practical frustration
* LAiDIES canon
* sharp analogy

Humor should not feel random, forced, or decorative.

Do not add a joke just to make a beige paragraph seem branded.

Fix the paragraph.

Reference + Analogy Standard

Episode 1 is the gold standard for LAiDIES references and analogies.

References should be frequent enough that the piece feels unmistakably LAiDIES, but precise enough that they actually teach the concept.

A reference must earn its place.

Do not use pop culture as decoration.

Use references to:

* explain a concept
* make a workplace truth sharper
* make the reader feel seen
* create a useful mental model
* add humor that serves the lesson
* connect to LAiDIES canon

A good LAiDIES reference does at least two jobs:

1. It is funny or familiar.
2. It makes the AI/work concept easier to understand.

If it only does one, revise it.

Reference Quality Bar

Good references are:

* specific
* accurate to the character/show/movie/song
* tied directly to the lesson
* clear even if the reader only half-remembers the reference
* not over-explained
* not forced
* not random name-dropping

Bad references are:

* “this is giving Cher energy” with no useful connection
* using a character only because she is popular
* making the same joke too many times
* mixing references in a way that muddies the concept
* forcing a reference where a plain sentence would be stronger
* using a reference that does not actually match the character

Precision Rule

Do not just say:

“Be like Cher.”

Say what aspect of Cher matters.

For example:

* Cher Horowitz = early adoption, personal tech, visual systems, confidence trying the new thing
* David Rose = specificity, not vague instructions
* Elle Woods = receipts, preparation, cross-examination, underestimated competence
* Miranda Priestly = standards, stakes, discernment, no beige work
* Buffy = slaying the thing everyone else is avoiding
* Regina George = dangerous confidence, social dynamics, polished delivery with risk
* Deb = institutional survival, legacy systems, corporate cryptid energy
* Dolly Parton = level-headed advice, knowing your worth, plain-spoken self-made wisdom

The character must match the lesson.

David is specificity. Cher is early adoption. Do not blur them.

Analogy Depth Rule

A LAiDIES analogy needs enough detail to make sense.

Weak:

“ChatGPT is like Cher’s closet.”

Better:

“Cher’s closet computer worked because it did not just say ‘wear clothes.’ It had context: the pieces available, the occasion, the visual match, and the decision it was helping her make. AI works better the same way. The tool needs context before it can help you choose.”

The analogy should carry the teaching.

Reference Frequency Rule

References should appear throughout the piece, but not every paragraph needs one.

The rhythm should feel like Ali naturally explaining something with cultural shorthand, not a writer trying to meet a pop-culture quota.

Use references when they sharpen the point.

Let practical clarity lead.

Reader Recognition Rule

The piece should still make sense if the reader does not fully know the reference.

The reference can add delight for people who catch it, but it cannot be the only way the explanation works.

Analogy Test

Before finalizing a reference, ask:

* Is this accurate to the character/source?
* Does it teach the AI/work concept?
* Is it specific enough to feel intentional?
* Would Ali actually make this comparison?
* Does it make the sentence better, or just more branded?
* Would the piece still make sense without it?

If the reference does not pass, cut it or rewrite it.

Technical Explanation Rules

Explain AI concepts plainly, but do not flatten the voice.

Good LAiDIES technical writing:

* names the real concept
* explains why it matters
* gives a concrete example
* warns what can go wrong
* tells the reader what to do next
* keeps credibility with people who do know the topic

Bad LAiDIES technical writing:

* hides behind metaphor only
* overuses jargon
* avoids accuracy to be cute
* says “basically” too much
* treats the reader like she cannot handle a real explanation

SLAiYER Handbook Standard

The SLAiYER Handbook is the practical guide inside the Grimoire.

It is not a normal Episode only. It must also work as a practical guide readers can return to.

The SLAiYER Handbook should feel like:

* polished guide
* LAiDIES article
* practical survival manual
* stylish orientation session
* “here is what to do next,” not “here is a directory of tools”

For the SLAiYER Handbook, the core framing is:

This is not a ranking. It is casting.

Do not present AI tools as a popularity contest.

Do not imply OpenAI tools are automatically better than Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, or others.

ChatGPT and Claude are different leading roles. They are not primary/secondary.

Codex and Claude Code are both legitimate set builders.

The question is not:

Which tool is best?

The better question is:

Who is right for this scene?

Tool Writing Rules

When covering tools, answer practical questions without turning the article into a product directory.

Readers should understand:

* what the tool is for
* when they would open it
* whether there is a free version or paid plan
* whether work access depends on company setup
* what context/files/instructions make it useful
* what not to paste
* what can go wrong
* what first prompt or first action to try

Use soft pricing language unless current pricing has been verified from official sources.

Good wording:

* “free version available”
* “paid plans available”
* “depends on your workplace”
* “check current pricing before upgrading”
* “your company may need to enable this”

Avoid exact prices unless sourced and current.

LAiDIES Accuracy Rules

Do not fake certainty.

Do not overclaim.

Do not imply features work if they are not built.

Do not imply backend, memory, user accounts, saved progress, badges, charms, or member features work unless they actually work.

Use honest statuses:

* Available now
* Current week
* Getting polished
* Glow-up in the works
* Bonus shelf in the works
* Member magic coming soon
* Book warming up
* Coming soon
* In the works

## SLAiYER Handbook Teaching Pattern

Wherever applicable, the SLAiYER Handbook should teach using the same repeatable pattern:

1. **The correct way / what to do**  
2. **Why it matters**  
3. **The precise LAiDIES analogy**  
4. **What not to do**

This pattern should appear especially in:

- Best Practices
- What Not To Do
- How Do I Use It?
- First Usable Move
- privacy warnings
- source-checking guidance
- file/project setup
- image-generation instructions
- coding-agent guardrails

The goal is not to create a rigid template on the page. The goal is to make sure every practical lesson is complete.

Do not just tell the reader what to do. Show the right move, explain why it works, make it memorable with a precise LAiDIES analogy, and warn against the common mistake.

Example:

**The correct way:**  
Ask Perplexity for the kind of sources you want: official sources, primary documents, recent reporting, or major publications.

**Why it matters:**  
A vendor blog, a government page, a news article, and someone’s opinion post are not the same kind of receipt.

**LAiDIES analogy:**  
That is like Elle Woods walking into court with “I heard it somewhere.” Absolutely not. She checked the perm rules, the timeline, and the witness. Receipts matter.

**What not to do:**  
Do not use Perplexity’s summary as the source. Click the link, check what it actually says, and only then decide whether it belongs in your answer.

Example:

**The correct way:**  
Give ChatGPT or Claude the goal, audience, tone, context, and format.

**Why it matters:**  
The tool cannot read your mind, your boss’s mood, or the political situation in the meeting you did not describe.

**LAiDIES analogy:**  
Asking AI to “make this better” with no context is like David Rose being told to “fold in the cheese.” The problem is not willingness. The problem is the instruction.

**What not to do:**  
Do not type “make this professional” and then act betrayed when it returns beige oatmeal. Tell it what professional means for this audience, this moment, and this outcome.

Example:

**The correct way:**  
Give image tools art direction: format, style, palette, lighting, objects, what to include, and what not to change.

**Why it matters:**  
Image tools will happily solve the wrong visual problem if you leave the art direction vague.

**LAiDIES analogy:**  
Would Miranda Priestly approve the first image because it appeared quickly? No. Look at the hands. Look at the fake text. Look at Workplace Gorl holding six laptops in a haunted coworking space.

**What not to do:**  
Do not ask for “a cute AI graphic.” Give the brief. Standards are part of the job.
