## AIDB edition — attributed reasoning preserved

AIDB's main argument is that “will AI take all our jobs?” is too blunt. Its synthesis of Every's first 25 Thesis Statements says AI may change three things at once: what people and teams attempt, how organizations coordinate work, and which human capacities become valuable.

The reasoning chain is:

1. Stable, repeatable and well-framed tasks are increasingly automatable.
2. That can move human work toward framing, checking, judgment and consequential decisions, but does not prove that displaced workers automatically receive better jobs.
3. “Work” includes caregiving, household work, identity and purpose, not only paid employment.
4. The more useful distinction may be **efficiency AI**—doing existing work faster or cheaper—versus **opportunity AI**—making new work or workflows possible.
5. Organizations may become human-agent systems. AIDB highlights shared, continuously updated status, goals, permissions and constraints as coordination infrastructure, while correctly noting that synchronization is not the same as alignment.
6. The human value proposition may shift toward judgment, discernment, connection, taste, responsibility and self-knowledge.

This is a valuable attributed synthesis, not representative labour-market evidence. The source set is selected from frontier builders, founders and investors; it is optimistic and future-facing. AIDB itself acknowledges broad uncertainty. Exact Every collection and primary authored essays by Dan Shipper, Tom Critchlow, Sari Azout and Tina He were recovered, but empirical job outcomes, worker power, distributional effects and failures remain missing.

## Audience-gap translation

AIDB assumes knowledge of agents, models, automation, agentic workflows, tools, permissions, task routing, orchestration, shared status and machine-to-machine systems. It also assumes readers have meaningful AI experience and some influence over organizational design.

LAiDIES should enter through the decision chain: **a task is assigned → a model and product receive tools, data and permissions → the workflow acts → evidence is checked → a person still owns the consequential decision.** The reader question is not “which new jargon should I learn?” It is: which parts of my work are stable and verifiable, which require judgment, who gets the saved time or new opportunity, who bears the risk, and who can stop or change the system when it is wrong?

Relevant maintained concepts include automation versus AI, model versus product/system, agents, tools and permissions, evaluation, governance, agency and decision rights. These links explain the news; they do not turn every article into a lesson or automatically add a Fundamentals chapter.
