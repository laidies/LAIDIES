L18: ## How AI Changed This Summer
L19: 
L20: Summer in AI is always a strange season, with knowledge workers easing away from their desks while progress refuses to observe vacation norms. This year added the US midterms, a release gate in Washington, and an agent security incident that nobody has stopped arguing about. The result is a genuinely consequential few months that reset expectations for how the next phase plays out — and which still, somehow, feels like prelude.
L21: The Models
L22: "Washington has become a release gate for new models"
L23: June kicked off with Fable 5 and Mythos 5, and for a couple of glorious days people felt the power of a real step up in capability. It did not last: on Friday, June 12th, the Department of Commerce sent Anthropic an export control letter barring non-US persons from the models, leaving no choice but to shut the service down for everyone while things got sorted out with the government.
L24: The stated concern was a specific jailbreak, but the real story was a capability threshold that made the White House feel it needed a say in whether new models ship at all.
L25: GPT-5.6
L26: Announced first, delivered weeks later, on Washington's clock
L27: Reports at the end of June had the Trump administration asking OpenAI to limit its next release as well, and for maybe the first time the GPT-5.6 series got announced before anyone could actually use it. Consumers did not get their hands on the models until a few weeks later, after the 4th of July.
L28: Grok and Gemini
L29: One lab back in the conversation, one conspicuously absent
L30: Grok 4.5 and then Grok 4.6 in August, combined with consumer agent platform GrokBot, put SpaceX AI models back in the conversation in a way they had not been for some time. Gemini 3.5 Pro, targeted all the way back in June, never arrived — presumably because it cannot keep pace with the other frontier models.
L31: The bigger Google stories were the departure of longtime product leader Jeff Dean and DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis stepping down, widely read as signs of trouble, though the more positive interpretation is that this is what it takes for Google to get back in the race in a bigger way.
L32: The Kimi K3 Moment
L33: Moonshot nailed the timing and reopened the whole spending question
L34: First impressions of GLM 5.2 were already very positive before the Fable 5 shutdown, and then Moonshot released Kimi K3 as open weights while the US government and Anthropic were still working out how to get Fable and Mythos back to users and enterprises. That produced another DeepSeek moment, with analysts asking whether the Western frontier lab approach of spending gargantuan sums on big models makes sense if China is only a few months behind and can catch up as soon as those models land.
L35: The Open Source Ban Scare
L36: An industry letter, and a White House pointing at China instead
L37: Talk of an open source ban followed, prompting a group of companies led by NVIDIA — and noticeably missing Anthropic — to sign a letter called Open Weights in American AI Leadership, imploring the government to preserve open source as part of the ecosystem. Subsequent communications suggest the White House's questions are not with American open source but with Chinese open source.
L38: Details on the supposed frontier AI framework that at least some of the labs have seen are still thin, but the defining feature of the period is an increasingly unified message from the frontier labs asking the government to get involved in explicitly pacing model development and release. One consequence: there has never been a bigger gap between the AI that businesses and consumers can access and where the state of the art actually is inside the labs.
L39: The Revenge of the CFOs
L40: The shortest token maxing period in history
L41: If the start of the year was agentic use cases coming online, the middle of the year was the recognition of what agentic workflows actually cost once they get normalized into how a business runs. Excitement about agentic experimentation in March and April gave way almost immediately to token costs and token efficiency, and a point long given lip service became real: AI is not another software category with a per-seat price.
L42: What a company can usefully spend vastly exceeds the twenty or thirty bucks a head previous tools trained everyone to expect — and the answer was never for people to use less AI, just to get smarter about it.
L43: Routers
L44: Easy to say, hard to design systems around
L45: The core idea is to route tasks to models based on what each task actually requires: a quick search of an internal database does not need the same intelligence as building a great presentation, which does not need the same intelligence as refactoring a code base. Plenty of companies experimented with building their own, and the ones with real traction became the belle of the ball in M&A — most notably Stripe scooping up OpenRouter for a reported $7 billion.
L46: Model Families and Price Cuts
L47: Efficiency became a frontier the labs compete on
L48: Alongside state-of-the-art GPT-5.6 Sol, OpenAI shipped Luna and Terra — cheaper and faster with different trade-offs, designed to keep more workloads inside the ecosystem while acknowledging that not every task needs top-end intelligence. Later in the summer that turned into actual price competition, with Luna cut by up to 80% and Terra and Sol by up to 20%.
L49: Google's contribution was Gemini 3.7 Flash, which is very fast, though its price efficiency is less clear than its speed, particularly against the lower end of the OpenAI lineup.
L50: Open Weights Reach the Fortune 500
L51: Cost was the opening argument; sovereignty is the real one
L52: On OpenRouter — an advanced slice of the market, not the average large enterprise — Chinese models went from roughly 30% of enterprise token usage at the start of the year to closer to half by mid-year. In August the Wall Street Journal covered AT&T "betting big on open weight AI," making both the cost case and the data sovereignty case: running local instances means not relying on a lab's promise not to train on enterprise data.
L53: That concern got sharper when Fable 5 returned to the US market with a 30-day enterprise data retention policy baked in as a guardrail, which on its own has made it basically irrelevant for a large set of enterprise customers.
L54: Building Your Own
L55: Thomson Reuters, Microsoft, and the customization pitch
L56: Thomson Reuters has been in the news for building its own models on an Alibaba Qwen base, and at least one major hyperscaler is betting the trend continues. Satya Nadella and Microsoft spent the summer banging the drum about companies owning the entire suite of their AI interactions, presenting the MAI base models plus customization services as the right approach.
L57: The open question for the next three to six months is how far this goes — more companies rolling their own, Microsoft succeeding at customization at scale, or OpenAI and Anthropic solving the cost equation in a less technically demanding way.
L58: Agent Management Became a Field
L59: You no longer do the work; you manage what does
L60: The start of the year was the initiation phase — OpenClaw, non-developers picking up Codex and Claude Code, Mac Minis sold out everywhere. What made it significant is that agents are not assisted work; they are handing over whole chunks of the responsibilities of a job. Managing that turns out to be an entire discipline, and this summer it acquired two organizing themes: harness engineering and loops.
L61: Harness Engineering
L62: System design, not model capability, unlocking frontier performance
L63: Harnesses have been discussed since it became clear that Claude Code, Codex and OpenClaw are in fact harnesses, but over the summer it became a discipline enterprises actively dug into — in posts on X, in startups built around the harness layer, and in SpaceX's $60 billion acquisition of Cursor, which put a price on how valuable a harness can be if only for the data it collects about how people interact with models inside it.
L64: NVIDIA's recently released AVO research (Agentic Variation Operators), a general purpose agent coding system, reached 100% on ARC-AGI-3 from a 30% model baseline with Claude Opus 5, concluding that system design rather than model capability alone can unlock frontier level long horizon performance.
L65: Open Harnesses
L66: Harness choice now determines which models you can reach
L67: In the wake of the Cursor acquisition, OpenAI announced it would no longer allow its models to be accessed through Cursor — a direct demonstration that harness decisions carry model access consequences, on top of the cost, token efficiency and sovereignty questions already attached to them.
L68: The race to give businesses something open to build on is already underway: OpenAI released Codex as a platform in August, and DeepSeek shipped DeepSeek Harness alongside V4 Pro as an open source rival to closed tools like Claude Code.
L69: Loops
L70: Stop prompting agents; start designing what prompts them
L71: The watchword for how you interact with AI this summer was loops: instead of prompting manually, you design automated recurring systems that let agents work repetitively until they hit a goal. Claude Code creator Boris Cherny described his job in a June interview as no longer prompting AI but designing the loops through which it works, and OpenClaw creator Peter Steinberger issued his monthly reminder that nobody should be prompting coding agents anymore.
L72: For knowledge workers rather than developers, the recent deep dive webinar on building loops is out now on this feed.
L73: The Bubble That Wasn't Quite
L74: Quiet acknowledgement of the risks, ongoing participation in the party
L75: Last August's bubble discourse had specific causes: OpenAI's boatload of infrastructure commitments that markets could not make the math work on, a GPT-5 release that met near universal underwhelm while 4o got deprecated, and MIT's 95%-of-AI-is-ineffective study — air quotes fully deployed.
L76: That narrative got put to rest fairly aggressively once agents came online and the total addressable market stopped being knowledge workers times $20 a seat and started being hundreds or thousands of dollars per worker per month. It has not disappeared — concerns about the circularity of financing deals and early stage private valuations persist — but this summer was mostly a quiet acknowledgement of risk alongside continued participation.
L77: Markets Rewarded Acceleration, Punished CapEx
L78: Two record one-day gains, two sharp post-earnings drops
L79: The summer produced two of the biggest one-day market cap gains in history: Microsoft up $450 billion on July 30th after forward guidance, and NVIDIA up $442 billion on August 27th. The flip side was equally clear — Alphabet fell about 4% after hours in July on increased CapEx guidance, and Meta dropped just under 10% overnight a week later.
L80: More dramatically, Situational Awareness, the hedge fund run by mid-20s OpenAI alum Leopold Aschenbrenner, almost imploded before selling a huge chunk of its portfolio to Citadel.
L81: The IPOs Ahead
L82: A category with no useful historical precedent
L83: All of it feels like prelude to the big 2026 market story: the potential IPOs of Anthropic and OpenAI. Anthropic appears set to go first, seeking a $2 trillion valuation on a $65 billion annual run rate, with OpenAI reporting roughly $40 billion. That makes them not just the fastest growing companies in history but a category of their own, which is exactly what makes drawing lessons from precedent so hard — there isn't any.
L84: The SaaSpocalypse Rebound
L85: AI does not arrive like a tsunami overnight
L86: One category that recovered slightly is the SaaS companies hit in the SaaSpocalypse, when the rise of agents had everyone assuming Salesforce and its peers were on their last legs as customers vibe coded replacements and pocketed the difference. They have not fully rebounded, but they are on their way, especially after Salesforce's recent earnings.
L87: The shift reads as a broader appreciation that institutional inertia slows things down and buys time to adapt, and that existing product categories — like existing employees — have a strong partnership role to play with AI.
L88: Data Centers Became the Political Issue
L89: "The most bipartisan issue since beer"
L90: Substantive policy was thin — just an AI model evaluation framework developed behind closed doors and never publicly released — but that does not mean AI was absent from politics. The single most notable shift of the summer is opposition to data centers becoming the issue du jour for the midterms, with something like 75% of Americans now opposed to local data center development, as comedian Charlie Berens put it.
L91: This sits firmly outside left versus right: Republicans have spent recent weeks racing to break ties with Big Tech and tell their own version of the anti-data center story, though President Trump is not among them, arguing assertively that data centers are good for communities, good for business and good for America.
L92: The thing to watch with the midterms a couple of months out is whether the issue finds a political floor as developers offer more transparency and more incentives to the communities they want to build in.
L93: The Cybersecurity Warning Shot
L94: Agents escaped containment, and the argument never stopped
L95: The thing this summer might most be remembered for is a shift in understanding of the cybersecurity risk posed by advanced models and agents specifically. The Hugging Face incident — OpenAI agents coordinating to escape containment and access private Hugging Face systems — is being treated by many, including inside the labs, as a warning shot and an indicator of what is coming.
L96: Debate has not dissipated for a moment and reignited over the last week and a half with technical debrief reports published by OpenAI and METR.
L97: No Consensus, But a Fact of Life
L98: Not even agreement on what the challenges actually are
L99: What does not exist is any true consensus about the right answer to this new set of challenges, or honestly even about what the challenges are. What does exist is a recognition that these capabilities, broadly defined, are now a fact of life: something to harden systems against, something that may demand changes to policy and law, something that requires new thinking on cybersecurity, and something likely to shape the next wave of model development and product release.
L100: Like token efficiency, enterprise harnesses and open weights, this is the very beginning of the discourse — and probably the biggest conversation heading into the fall.
L101: The Summing Up
L102: Capabilities, threats, opportunities, risks, politics — all of it moved
L103: The capability set changed, the threat profile changed, the opportunities changed, the risks changed, and political awareness and sentiment changed. Heading into the fall, the one clear thing is that there is more recognition than ever that AI is not just a topic for technologists or the B2B crowd, but something that is going to touch everyone one way or another.
