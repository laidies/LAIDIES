# Fifteen million Gemini conversations do not show AI doing fifteen million jobs

Google has published its first ATLAS report, built from nearly 15 million
de-identified interactions across the Gemini app, Google AI Mode and the free
Gemini API. Its most useful finding is also its least sensational: in Google's
sample, people mostly used AI to help with parts of tasks. Fewer than 10% of
work interactions were classified as full automation.

Picture a coffee shop where AI is taking orders, checking recipes and helping
with the schedule. ATLAS counts where that assistance appears; it does not
prove the AI ran the whole shop, improved the coffee or increased profit.

The Rewind Era equivalent is the office PC arriving on every desk. Seeing
people use Word, email and spreadsheets across many occupations tells us work
is changing. It does not mean the computer performed every job end to end. The
analogy stops at measurement: ATLAS infers tasks from conversations using
Google's own automated classifiers.

That caveat matters. The study covers two weeks of selected Google products,
excludes paid enterprise API conversations from granular analysis, and cannot
tell whether a conversation saved time or produced a successful result.
Classification is also much more reliable at broad levels than at exact tasks.

The real takeaway is not “automation is overhyped” or “jobs are safe.” It is
that the current evidence points to selective assistance inside jobs, while the
big questions—productivity, job redesign, who benefits and who is left
out—still need outcome data.

Sources: [full Google ATLAS report](https://ai.google/static/documents/GoogleATLASv1.pdf)
and [Google's overview](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/research/understanding-the-ai-economy/).
