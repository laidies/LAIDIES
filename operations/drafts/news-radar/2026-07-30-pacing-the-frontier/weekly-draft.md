# The Weekly: Who can pull the brake when no one can slow alone?

The Pacing the Frontier statement and public OpenAI/Anthropic support are best
understood as a collective-action problem, not a pause announcement.

Frontier labs compete for customers, talent, investment and geopolitical
advantage. Even if every lab privately preferred more time to test a new
capability, the first one to slow alone could lose while rivals continue.
That creates a race in which individual incentives and the shared outcome can
point in opposite directions.

The statement asks government to help develop an international coordination
option. It leaves the hard engineering and political questions open:

- What measured capability triggers pacing?
- Who can inspect proprietary systems and compute?
- How is compliance verified across countries?
- Does the mechanism govern training, release, access or all three?
- How are open-weight models handled after copies spread?
- Who decides when development resumes?

Existing proposals answer smaller parts of this puzzle. The U.S. FRONTIER Act
would require transparency, audits and incident reporting for major developers,
but it is not an international release brake. Company safety frameworks can
set internal thresholds, but they do not solve the incentive to race another
lab or country.

The durable lesson is that “slow down” is not yet a policy. It is a label for
several different interventions whose legitimacy and effectiveness depend on
measurement, verification, participation and accountability.
