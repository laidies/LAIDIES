Chapter 1 — Meet the parts of the system

First, stop calling the whole thing “the AI.” The app you open, the model inside it, the company behind it and the tools it can reach are different parts. That distinction is not pedantry; it tells you what to fix.

AI system
An AI system receives input and produces an output such as content, a prediction, a recommendation or a decision. The system you experience may combine models, an interface, instructions, data sources, tools, permissions and human controls.

Think of a workplace team: the finished work depends on the people, information, equipment and approval process together. Where it stops: an AI system is software and machines, not a team of people with judgment or responsibility.

In real life. The handover draft depends on the app, model, notes, policy, tool access and your check—not on one invisible “AI brain.”

This is the complete container around every other concept in this book.

Provider
A provider develops or operates an AI model, product or service.

A car manufacturer and a taxi company can both affect your journey. Where it stops: AI providers may build several layers at once, so the roles are not always cleanly separated.

In real life. A provider may set which models an app offers, what data controls exist and which tools are available.

The provider sits behind parts of the system; it is not the model, product or answer itself.

Product / app
The product or app is what you open. It can control the interface, account, model choices, instructions, available context, tools and settings.

Cher’s wardrobe computer in Clueless is a useful product analogy: the screen, clothing catalogue, matching rules and selection mechanism work together to create the experience. Where it stops: an AI product can generate and act, not merely match stored outfits, and its model is only one component.

In real life. Two apps can use related models yet give different handover drafts because one app supplies web search, saved preferences or different instructions.

The product receives your request and assembles what reaches the model.

Model
A model is the trained mathematical component that transforms input into output.

Think of the mechanism inside a Walkman: essential to what happens, but not the cassette you chose, the buttons you pressed or the headphones you hear through. Where it stops: models perform learned mathematical transformations; generative models such as LLMs use probabilities to produce content, while other model types may classify, rank, forecast or detect.

In real life. The language model turns the supplied handover instructions and context into a draft. It does not automatically know which policy is current.

The model sits inside the product, after context is assembled and before the output returns to you.

Model types
Different models are built for different transformations: language generation, image generation, speech recognition, classification, forecasting, recommendation, ranking, object detection or control.

A toolbox contains different tools for different jobs. Where it stops: models are not fixed hand tools; one model may perform several tasks and products may combine models.

In real life. One product might transcribe a recorded meeting with a speech model, summarize it with a language model and classify follow-up tasks with another model.

Model type tells you what transformation is being attempted, not whether the whole product is suitable or accurate.

Large language model (LLM)
An LLM is a model trained to work with language. It reads and produces text in small pieces called tokens; Chapter 2 explains tokens in full.

It is an extremely capable pattern-completion system for language. Where it stops: “completion” does not mean simple autocomplete, and fluent language does not prove understanding or truth.

In real life. An LLM can draft the handover email in your preferred tone while still inventing a plausible commitment that the policy never made.

An LLM is one model type, not a synonym for every kind of AI.

Generative AI
Generative AI produces new content—such as text, images, audio, video or code—from learned patterns and current input.

It resembles creating a new collage from patterns learned across many examples. Where it stops: the model is not literally cutting and storing pieces of original works, and the output may not trace cleanly to a source.

In real life. The draft email is newly generated language, not a policy passage retrieved word for word.

“Generative” describes the kind of output; it does not mean verified, original in the legal sense or correct.

Multimodal
A multimodal system can work with more than one kind of material, such as text, images and audio.

It is like receiving a written brief, a photograph and a voice note for the same job. Where it stops: accepting a format does not guarantee complete or correct interpretation.

In real life. You can give the product a photographed whiteboard and typed policy, but must still check names, dates and small labels against the originals.

Multimodal input broadens what can enter the model; it does not remove the evidence check.

Tool
A tool gives the system a capability beyond generating from the model alone: search, retrieval, calculation, code execution or action in another service.

Miss Jeeves can answer from what is on her desk or use the catalogue, calculator or scanner. Each tool changes what she can do. Where it stops: AI tools can carry permissions, external data and automated consequences; the model using one is not a responsible librarian.

In real life. A search tool can fetch the current service policy; an email tool could save or send the handover.

Tools connect around the model. Having one available does not prove it was used, or used well.
