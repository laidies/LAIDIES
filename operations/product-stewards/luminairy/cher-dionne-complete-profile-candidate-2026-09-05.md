# Cher Horowitz + Dionne Davenport

**PATRON SAiNTS of Trendsetting**

**The Trendsetters**

Cher and Dionne are fictional characters, and this is a LAiDIES teaching interpretation—not a biography, endorsement or quotation.

## Why they are here

In *Clueless*, Cher and Dionne do not disappear into matching uniforms. Their looks coordinate, but each woman is immediately herself. Costume designer Mona May has described designing Dionne's black-and-white suit to complement Cher's yellow plaid without overpowering it. That is the useful clue: a trend is not a copy. It is a pattern strong enough to be recognized and flexible enough to be made your own.

That is why they belong in an AI learning room. Being first to try a new tool is early adoption. Trendsetting is the harder, more useful job that comes next: turning one successful experiment into a way of working that another person can understand, test and adapt.

## The move

A useful AI pattern travels through five steps:

1. **Start with a real recurring problem.** “Use more AI” is not a problem. “Our weekly project update takes 90 minutes and still misses decisions” is.
2. **Define what better means before the demo.** Time saved, fewer omissions, a clearer decision or a more useful first draft gives you something to judge.
3. **Make the whole method visible.** Keep the input, the AI's exact job, the human checks and the point where a person takes over. A polished output without its checks is not a reusable pattern.
4. **Let someone else adapt it.** If the method only works while its inventor hovers over every step, it is a performance—not a practice.
5. **Keep the evidence with the pattern.** Say what improved, what did not and when you last tested it. A trend should not outlive the reason it was useful.

## What this looks like at work

A manager is tired of assembling the same Friday update from meeting notes, task boards and email. In this illustrative example, she gives an AI tool only the approved project notes and asks for a first draft in a fixed format: decisions, owners, deadlines and unresolved risks. She then checks every name, date and commitment against the source before sending anything.

After two trial weeks, she compares the new process with the old one. She records the time it takes, the omissions she catches and whether the update helps the team act. The AI draft is faster, but it regularly softens unresolved risks. So the shared method includes both the useful prompt and a non-negotiable human risk check.

Her colleague does not copy the prompt word for word. She adapts the method for client notes, removes confidential material before using AI and adds a tone check. The useful structure survives; the judgment stays with her.

## Try it this week

Choose one task you repeat. Write down how long it takes now and the mistake you most want to prevent. Test one small AI-assisted version, then save a four-part recipe:

- what information may go in;
- what the AI is responsible for;
- what a person must verify; and
- what result would make the method worth using again.

Give the recipe to one colleague and ask her to change it for her own work. What still works after her changes may be worth sharing. What breaks tells you which parts still depend on you.

## Do not confuse it with staying current

Carrie Bradshaw's lesson is to notice what has changed: a new tool, norm, risk or expectation. Cher and Dionne's lesson is to decide whether your response deserves to travel. Staying current reads the movement. Trendsetting creates a pattern other people can use.

## Where the analogy stops

Popularity is not proof. A widely copied workflow can still produce errors, expose private information or flatten everyone's work into the same voice. Before sharing a pattern, check that the inputs are appropriate, the result is better than the old way and a named person remains accountable for the final decision.

The point is not matching plaid. It is making the useful parts visible enough to borrow—and the judgment visible enough not to.
