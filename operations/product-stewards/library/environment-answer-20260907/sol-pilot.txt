AI can be bad for the environment, but there is no honest universal “footprint per prompt.”

AI runs in data centres (buildings packed with powerful computers). These use electricity and water for cooling. Their equipment also requires mining, manufacturing and transport, then eventually becomes electronic waste. UNEP says the full lifecycle matters: energy, water, minerals, emissions and discarded hardware—not just the moment you type a question. ([wedocs.unep.org](https://wedocs.unep.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.11822/46288/AI-Environmental-Impact-Issues-Note.pdf))

A few distinctions save this conversation from nonsense.

Not every data centre runs AI. Electricity use is not automatically carbon emissions, either: emissions depend heavily on how that electricity is generated. The IEA projects electricity use from all data centres—not AI alone—to rise from 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 950 in 2030. AI-focused centres are expected to grow faster. ([iea.org](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary))

Meanwhile, individual AI tasks are becoming much more energy-efficient. But total demand can still rise because far more people are using AI, and video generation and complex tasks may consume hundreds or thousands of times more energy than simple text. ([iea.org](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary))

AI may also help reduce energy use elsewhere, but possible benefits do not erase its direct costs. The sensible question is not “Is AI good or bad?” It is whether a particular use is worthwhile, efficient, powered cleanly and transparent about its full environmental impact.
