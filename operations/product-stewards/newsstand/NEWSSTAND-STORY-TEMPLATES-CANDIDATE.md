# NewsStand story templates — candidate for Ali review

**Status:** `CANDIDATE — NOT APPROVED / NOT PRODUCTION AUTHORITY`

**Purpose:** define the exact reader-facing structure, analysis job and delivery
job for each NewsStand story format before another story is drafted.

This file is not a checklist that can be satisfied by labels. A producer must
perform every section's job in the exact prose. It cannot use this candidate for
autonomous drafting until Ali accepts the relevant format and one complete
publish-shaped example for that same format.

## Shared requirements for every story

Every story must let a non-technical reader answer, where applicable:

1. What happened or what question is being answered?
2. Who acted, spoke, studied or published?
3. What exactly did they examine, release, share or change?
4. Where and how did it happen?
5. How does the mechanism work, link by link?
6. What does the evidence establish and not establish?
7. Why does it matter to the reader?
8. Where could she encounter it at work and outside work?
9. What can she usefully do, ask, test, notice, wait for or ignore?
10. Which sources support the material claims and what would trigger an update?

Meaning precedes terminology. Unfamiliar terms such as `API`, `agent`,
`reasoning block`, `sandbox`, `token` or `model weights` cannot carry reasoning
until their ordinary meaning has been established.

## The complete Daily issue — composition template

The Daily is the complete dated newspaper, not one article surrounded by
controls.

| Position | Content job | Analysis job | Delivery job | Failure condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Breaking strip | A genuinely time-sensitive interruption, only when qualified | Prove that waiting until the next Daily would materially disadvantage the reader | Short, factual and visually distinct at the top | Interesting or newly posted is treated as Breaking |
| Lead story | The most consequential qualified story of the issue | Rank by likely reader consequence and explanatory value, not virality | Largest headline, useful standfirst and visible story body/continuation | Only story by default; weak story promoted merely because it is ready |
| Secondary stories | Every other qualified dated development | Preserve their distinct news value without pretending they equal the lead | Shorter but meaningful summaries with clear routes to full coverage | Material stories are omitted or collapsed into a link list |
| Service columns | Admitted practical, career, non-work, reading and town features | Each column performs its own registered job and does not repeat another | The useful substance is readable in the paper; opening is for genuine depth | Reader must open every card to discover the tip or column |
| Related learning | Exact relevant STRAiGHT TALK, Big Picture, Library, Class or Episode destinations | Link only when the destination adds a different maintained job | Small contextual route beside the relevant story | Generic site promotion or unrelated cross-links |
| Archive and topics | Search, tags, dates and prior coverage | Preserve discoverability and story continuity | Easy to find but secondary to today's issue | Archive controls displace the newspaper or old stories become buried |

The issue must visibly read as one colourful LAiDIES newspaper at desktop and
mobile. The exact beige v26 page/paper/service combination produced by
`#fff7e1`, `#fffaf0` and `#fff4d2` is a bound rejected treatment. This is not a
blanket ban on vibrant yellow accents or every light neutral; rendered pixels,
not colour-name assurances, decide whether the rejected treatment returned.

## The Breaking

**Use only when:** delay until the next Daily would materially disadvantage the
reader. Target: 180–450 words.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Headline | Confirmed development and affected group | Remove speculation and identify the real subject | Factual statement, normally naming the organization/product | Tease danger or order everyone to act |
| What just happened? | Event, actor, time and source of confirmation | Establish the genuinely new fact | Answer in 2–3 plain sentences | Open with history, jargon or suspense |
| Why we are interrupting | Decision that cannot safely wait | Prove the time-sensitive reader consequence | One short paragraph | Substitute novelty, popularity or fear for urgency |
| What is confirmed—and what is not | Facts, company framing, uncertainty and unknowns | Separate evidence, framing, inference and unknown | Compact prose or labelled bullets | Blend claims into one confident narrative |
| What to do now | Proportionate action for affected people and boundary for unaffected people | Match action to evidence and real exposure | Clear imperative with audience named | Say only “be careful” or create panic |
| What happens next | Next investigation, fix, report or verification point | Identify the next fact capable of changing the advice | Give a date when one exists | End with vague “watch this space” |

## The Daily — report, announcement or research finding

**Use when:** one dated development is worth explaining and the underlying
reporting is not materially misleading. Target: normally 350–700 words for the
lead; secondary Daily stories may be shorter when their useful truth is small.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Headline | The actual change and subject | Select the useful distinction, not the most alarming fragment | Factual, non-teasing statement | Ask a weak question or hide the point |
| The news in one breath | What happened, why it matters and audience boundary | Decide who should care and who need not worry | No more than 90 words; answer first | Begin with a statistic or technical noun |
| Before today | Minimum newcomer context, including named study/company/publisher and status where relevant | Identify only the background required for comprehension | Plain nouns and verbs | Assume the reader knows the incident, study or product |
| What actually happened | Real actor, object, channel and sequence | Build the cause-and-effect chain, including where and how | Concrete explanation in causal order | Make the reader infer what was shared, where it lived or how the result arose |
| What the evidence found | Results, attribution, units, bases and limits | Reconcile whole sample, subsets, filters and uncertainty | Name source and date at first material claim | Dump numbers or leave source identity to the footer |
| What this does—and does not—mean | Correct conclusion and tempting overread | Calibrate the evidence without manufacturing controversy | Plain contrast | Turn every story into a media takedown |
| Where you might encounter this | One realistic work and one realistic non-work case when material | Transfer the same mechanism into unfamiliar settings | Concrete human situations | Add decorative examples unrelated to mechanism |
| What to do with this | Action, decision rule, useful question or reason to wait | Give the smallest proportionate reader change | Direct, specific landing | Repeat “check the receipts,” “be careful” or “stay informed” without a method |
| Sources and watch point | Reader-facing sources, check date and correction trigger | Name what evidence would change the story | Short transparent close | Hide volatility or silently rewrite later |

## The Daily — headline or reporting check

**Use only when:** a public headline or account materially distorts, omits or
overstates the underlying evidence. Target: 400–750 words.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What you may have seen | Fair summary of the exact headline/account | Identify the operative claim without caricature | Name and link the reporting | Invent a bad claim nobody made |
| What it makes it sound like | Likely non-expert takeaway | Explain why that reading is reasonable | One plain paragraph | Scold the reader for misunderstanding |
| What the evidence actually says | Primary evidence and correct scope | Return to the study, filing, announcement or record | Attribute claims at point of use | Treat AIDB or another summary as primary authority |
| The missing piece | Omitted background or mechanism | Show what the reader needed to interpret the claim | Cause-and-effect explanation | Add jargon or unrelated context |
| What is wrong or unclear | Exact mismatch, omission or overstatement | Distinguish material defect from ordinary compression | Precise and proportionate | Attack a publication merely to create drama |
| The real read | LAiDIES' evidence-backed interpretation | Reconstruct the most defensible conclusion | Answer clearly | Replace evidence with attitude |
| Why the difference matters | Decision or understanding changed by the correction | Demonstrate consequence | Work/non-work example when useful | Make the correction feel academic and unusable |
| What to do or ask next | Concrete check or better question | Give a reusable reader method | Short direct close | Default to “open the citation” as the whole method |

## The Daily — update to prior coverage

**Use when:** new evidence changes or clarifies a LAiDIES story. Never silently
overwrite the earlier article. Target: 300–650 words.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What we previously reported | Earlier date, headline and conclusion | Represent the earlier evidence boundary fairly | Direct link and short summary | Pretend LAiDIES never made the earlier claim |
| What is new | New evidence, statement, correction or investigation | Establish that it is genuinely new and relevant | Name actor, date and source | Use thematic similarity as an update |
| What changes | Exact prior conclusions/actions affected | Trace the impact of new evidence | Explicit before/after contrast | Say only “the story has developed” |
| What does not change | Earlier conclusions still supported | Prevent false total reversal | Short clear boundary | Leave readers assuming everything collapsed |
| The updated read | Current conclusion, uncertainty and action | Recompute the evidence boundary | Answer first, then needed explanation | Bury the corrected conclusion |
| Follow the story | Old/new links and next trigger | Preserve a traceable story history | Bidirectional links and visible old-story notice | Quietly edit history or rely on one hidden correction field |

## The Weekly

The Weekly is a complete newspaper, not one long synthesis article. It gives a
reader the important news she missed, updates the stories she already saw and
adds analysis that a stack of separate Daily articles cannot provide.

### Complete Weekly paper composition

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cover and date | Weekly identity, exact period covered and one-sentence orientation | Establish which reporting window is complete | Newspaper masthead and clear date range | Look like another Daily or an undated magazine |
| The week in one minute | The 3–5 developments that mattered most and the current state of each | Rank by consequence, evidence and reader usefulness—not clicks | Compact front-page briefing; each item names what happened and why it matters | Become a headline list with no explanation |
| The week's biggest story | The most consequential development or connected story thread | Explain why it outranks the other qualified stories | Lead placement with a useful standfirst | Promote whatever draft happened to be ready |
| Biggest stories this week | Short, self-sufficient accounts of other major Daily stories | Preserve distinct developments even when they do not share one theme | 80–180 words each plus links to dated Daily coverage | Reprint full Daily articles or omit major stories because they do not fit the lead theme |
| What changed after we first reported it | Material dated updates to earlier coverage | State what changes and what remains supported | Before/after briefs with bidirectional story links | Quietly edit the old article or call repetition an update |
| Weekly analysis | One synthesis only when at least two developments support a useful connection | Add mechanism, pattern, disagreement or direction that the individual Daily stories could not establish | Main analytical feature; 700–1,200 words when earned | Force unrelated stories under a fake theme or concatenate summaries |
| Term of the Week | One term needed to understand this week's AI news | Explain the term, its place in the larger system and why it mattered this week | 200–450 readable words, visible in the paper | Publish a detached glossary definition or unexplained jargon |
| STRAiGHT TALK | One admitted recurring question made relevant by the week's reporting | Link current news to the maintained canonical answer | Compact answer/continuation with current check date | Create a second competing answer canon |
| Dear Miss Jeeves | One admitted practical recurring problem | Diagnose why it happens and give a realistic next move | One readable weekly advice column | Treat a magic prompt as a system fix |
| Tip of the Week | One useful action earned by the week's evidence | Select the highest-value practical move rather than fill a slot | Fully readable compact column | Repeat a Daily tip or substitute a tip for reporting |
| What to watch next week | Named reports, releases, tests, decisions or dates | Identify evidence capable of changing the Weekly's conclusions | Short forward calendar | End with generic “AI moves fast” language |

Daily and Weekly stories may cover the same development, but they perform
different jobs:

- The Daily records and explains the dated change while it is current.
- The Weekly gives the latest state, ranks it against the week's other stories
  and adds only what became visible across time or across developments.
- The Weekly links to the Daily instead of repeating it at full length.
- If nothing new can be learned from the week's sequence or comparison, the
  item remains a “biggest story” recap and does not become a forced synthesis.

### Weekly analysis feature template

**Use when:** at least two dated developments earn a connected analysis. Target:
700–1,200 words inside the complete Weekly paper.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The question this week | One useful question that requires several developments | Define the additive synthesis job | Human, specific question | Use a generic theme such as “AI had a busy week” |
| The short answer | Current conclusion and main limit | State what the combined evidence adds | Concise answer first | Withhold the conclusion for suspense |
| The developments | Minimum facts and dates from each contributing story | Include only events necessary for the analysis | Short linked accounts | Reprint the Daily stories |
| Why they belong together | Shared mechanism, incentive, tension, disagreement or direction | Prove the connection rather than assume it | Largest explanatory section | Treat timing or topic similarity as causation |
| Where the evidence agrees and conflicts | Supported common ground and material disagreement | Reconcile sources, populations, units and outcomes | Fair comparison | Smooth over contradictions or count repetition as confirmation |
| What changed by week's end | Belief or action that should—or should not—change | Compare the evidence boundary across the week | Explicit conclusion | Merely repeat the chronology |
| Work and non-work consequences | Human implications when material | Transfer the combined mechanism | One specific example each | Force irrelevant examples |
| What would change the read | Evidence capable of strengthening, weakening or overturning the analysis | Keep the synthesis updateable | Specific triggers | Present one week as a permanent trend |

## Term of the Week

**Use when:** one term is necessary to understand the week's reporting and its
place in the larger AI system. Target: 200–450 words inside The Weekly.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The term | Exact term and one-sentence ordinary meaning | Select a term the week's reporting genuinely requires | Large readable label plus plain definition | Choose jargon merely because it sounds current |
| What problem it names | The real object, process or distinction the term describes | Establish why the term exists | Familiar concrete situation | Define it with more unexplained terms |
| Where it fits | Its place in the larger AI system or workflow | Connect it to inputs, models, tools, outputs and people as relevant | Small causal map in prose; diagram only when useful | Leave it as an isolated vocabulary card |
| Why it mattered this week | Exact story or stories where the term changed understanding | Reconnect durable meaning to dated evidence | Short sourced bridge with links | Let current news silently redefine the durable term |
| Work example | One professional encounter | Apply the same mechanism | Concrete scenario | Turn it into generic career advice |
| Non-work example | One home, school, family or daily-life encounter | Test transfer | Concrete scenario | Repeat the work example with new nouns |
| Do not confuse it with | Closest misleading neighbouring term or popular misuse | Protect an important distinction | One clear contrast | Build an unnecessary glossary list |
| Learn more | Maintained Library/STRAiGHT TALK/Class destination when one exists | Preserve one durable concept owner | Exact deep link and freshness date | Link to an unfinished or stale destination |

## The Big Picture

**Use when:** a consequential question or pattern requires a deeper sourced
argument behind several headlines. Target: 1,200–2,200 words.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Big Question | The emotionally and intellectually real reader question | Frame the actual uncertainty | Ordinary reader language | Use a grand but empty theme |
| The short answer | Current conclusion plus crucial limitation | Commit to the most defensible answer | 2–4 sentences, answer first | Hide behind “it depends” |
| Why people are asking now | Relevant current events | Explain why the question is live | Brief connected context | Become a recap of headlines |
| What we know happened | Confirmed incidents/evidence and sources | Separate fact, company framing and unknown | Dated factual account | Repeat “rogue” or other loaded shorthand as fact |
| How the mechanism works | Causal explanation link by link | Make the invisible process visible and reconnect examples to it | Largest share of the article | Use analogy as substitute for mechanism |
| What the pattern may mean | Strongest supported inference | Label inference and its limits | Evidence-led argument | Present inference as confirmed fact |
| Strongest alternative explanation | Best credible counterargument | Test the conclusion honestly | Fair steelman | Use a weak straw person |
| Should people be worried? | Present risks, bounded fears and irrelevant science fiction | Calibrate probability, consequence and time horizon | Direct answer to emotional question | Dismiss fear or amplify it for drama |
| What it looks like in life | Workplace and non-work consequences | Translate the pattern into decisions and lived experience | Concrete cases | Drift into abstract policy only |
| What would change our conclusion | Disconfirming/confirming evidence | Make reasoning updateable | Specific evidence conditions | Treat the argument as permanent |
| The better next question | More useful question enabled by the explanation | Demonstrate reader learning | Memorable close | End with a grand moral |
| Sources and related coverage | Primary evidence, Daily/Weekly precursors and maintained concepts | Preserve evidence and story lineage | Grouped, labelled links | Use citations as decoration |

## STRAiGHT TALK

**Use when:** the reader has a recurring, durable AI question. It consumes one
canonical maintained answer rather than creating a competing NewsStand canon.
Target: 500–900 words.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The question | Wording a reader would actually use | Remove expert assumptions | Plain question | Rewrite it as industry jargon |
| The straight answer | Answer and main boundary | Identify the stable principle | 2–4 sentences | Say only “it depends” |
| What is solid | Dependable facts/principles | Separate stable knowledge from volatile product detail | Clear explanation | Overstate certainty |
| What it depends on | Conditions that change the answer | Name decision variables | Structured prose or compact list | Create an unbounded checklist |
| What remains unknown | Genuine uncertainty | Protect the reader from false precision | Candid boundary | Treat unknown as zero or impossible |
| How to decide or check | Reusable decision method | Turn explanation into judgment | Numbered method when helpful | Default to “check the sources” without showing how |
| Work example | Professional application | Apply the same decision rule | Specific scenario | Introduce a different mechanism |
| Non-work example | Daily-life application | Test transfer | Specific scenario | Repeat the work example with new nouns |
| Keep this answer current | Last check, sources and dated updates | Preserve one maintained answer through change | Visible date and links | Quietly replace the historical answer |

## Dear Miss Jeeves

**Use when:** one familiar practical AI problem needs a realistic diagnosis and
next move. It is not current news and it never implies that one prompt can
enforce a system rule. Target: 500–900 words.

| Section | Content | Analysis | Delivery | Must not do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dear Miss Jeeves… | Natural pre-generated reader question | Preserve the real frustration and context | Short letter/question | Create an artificial problem to fit a tip |
| The answer first | Honest diagnosis without blaming the reader | Name the dominant cause or bounded possibilities | Direct, humane answer | Start with a lecture |
| Why this keeps happening | Relevant mechanism: instruction competition, context limits, ambiguity, tool state, model behaviour, etc. | Explain cause and distinguish plausible causes | Plain causal chain | Attribute every failure to prompting |
| Advice that is not enough | Common outdated, impractical or incomplete advice | Explain its limit fairly | Short correction | Mock beginners or claim the advice never works |
| What to try next | Prioritized actions beginning with lowest effort | Match each step to the diagnosed cause | Numbered sequence | Dump an exhaustive troubleshooting list |
| A practical example | Before/after instruction, workflow or decision | Demonstrate why the change helps | Concrete worked case | Provide a magic prompt without mechanism |
| When the fix is not a prompt | Validator, smaller task, tool change, stored state, human check or product limit | Identify system-level boundary | Clear stop/escalation rule | Promise prompt wording can enforce unavailable behaviour |
| Miss Jeeves' final word | Useful memorable landing | Reinforce the actual decision rule | One concise line; humour optional | Let humour replace the answer |

## Approval boundary

Ali may accept, revise or reject each format separately. Acceptance of a
template authorizes creation of a private complete example for that exact
format only. It does not accept an example, authorize autonomous drafting,
promote a story, compose an issue, deploy or publish.
