<!-- paige-01-follow-up -->
# Turn meeting notes into a follow-up you can actually send.

Make the draft keep track of what was agreed—and what wasn’t.

Before you ask AI to write a follow-up email, separate your notes into decisions, actions and unresolved questions. Use only information you are allowed to put in that tool.

Try: “Draft a short follow-up from these notes. Include each agreed action, its owner and its deadline. If a name or date is missing, write ‘to confirm’. Keep proposals separate from decisions. Do not add commitments.”

That last instruction matters: a tidy email can quietly turn ‘we could launch in October’ into ‘we will launch in October’. Compare the draft with your notes before sending. Check names, dates and promises first; polish the greeting afterwards.

<!-- paige-02-fix-one-thing -->
# Fix the paragraph without rewriting the whole thing.

Tell the tool what to change—and what to leave alone.

You like the draft, apart from the opening paragraph. Asking for “a better version” gives the tool permission to change far more than you wanted.

Try: “Revise only the first paragraph. Make the request clear in the first sentence and remove the apology. Keep the rest exactly as it is. Do not change names, dates, figures or commitments.”

Keep a copy of the original and compare the two. The instruction narrows the job; it does not guarantee that every other word stays put. If the tool still changes unrelated text, ask for the replacement paragraph by itself and insert it into your saved draft.

[Read Working with AI: the art of iteration](https://laidies.ai/library.html#working-with-ai-101::%40chapter-4-the-art-of-iteration-4-3-how-to-give-feedback-that-works)

<!-- paige-03-save-good-example -->
# Save the example, not just the prompt.

A useful result gives you a concrete standard for the next attempt.

When an AI-assisted email finally sounds like you, save a version with private details removed. Keep it beside the instruction that produced it and a short note about what you changed.

Next time, try: “Use this example for length and tone, not for its facts. Write a new email from the notes below. Do not carry over the example’s names, dates or promises.”

An example makes ‘friendly but direct’ less vague. It can also contaminate the new draft with old details, which is why the final check should compare facts with today’s notes, not with the sample.

# When someone takes credit for your idea.

A script for putting your contribution back into the conversation.

You explained the idea yesterday. Today a colleague presents it as theirs. If you can respond calmly, make your contribution specific: “I’m glad we’re taking this forward. When I proposed the pilot yesterday, the problem I wanted to solve was the handover delay. I can walk us through the first step.”

If the meeting has moved on, follow up privately: “In today’s meeting, you presented the pilot without mentioning my contribution. I developed the proposal we discussed yesterday. Can you clarify that in the follow-up?”

Use details you can support. If this keeps happening, keep the relevant drafts and messages, and discuss the pattern with a manager you trust. You do not have to improvise a confrontation with someone who can retaliate against you.

Dorie Clark’s work on professional visibility informs this column; Amy Gallo’s guidance addresses credit-taking specifically. The scripts here are written by LAiDIES, not quotations from either author.

# What should move when another “urgent” job arrives?

Ask for a priority decision instead of silently accepting two incompatible deadlines.

Your manager needs a presentation by tomorrow. You already have a report due then. Before you agree to both, try: “I can finish the presentation tomorrow, but the report would move to Thursday. Which should come first?”

Bring a realistic estimate, including time to check the work. If both deadlines really are fixed, ask what can change: “Could we reduce the report to the essential figures, or could someone take the first draft of the slides?”

Dorie Clark writes about protecting time for chosen priorities and leaving room between commitments. This is a LAiDIES script for making that trade-off visible at work. It will not manufacture extra hours, but it gives your manager a concrete decision to make.

[Dorie Clark: practical productivity strategies](https://dorieclark.com/blog/practical-productivity-strategies/)

# Send the message before you need a favour.

Reconnect with something specific, not a mystery invitation to “pick your brain”.

You liked working with someone, then six months disappeared. You do not need an elaborate reason to get back in touch.

Try: “I was reminded of our conversation about onboarding when I saw this example. The section on first-week checklists made me think of your project. How did the new process work out?” Only send the link if it is genuinely relevant; a warm check-in can stand on its own.

Dorie Clark recommends building relationships before an immediate need arises. The point is to stay interested in the person’s work, not to disguise a request. If you do need help, say what you are asking for and make it easy to decline.

<!-- concept-01-context -->
# Context: the information available for this answer.

Your chat history and what the model can use right now are not necessarily the same thing.

Ask an AI to write an invitation and it can produce something plausible. Tell it that this is a casual lunch for six colleagues, not a customer event, and you have changed the material it can work with. That material is context.

Context can include your message, instructions, earlier conversation, material from files and results returned by tools. What actually reaches the model depends on the product. A document sitting in your account is not proof that its contents were used.

The context window is a different thing: it is the limit on how much material the model can process at once, measured in tokens. Context is the material; the window sets a capacity limit. If an important detail has disappeared from a long conversation, restate it and check that the next answer follows it.

[Read AI Fundamentals 101: context and memory](https://laidies.ai/library.html#ai-fundamentals-101::%40ch-8-8-2-the-two-sources-of-knowledge)

<!-- concept-02-token -->
# Tokens: the pieces a language model processes.

A token is not reliably one word, so word counts and token limits differ.

Before a language model works with text, a tokeniser breaks it into reusable pieces and represents them as numbers. A piece might be a whole word, part of a word, punctuation or a single character. The exact split depends on the tokeniser.

That is why a limit of 10,000 tokens does not mean 10,000 words. The document, instructions and conversation can all take up space, and the system also needs room to produce its answer.

If a document is too large for a task, shorten irrelevant material or work on clearly labelled sections. Keep the passages needed to interpret each section together. Cutting a policy into pieces is not helpful if the exception ends up separated from the rule.

[Read AI Fundamentals 101: tokens](https://laidies.ai/library.html#ai-fundamentals-101::%40ch-4-4-2-what-a-token-actually-is)

<!-- concept-03-hallucination -->
# Hallucination: a convincing answer with an invented detail.

A response can be mostly useful and still include a false citation or date.

In AI, a hallucination is false or unsupported information presented in a generated answer. It does not have to be an entirely invented story. One nonexistent study in an otherwise sensible summary is enough.

A language model generates its response; it does not automatically verify every sentence against a reliable record. Search and supplied documents can give it evidence to use, but the answer can still misread that evidence or add an unsupported detail.

When a claim matters, open the cited source and find the passage that supports it. Check that the author, date and conclusion match the answer. A real link to a real paper is not enough if the paper does not say what the answer claims.

[Read AI Fundamentals 101: hallucination](https://laidies.ai/library.html#ai-fundamentals-101::%40ch-11-11-6-hallucination-why-it-makes-things-up)

# Why does my AI keep getting the day wrong?

A calendar question needs a reliable date, not just a convincing sentence.

Dear Miss Jeeves, my AI can explain quantum physics, then confidently tell me Monday is Wednesday. Why is it so bad at knowing the day and time—and how do I stop it messing up my plans?

— Temporally Confused in Toronto

Dear Temporally Confused,

Explaining a calendar and checking today’s date are different tasks. A model can generate an answer about dates from the information it has, but the app must supply a current date or give it access to a suitable tool if you want it to check the time now.

Some products supply date information automatically. Some can use search or other tools. That still leaves room for mistakes: the answer might use the wrong time zone, carry forward an old date from the conversation or get the day-of-week calculation wrong. Saying “Are you sure?” does not supply the missing fact.

For a scheduling task, give it the confirmed date, place and time zone: “Today is Monday, 7 September 2026, in Vancouver. The meeting is on Thursday, 10 September at 2 p.m. Vancouver time. Draft an invitation using those details.” If you are converting between time zones, use a calendar or time-zone converter to confirm the result.

For a question about right now, ask the tool to check a current clock if it has access to one. If it cannot, check your device’s clock yourself. For flights, appointments and deadlines, confirm the final date against the booking or calendar before acting. Miss Jeeves would rather you checked twice than arrived on Wednesday for a Tuesday lunch.

[Read AI Fundamentals 101: what reaches the model](https://laidies.ai/library.html#ai-fundamentals-101::%40ch-8-8-2-the-two-sources-of-knowledge)

<!-- jeeves-02-citation -->
# Why does the link work but the claim doesn’t?

A citation is somewhere to check—not proof that the sentence is right.

Dear Miss Jeeves, I clicked the source in an AI answer. The paper is real, but I cannot find the statistic anywhere. Am I missing something?

— Footnote Detective in Fredericton

Dear Footnote Detective,

You have found a particularly irritating kind of mistake: the source exists, but the answer misrepresents it. The model may have mixed up two studies, misunderstood a result or attached a plausible link to a claim it generated.

Open the original source and look for the exact finding. Check who was studied, when, and what was actually measured. A survey about whether people use AI cannot prove that AI made them better at their jobs.

If the tool can read the source, try: “For each factual claim in this paragraph, show the short passage in the source that supports it and its location. Mark anything you cannot support.” Then compare those passages with the original yourself. The tool can also invent quotations, so its list is a checking aid, not the final check.

If you cannot access the source, do not present the claim as verified. Find an accessible original source or leave the claim out. A footnote can look very scholarly while doing absolutely no useful work.

[Read AI Fundamentals 101: hallucination](https://laidies.ai/library.html#ai-fundamentals-101::%40ch-11-11-6-hallucination-why-it-makes-things-up)

<!-- jeeves-03-style -->
# Why does “make it professional” make me sound unbearable?

Give the tool an example of your voice and a specific job to do.

Dear Miss Jeeves, every time I ask for a professional email, I get “I hope this message finds you well” followed by three paragraphs nobody needed. How do I make it sound like me?

— Trapped in Corporate in Calgary

Dear Trapped in Corporate,

“Professional” leaves a lot for the tool to decide. It might mean concise and courteous to you, but produce an email full of formal introductions and impressive-sounding filler. The instruction names a broad style without showing what you want.

Give it a short, privacy-safe example of your own writing. Then name the changes: “Write this in my usual style. Put the request in the first sentence. Keep it warm and direct. Use ordinary words. Do not add a greeting longer than the request.”

If the draft is already close, ask it to change the offending sentences only. You might write: “Replace ‘I am reaching out to inquire’ with ‘Can you tell me’. Keep the rest unchanged.” Compare the revision with your original; dates, names and commitments should not change during a tone edit.

An example gives the tool something concrete to follow. You still choose what sounds like you. If a sentence would make you wince saying it to a colleague, it does not become professional because an AI put it in an email.

[Read Working with AI: controlling what comes back](https://laidies.ai/library.html#working-with-ai-101::%40chapter-3-controlling-what-comes-back-3-2-making-it-sound-like-you)
