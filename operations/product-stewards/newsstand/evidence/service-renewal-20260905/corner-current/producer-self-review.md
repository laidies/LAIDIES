## corner-02-priorities

# What should move when another “urgent” job arrives?

Ask for a priority decision instead of silently accepting two incompatible deadlines.

Your manager needs a presentation by tomorrow. You already have a report due then. Before you agree to both, try: “I can finish the presentation tomorrow, but the report would move to Thursday. Which should come first?”

Bring a realistic estimate, including time to check the work. If both deadlines really are fixed, ask what can change: “Could we reduce the report to the essential figures, or could someone take the first draft of the slides?”

Dorie Clark writes about protecting time for chosen priorities and leaving room between commitments. This is a LAiDIES script for making that trade-off visible at work. It will not manufacture extra hours, but it gives your manager a concrete decision to make.

[Dorie Clark: practical productivity strategies](https://dorieclark.com/blog/practical-productivity-strategies/)

Producer assessment: The presentation and report both due tomorrow create a concrete conflict. The first dialogue offers a date trade-off. The second offers scope reduction or another drafter if both dates are fixed. The ending explicitly avoids promising extra time. Make the finite time and displaced work visible so the manager can decide priority, scope or help. The script cannot create extra capacity.

Producer transfer analysis (not an observed reader): For two client calls that overlap, offer moving one, sending a qualified colleague, or narrowing the meeting; ask the decision maker to choose rather than silently accepting both.

## corner-03-reconnect

# Send the message before you need a favour.

Reconnect with something specific, not a mystery invitation to “pick your brain”.

You liked working with someone, then six months disappeared. You do not need an elaborate reason to get back in touch.

Try: “I was reminded of our conversation about onboarding when I saw this example. The section on first-week checklists made me think of your project. How did the new process work out?” Only send the link if it is genuinely relevant; a warm check-in can stand on its own.

Dorie Clark recommends building relationships before an immediate need arises. The point is to stay interested in the person’s work, not to disguise a request. If you do need help, say what you are asking for and make it easy to decline.

[Dorie Clark: building future opportunities](https://dorieclark.com/blog/system-future-opportunities/)

Producer assessment: Six months of silence is recognisable, and the onboarding/checklist message shows specific continued interest. The link is optional, so the advice does not require an artificial excuse. The final paragraph distinguishes staying interested from concealing a request and makes declining acceptable. A remembered shared topic gives the person a real reason to reply; curiosity is distinct from a hidden transaction. If help is the purpose, state the request and let the person decline.

Producer transfer analysis (not an observed reader): For a former teammate who mentioned studying, ask how the course went rather than forwarding an irrelevant link. If you need a reference, ask that plainly and give them an easy way to say no.
