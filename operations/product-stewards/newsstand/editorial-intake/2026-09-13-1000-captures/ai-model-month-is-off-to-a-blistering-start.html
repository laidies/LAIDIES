HEADLINES

OpenAI Says It Solved Navier-Stokes. The Fallout Was Immediate.

On Tuesday OpenAI published a solution to the Navier-Stokes problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize problems and only the second ever claimed since the prize was established in 2000. The result was found by an internal model significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra, cost several million dollars, and took a week or two. But the story got swallowed by the drama: NYU professor Tristan Buckmaster says he and Anthropic's Levent Alpoge had spent over a year on the problem inside Codex, that OpenAI moved to publish days after learning of their work, and that he was offered co-authorship only if Alpoge was removed. OpenAI leadership called the allegations false and inflammatory while conceding they cannot rule out that de-identified data from product usage improved their models. Two conversations are running in parallel — academics furious about scooping culture, and everyone else asking whether the labs can see your work and beat you to it when the stakes are high enough.

Anthropic Sued Over What Max Subscribers Actually Get

A class action filed on behalf of Claude Max subscribers alleges Anthropic used deceptive marketing and opaque fine print to underserve its highest-paying customers, claiming the $100 5x plan and the $200 20x plan don't deliver five and twenty times the usage of the $20 Pro plan. The mechanism at issue is how five-hour and weekly limits interact, which plaintiffs say drags real usage well below the advertised multiples. Whether it goes anywhere is another question — but it is representative of the scrutiny that arrives once AI subscriptions become a normal cost of doing business. The lawyers running it say what made the case compelling was hearing from workers who felt they had to buy a top-tier subscription to stay relevant and didn't feel they got what they paid for.

ElevenLabs Hires a CFO and Points Toward an IPO

It isn't just OpenAI and Anthropic eyeing public markets. ElevenLabs has hired Ethan Tandowsky, most recently CFO of Dutch fintech Adyen, to help prepare the company for a listing. The company says it is on track for $600M in annualized revenue by year end, up from $350M at the end of last year, has reached profitability, and now generates more than half its revenue from large enterprise customers.

Cognition Raises $2B at a $48B Valuation

The rumored round is done, nearly doubling Cognition's valuation from $26B in May in the space of three months. Revenue run-rate moved from $492M to almost $900M over the same period. More than the numbers, the raise confirms Cognition stays independent after SpaceX's $60B acquisition of Cursor sparked rumors it would be next — and after watching OpenAI cut off access to Cursor customers post-acquisition, the value of independence looks a lot more concrete. As Cognition put it, the ability to choose and combine the best models for the work, including their own, is core to the strategy.

MAIN STORY

AI Model Month Is Off to a Blistering Start

The theme of the summer was the move away from a single-model paradigm — pick the best model, stick with it — toward an architecture where individuals and teams navigate nimbly between models and harnesses depending on the job. It also became clear that getting the most out of AI isn't only about capability, but about efficiency and cost, especially as workloads become more agentic. September has obliged with a cavalcade: Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra, and now three more models and one agent product that add up to far more diversity in the stack you can build for your actual life and work.

SUMMER MODEL WAVE

Gemini 3.8 Flash
"A very good Flash model, but not equivalent to a frontier model"
Google's third updated Flash model in six weeks is trained to work harder than 3.7 — calling tools iteratively and taking more reasoning steps — and the benchmarks are solid but spiky: 73.7% on DeepSWE, a hair under Opus 5, but a collapse from 89.4% on Terminal Bench 2.1 to 19.1% on 4.0. It scored 59 on Artificial Analysis' index at release, then slipped from 7th to 12th once AA rushed out a reweighted version built around computer use and agentic tasks rather than saturated knowledge tests. Flash remains the undisputed speed leader and, briefly, the cheapest model measured at its intelligence level — the real question is which use cases justify the trade-off, and whether being able to run something 39 times over beats letting Opus do it once.

Muse Spark 1.3
"Frontier performance almost too cheap to meter"
Meta's new model beat Opus 5 on Terminal Bench and DeepSWE, tied Opus for first on Artificial Analysis' coding agent index at max settings, and did it at 55 cents per task — roughly a quarter of Opus 5's cost and, per AA, the most cost-efficient model at its intelligence level. The obvious suspicion is benchmaxxing, and SemiAnalysis made the case directly, pointing at the gap between Terminal Bench 2.1 and 4.0 and the RL environment startups selling tasks shaped to mimic public benchmarks. Alexandr Wang's response conceded the framing without conceding the charge: Spark 1.3 isn't claimed to be as strong as Astra or Fable 5.1, it's claimed to be significantly more cost-effective, and future models will compete more directly.

Muse
"Always-on, wicked fast, can use a browser, designed to be secure"
Meta's long-rumored personal agent, built under the codename Hatch, is essentially OpenClaw for normal people: inbox triage, calendar, bookings, shopping, and a separate virtual computer in the Grokbot mold. The security architecture is the serious part of the pitch — each Muse runs in its own isolated VM with a separate system, the Sentinel, checking every action before anything leaves it, and the agent never sees actual passwords or card numbers. What makes this worth watching beyond Meta is that they are the only company at this scale aimed primarily at consumers rather than business, which makes Muse a real test of whether agentic shopping becomes a thing and whether ordinary people want a personal assistant at all. Trust remains the open question: the distribution advantage that could put this in front of hundreds of millions is the same reason people hesitate before pressing connect email.

ChatGPT Images 2.5
"How well it understands what not to change"
The model powering ChatGPT's built-in image generation — a feature still producing more than three billion images a week — gets sharper detail, more precise editing, and a 50% latency reduction, in two variants: Flare for fast iteration and Sunburst for professional workflows needing control across edits. Control is the key word, the same axis NanoBanana moved on, and it is already opening up genres like stop motion that weren't viable before. The update feels routine, but images are underrated as a business differentiator, not just a consumer one — being able to call GPT image for UI elements and aesthetics inside Codex changes which model you reach for.
