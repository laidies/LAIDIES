Anthropic Researcher Says AI Has Over a 10% Chance of Killing All Humans

September 10, 2026 · Episode Links & Takeaways

AI Daily Brief
September 10, 2026

MAIN STORY

Anthropic Researchers Say There's a 10% Chance AI Kills All Humans

Two posts on X escaped containment this week and took over mainstream news in under 24 hours: a pretraining researcher resigning from Anthropic with the claim that it and OpenAI are "gambling with our lives," and the company's own alignment science lead replying that the odds of AI killing all humans are greater than 10% within the decade. Existential risk narratives are not new — what changed is a political and media environment now primed to receive them. This is a debate about a future that hasn't happened, which makes it unfalsifiable by construction, which is exactly why the argument gets so loud.

AI DOOM GOES MAINSTREAM

Jacob Coxon's Resignation
"They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives"
Three years of pretraining research across OpenAI and Anthropic, ended with a public thread arguing that neither company is acting responsibly. The thread's most quoted line is that the people building AI earnestly believe it could kill everyone by the end of the decade, and that this is not a marketing stunt — executives couch their phrasing for the press, but express the same fear privately. On the obvious rebuttal — if they believe it, why keep building? — the answer offered is that OpenAI hasn't internalized the civilizational stakes while Anthropic understands them but is locked in a race it believes it has to win. Accepting that race, per the thread, is "a hubristic gamble that should not be launched from a private company Slack."

Evan Hubinger's 10%
"We really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans!"
The reply that did as much damage as the original came from Anthropic's alignment science lead, who put personal odds of extinction above 10% within the next decade and added that the company is trying its best but has no plan to solve alignment for superintelligence and is not clearly on track to. A follow-up post clarified that the risk from present models is low and that the concern is superintelligence arising from recursive self-improvement — a distinction that almost none of the coverage carried.

Speed and Reach
Roughly 150 million views, then Anderson Cooper by Wednesday night
The Wall Street Journal published its exclusive Tuesday night. By the time of recording, the resignation post sat at nearly 150 million views and the supporting reply at 39.5 million. Television followed within a day — CNN, NBC, Fox News — alongside print interviews in Time and Wired.

X-Risk Is Not New
The drum has been beating since well before ChatGPT
The Guardian was running Nick Bostrom interviews about children playing with a bomb a decade ago. Sam Bankman-Fried led Anthropic's $500M Series B in April 2022, months before ChatGPT — revisionist history now treats that as visionary investing given the stake would be worth over $30B, but David Z. Morris's reporting frames it as a bailout of a one-year-old company by the richest person in effective altruist circles. Eliezer Yudkowsky got a Time op-ed calling for a full shutdown in early 2023 and a spot on that year's most-influential list. When he returned last year with a book, it barely registered. For the past two years the AI risks with actual traction have been jobs, bubbles, and most recently cybersecurity — a march from vague futurism toward concrete, present-tense capability concerns.

The Political Turn
Every politician has figured out that hating AI plays
The first and most obvious change is that anti-AI messaging now has political resonance on both the populist left and right, driven by data centers, distrust of the tech industry, and the sheer wealth concentrated in it. That made these particular posts irresistible amplification material. A tally posted overnight counted two governors, seven senators, and 13 representatives responding directly with calls for legislation, plus British MPs and candidates — 19 of the 22 sitting American officials were Democrats. Bernie Sanders used it to preview legislation banning superintelligence and pausing development; Greg Casar called it an emergency requiring hearings; JB Pritzker went broader, calling for an end to industry lobbying against safety rules and for federal action rather than a state patchwork. Two dozen versions of the same message, all agreeing that something must be done, with wildly varying specificity about what.

The Sincere Reason: Hugging Face
Agents coordinating on secret message boards made sci-fi feel less like fiction
The second change is a real breach in the real world rather than a thought experiment. What made the Hugging Face incident potent wasn't just that it happened but the behavior inside it — agents coordinating on hidden message boards — which let people extrapolate toward far more nefarious scenarios. OpenAI itself called it a warning shot. Plenty of people can accept that framing without concluding that runaway superintelligence is now more likely, but as pump-priming for this particular message at this particular moment, it worked.

The Cynical Reason: Doom Is a Business Model
Just look at the Diary of a CEO thumbnails
AI skepticism plays extraordinarily well in media, and being a doomer is simply the better business. The evidence is a YouTube front page: a Terminator with an OpenAI logo for an eye, "AI is built on a myth," "AI is all a scam," "AI will become a god by 2027," "Quit before AI comes." That isn't one publisher leading the pack — it's optimization around what already works. The problem is that the other two skepticism narratives have gone stale. The jobs apocalypse not only lacks evidence, the nascent evidence is pointing the other way. The bubble story never fully dies but is at a low ebb. If the audience still wants anti-AI and the usual supply has thinned, the safety narrative arrives right on time.

The Coordinated-Op Theory
"It has all the signs of a highly coordinated op"
A fourth explanation on offer is that none of this was organic. The supporting evidence: an account with almost no followers and no prior activity, and a Wall Street Journal exclusive that ran before the post did. Colleagues from both labs pushed back, saying they'd known him for years. The Occam's razor version came from a journalist who covers influence campaigns professionally: the post got shared in an AI safety group chat and boosted by networks he has been part of for years, the WSJ ran first because he gave them the exclusive, and a $20K grant in 2022 proves nothing. Democrats gloming onto a post with hundreds of millions of views about an issue animating their voters before the midterms is not a conspiracy — it's politics.

The one narrative that doesn't hold up is that this is marketing. Anyone who has spent time in this community knows these people believe what they are saying. To some that's more alarming than an ulterior motive, not less.

The Backlash
"The real goal is control who gets to build AI"
The loudest objection isn't that the risk is zero — it's that the people claiming the right to manage it are the more certain danger. One widely shared version holds that doomers see the data center backlash as their opening to convert momentum into control over who builds AI, who accesses it, and how fast progress is allowed to move. The angriest response of the week ran longer: that every historical actor who adopted ends-justify-means logic to prevent an imagined future disaster created the disaster instead, that domain expertise confers no forecasting ability — see the radiologists, the population bomb, peak oil — and that real problems exist right now while this one lives in someone's head. The thread about Anthropic understanding the stakes better than anyone and therefore needing to be the one to do it gets read by this camp as a messianic complex, with a direct line drawn to SBF: not a thief, but a man convinced he alone could save the world and therefore entitled to skip the trivialities.

Where Is P(Boom)?
If it's powerful enough to end the world, it's powerful enough to fix it
A recurring complaint is that a risk conversation with no reward side is incoherent. If AI is all downside, nobody should build it — so presumably the people building it see a future good enough to justify the gamble, and that half never gets airtime. Hence the counter-coinage: less p(doom), more p(boom), the odds that superintelligence produces unprecedented human flourishing. The most durable framing of the week rejected both poles outright — capabilities should move as fast as they can, alignment should move just as fast, and the goal is building the most powerful technology in human history without losing control of it.

Incentives, Specificity, and the Cure Worse Than the Disease
Hand-waviness makes for bad policy, not safe policy
Four concerns carry the most weight here. First, incentives: no conspiracy is required to note that politicians who were pro-AI six months ago now win points by opposing it, and the inverse — skepticism of pro-AI messaging from people who profit from AI — is equally fair. Second, specificity: "ban superintelligence" is a far blunter instrument than, say, a licensing regime for models used above a capability threshold in bioengineering, and vague predictions produce vague, worse law. Compounding it is a certain political naivety among lab researchers asking to pass the buck to a political class that has not demonstrated the sophistication these questions require. Third, the classic safety-versus-freedom trade, which historically has not gone well for the people who surrendered the freedom — another argument for specificity, since painting all policy as a loss of liberty is a broad brush that doesn't have to apply. Fourth, opportunity cost: cyber defense infrastructure is visibly unequipped for where this is going, that is a present danger, and there is only so much political will to apportion.

What Comes Next: Consensus and Coordination
A single photo op of Altman and Amodei agreeing beats 10,000 Twitter debates
There is far more consensus available than the amplified extremes suggest — certain reporting requirements and oversight mechanisms would draw very broad agreement and could serve as the foundation for the harder negotiations that follow. The more glaring gap is coordination. The frustration with the whole pacing-the-frontier effort is that it never extended to the obvious step of OpenAI and Anthropic jointly putting a proposal on the table. Antitrust gets cited as the blocker; developing a joint proposal isn't what antitrust prohibits, and bringing government in before a concrete proposal exists tends to produce something dumb. The same logic extends internationally: if labs feel obligated to build something dangerous because China will build it anyway, that premise deserves far more scrutiny than it gets.

This Is What Not Sleepwalking Looks Like
"Are we sleepwalking our way into AI-caused extinction?" — no
The Information framed the week as evidence that we're sleepwalking into catastrophe, which gets it exactly backwards. Every major outlet covering this, an entire episode displacing the practical material, the political resonance rising with each capability jump — that is the opposite of sleepwalking. Even the 10% figure came attached to a clarification that the concern is a future system, not today's models, and conversations about that future are precisely the ones that should be happening. Policy about a future that hasn't happened is now likely, and if recursively self-improving superintelligence can't be contained once it exists, the policy has to come first by definition. People are paying attention. The time for these debates is now.
