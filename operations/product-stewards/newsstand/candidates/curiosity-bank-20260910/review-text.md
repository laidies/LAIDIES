# Try this today — thirty private activity candidates

## 01. One example, please.

When an explanation loses you, ask, “Could you show me what that would look like in an ordinary situation?” Try explaining the example back in your own words. If it still does not make sense, ask about the part that does not. The nodding can wait.

## 02. Put her name back in.

The next time you pass along a useful idea, name the woman who gave it to you and what she contributed. “Maya found a simpler way to book the hall” tells people more than “We sorted it.” Check that you are not sharing something she meant to keep private.

## 03. Keep the sentence that sounds like you.

Choose a short, non-private message you have already written. Ask your AI tool for a plainer version, then compare the two. Keep any change that makes your meaning clearer; reject anything that changes your tone or adds a fact. You do not owe the machine a full acceptance speech.

## 04. Follow one claim home.

Pick one factual claim in a post you are about to share. Follow its link to the original report or announcement and find the passage that supports it. Does it say the same thing, about the same people and date? If you cannot find the support, leave the post unshared for now.

## 05. Leave room for one more question.

Before a group settles a plan, ask, “What have we not explained clearly yet?” Give people a moment to answer instead of immediately filling the silence. If a question comes up, settle that point before treating the plan as agreed.

## 06. Offer a choice, not a takeover.

If a friend is wrestling with something, ask whether she wants you to listen, suggest ideas or help with one practical step. Follow her answer. At the end, check whether that was the kind of help she wanted; an efficient solution to the wrong problem is still the wrong solution.

## 07. Tell her which part worked.

When something a woman made helps you, tell her the exact part and why. Perhaps her map made the meeting place easy to find, or her explanation cleared up one confusing step. Give her a detail she can use again, rather than leaving her to decode “Great job.”

## 08. Ask to see the missing step.

When instructions jump from “open the file” to “you are ready,” ask what happens in between. Try that missing step yourself while the person is there, if they have time. If you still cannot get to the next screen, say what you see instead of assuming you did it wrong.

## 09. Change one thing and compare.

For a low-stakes AI draft, change one instruction: perhaps the length, the reader or the tone. Keep everything else the same and compare the result. Which change actually helped? Save the useful instruction with that task, rather than collecting another enormous list of prompts.

## 10. Send the invitation with the useful bits.

If you spot an event or opportunity a friend might genuinely want, send the date, cost and reason you thought of her alongside the link. Make it easy to decline. Before forwarding, check that registration is still open; enthusiasm does not extend a deadline.

## 11. Whose preference is this?

Look at one shared plan: the restaurant, meeting time or activity. Did somebody actually choose it, or did everyone inherit the first suggestion? Ask the people involved what matters to them before changing anything. You may keep the plan; at least this time you will know why.

## 12. Make a useful introduction.

If two women might genuinely help each other, ask each privately whether she would welcome an introduction. Only connect them if both agree, and explain the specific reason. An introduction should arrive with context, not as a surprise assignment.

## 13. Show the first move.

If someone asks how you did something, show her the first step rather than sending a finished example alone. Let her try it while you watch, if she wants. The useful check is whether she can get started herself, not whether your demonstration looked effortless.

## 14. Name what “better” means.

Before asking AI to improve a low-stakes draft, write down what you mean by better: shorter, warmer, easier to follow, or something else. Use that one aim to judge the revision. A longer answer has not automatically done more for you.

## 15. Give her another way in.

When organising a discussion, offer people a way to send a question beforehand as well as asking aloud. Use a submitted question only with permission, and ask whether its author wants her name included. Afterward, check that the question actually received an answer.

## 16. Check the date on the good news.

Before passing along a promising announcement, check when it happened and when anything becomes available. Put those dates in your message if they differ. An old story can still be useful; it just needs to arrive wearing its own year.

## 17. Ask what the rule is for.

Choose a minor routine in a group you belong to and ask what problem it was meant to solve. Listen before proposing a change. If the reason no longer applies, suggest one small alternative the group can try and a way to tell whether it helps.

## 18. Pass along the useful part.

If someone wants a checklist or template you use, remove private details and explain which parts she should change for her own situation. Ask whether one step needs explaining. Sharing the starting point can be more useful than handing over a finished document she cannot adapt.

## 19. Return the floor.

If a woman is interrupted in a conversation you are part of, bring it back to her: “I wanted to hear the rest of your point.” Then let her finish without summarising it for her. The aim is for her idea to be heard in her own words.

## 20. Give the unknown a sentence.

While making a small plan, write one line beginning, “We still need to find out…” Name the missing fact and where you could check it. Once you have the answer, update the plan. A visible question is easier to resolve than a confident guess.

## 21. Explain it without the borrowed words.

After reading a useful explanation, put it aside and describe the idea in two ordinary sentences. Notice where you reach for a word you cannot explain. Return to that part and look for an example; you have found the next question, not failed an exam.

## 22. Ask what she would keep.

When someone asks for feedback on a draft or idea, first ask what she wants to preserve. Give your feedback against that aim. Before suggesting a rewrite, point to the exact place that confused you; she may have a better fix than yours.

## 23. Try being the newcomer.

Read directions you are about to send as though you have never been there. Is the entrance obvious? Is there a time and a way to ask for help? Add the one detail you were relying on everyone to know, then check that it is correct.

## 24. Find the detail you never supplied.

In an AI draft of a low-stakes message, look for dates, names or commitments you did not provide. Check each against what you actually know and remove unsupported additions. If there are none, leave the draft alone; the point is a check, not finding fault on principle.

## 25. Pass on the question that helped.

When sharing an explanation with a friend, include the question it answered for you. “This helped me understand why my earlier chat was not enough context” gives her a reason to open it. Let her decide whether that is a question she has too.

## 26. Make the offer specific.

Instead of “Let me know if you need anything,” offer one thing you can comfortably do: collect a parcel, read a short draft or bring something to a shared event. Let her say no or suggest another time. Then do the thing you both agreed to.

## 27. What would change your mind?

For a low-stakes choice you are discussing, ask what information would make you choose differently. Answer the question yourself too. Look for that information before adding more arguments; if nothing would change the choice, you may be discussing a preference rather than a fact.

## 28. Save the fix, not the whole saga.

When you solve a small recurring problem, write down the step that finally worked and the situation it applies to. Try following that note the next time. If you offer it to someone else, include the condition that matters, such as the device or version you used.

## 29. Check who the plan leaves out.

Before finalising a shared outing, ask the people coming whether the time, cost or access arrangements create a problem. Give them a private way to answer. Change what you can agree on; do not ask anyone to explain personal circumstances to the whole group.

## 30. Come back to the unanswered question.

If someone raised a useful question that got lost in a discussion, bring it back before the conversation closes. Ask whether it was answered or who could help find out. Keep the question in her words and credit her for raising it; you are returning it, not claiming it.
