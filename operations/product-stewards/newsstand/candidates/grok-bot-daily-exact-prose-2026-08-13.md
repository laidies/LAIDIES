# Grok Bot can keep working after you close your laptop

**xAI has released an early version of a tool that can stay online, sign into
apps and continue a job while you are away. The useful question is no longer
only “Is its answer right?” It is also “What did I give it permission to do?”**

## The news in one breath

On August 11, 2026, xAI launched Grok Bot as an early beta. Eligible subscribers can give Grok Bot a job on desktop or iOS so it can work inside signed-in apps and websites. Grok Bot keeps working on its cloud computer after the user's laptop closes, placing work in the connected tool. This matters to anyone considering connected AI access; it does not show that every job is safe or correct. Start with one low-consequence job and limit the accounts and actions it can reach.

## Before today

Most AI chats wait for you to return and send another message. Grok Bot instead
uses a computer running online. You can sign it into other apps, give it a job
and leave. xAI says it can continue across tools, remember how you work and come
back when it needs approval.

## What actually happened

xAI calls this an “AI teammate.” That is product language, not proof that the
Bot has a colleague's judgment or responsibility. The early beta is currently
offered to SuperGrok Heavy, Cursor Ultra and Cursor Teams Premium subscribers on
desktop and iOS. Enterprise access is waitlisted.

Closing your laptop does not stop the job because the Bot is not running on
your laptop. Its online computer remains awake. If you signed it into a help
desk, inbox or another service, that account remains the route through which it
can act.

That is the real shift. A chat can suggest an email; a connected Bot may be able
to place the draft inside the inbox—or send it if the account and workflow allow
that action. The laptop can be tucked in for the night; the permission slip is
still wide awake.

## What the evidence found

xAI's consumer terms say these actions can include browsing, running code,
sending communications, changing files and interacting with third-party
services. The same terms place responsibility for directed actions and their
consequences on the user.

## What this does—and does not—mean

The public documents LAiDIES checked do not give a complete Bot-specific account
of how credentials are isolated, how finely every app permission can be limited,
how connected-app information is retained or whether every Bot interaction is
excluded from model training. Those are questions to resolve before sensitive
work—not details to guess from the word “teammate.”

## Where you might encounter this

At work, a support team might let a Bot draft replies inside its help desk but
keep **Send** behind human approval while it proves itself on low-risk cases.
The useful result is a prepared queue; the unnecessary risk is giving the first
test authority to contact every customer.

Outside work, a Bot could compare flights and hotels using a separate planning
account. It does not need permission to book a non-refundable trip merely to
make a shortlist. The mechanism is the same: access should match the job, not
every possible next step.

## What to do with this

Before connecting an always-on Bot:

1. Choose one reversible, low-consequence job.
2. List the exact accounts and actions that job needs—and remove the rest.
3. Keep sending, paying, publishing and deleting behind an approval step.
4. Check the real destination where it worked, not only the summary it sends
   back.
5. Remove its access if the trial ends or the job changes.

Do not make its first unsupervised evening payroll, publishing and inbox zero
all at once. That is not delegation; that is a season finale.
