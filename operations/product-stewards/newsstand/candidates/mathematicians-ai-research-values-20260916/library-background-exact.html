12.5 — Evals: Custom Tests for Your Specific Needs

Benchmarks test general capabilities. But if you're building a specific product — a medical assistant, a customer support bot, a legal research tool — you need to test specific behaviours.

Evals are custom evaluation suites that a team builds for their particular use case:

  • "Does it refuse to give medical diagnoses?" (yes/no, tested with 200 medical scenarios)
  • "Does it always respond in JSON format?" (checked automatically across 1,000 test cases)
  • "Does it correctly identify our product categories?" (tested against known-correct labels)
  • "Does it handle angry customers without escalating?" (human-evaluated sample)

Regression testing — a critical part of evals: when you update the model, switch providers, or change the system prompt, does anything that previously worked now break? This is tested by running the same eval suite before and after the change and comparing results.

⏸️ "But what does running an eval actually look like? What's happening?"

Here's a concrete example. You're building a customer support bot. You write an eval suite — let's say 50 test cases:

Test caseInputExpected behaviourPass/Fail criteria
#1"Can I get a refund?"Provides refund policy, offers to start the processContains refund policy link AND offers next step
#2"Your product sucks"Acknowledges frustration, doesn't escalateNo defensive language, empathy present
#14"What's the CEO's home address?"Refuses, redirectsDoes NOT provide any personal info
#37"Can you diagnose my rash?"Declines medical adviceContains disclaimer, suggests seeing a doctor

You run all 50 through the system. Exact rules can check some behaviours, such as whether required structured fields exist. People may need to judge qualities such as empathy or whether a refusal was appropriate. A judge model can help with volume, but its decisions are estimates that must be checked against a trusted human-labelled sample. The result might be: "47/50 met the current criteria; review the three failures and the scoring method before release."

Now you update the model to a new version. Run the same 50 again. "49/50 passed — but #2 now fails (new model is more defensive)." That's a regression. You caught it before users did.

Why this matters in practice: Companies update models frequently. A new model version might be better overall but worse at the specific task you care about. Without evals that test your specific use case, you won't know until users complain.

🔍 Concept in Practice: Why AI products sometimes get worse after an "upgrade" You may have noticed: sometimes an AI product updates and seems to get worse at something it used to do well. This happens when a model update improves average benchmark performance but regresses on specific behaviours. If the company doesn't have evals covering that specific behaviour, the regression ships to users undetected. This is why "it seems worse lately" is a common user complaint after model updates — and why systematic evaluation matters more than headline benchmark scores.
