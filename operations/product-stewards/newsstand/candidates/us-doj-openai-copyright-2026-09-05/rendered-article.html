The US government backs OpenAI in the copyright fight

The Story

On September 1, the US Department of Justice submitted an argument supporting OpenAI in the New York copyright lawsuits. It urged the court to treat copying written work to teach AI patterns as fair use: certain uses US law permits without the copyright owner’s permission.

The document is a Statement of Interest—the government explaining its position to the court. It is not the court’s decision. What changed was the government’s intervention in the argument, not a new permission slip for everyone using AI.

The New York Times, one of the publishers involved, has accused OpenAI and Microsoft of copying its journalism without permission. Its 2025 annual report records its demand for compensation and a court order stopping conduct it alleges infringes its rights. Those are the company’s claims and requests, not findings that every disputed use is unlawful.

The LAiDIES Read

The filing separates three things that can get bundled into “AI used someone’s work”. A developer first obtains material. During training, software adjusts numerical settings using examples, developing patterns it can apply later. The trained part that produces a response is the model. When someone uses it, the answer it produces is an output.

The filing distinguishes the copyright questions at those stages. Its focus is copying works during training. The department argues that using articles to develop a tool that can generate responses has a different purpose from publishing those articles for people to read. It also argues that requiring developers to pay for permission to use works could make it harder for smaller AI developers to compete. These are reasons it gives the court for accepting its position.

Fair use asks about the purpose of a use, the kind of work copied, how much was taken and its importance, and the effect on the work’s market or value. The factors are considered together, case by case. There is no magic number of words you can copy that automatically makes a use fair.

Here comes the videotape. The filing reaches back to the VCR case: recording a television programme to watch privately later and selling copies of that recording are different uses. Same footage; different question for the law. Remembering to rewind was the easy bit.

That comparison explains why the government wants training considered separately from later answers. It does not establish that AI training has the same legal answer as home recording. The filing itself allows that an output which reconstructs and distributes a copyrighted work can raise a different question.

What This Means for You

You can want AI to help with your work and want the work you create to be respected. Understanding the disagreement lets you ask about both: what becomes possible through these tools, and whose material, permission and income are involved?

Imagine AI gives you a paragraph very close to a newsletter you found online. This filing does not establish that you can put it in a client presentation. Ask the person responsible for the material, “What is our basis for using this passage?” A claim about the model’s training does not answer that question about your proposed use.

When the next headline says the copyright fight is settled, look for who actually decided what. A court ruling addressing a particular use would be a different development from this government filing. This report covers the September 1 submission; we have not verified a later ruling on its argument. And the discussion is about US law, not automatic permission in every country.

The Cocktail Party Explanation

“The US government has backed OpenAI’s argument about copying material to train AI. That is a position put to a court. Getting the material, training with it and publishing an AI answer raise different questions—like the difference between recording a programme at home and selling the tape.”
