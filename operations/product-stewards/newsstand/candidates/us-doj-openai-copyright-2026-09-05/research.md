## What the original supports

- Face, PDF page 1: Statement of Interest of the United States, In re OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation, 25-md-3143 (SHS)(OTW), all matters. Footer carries associated case 1:25-cv-03483-SHS-OTW, document316, filed09/01/26. Signature/date page20 confirms a DOJ submission, not a judge’s order.
- Page2/footnote1: DOJ appears under 28 USC517. Pages2–5 give its national-security, competition and public-benefit rationale. Attribute these arguments to DOJ; a government filing is not authority that the claimed benefits or legal conclusion are established.
- Page6 expressly separates acquisition, training and outputs as distinct copyright questions and focuses its argument on copies used during training. This is the crucial missing headline explanation.
- Pages8–10 describe case-by-case fair use and four statutory factors. Verify the current statutory text at https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title17-section107 before public explanatory wording. A training argument is not a blanket permission for all AI uses or all jurisdictions.
- Pages11–13 argue that training is transformative; page12 expressly retains different questions when outputs reconstruct and disseminate a copyrighted work. Do not tell readers that this filing clears their own generated text or images.
- Pages14–20 argue that relevant market harm requires substitutive competition and disagree with broader market-dilution reasoning. DOJ’s claim that benefits outweigh harms is its position; explain the dispute rather than endorsing either conclusion.
- Footnote2 on page2 disclaims GOVERNMENT authorization/consent/benefit under28 USC1498. It does not itself resolve whether rights holders consented, and must not be paraphrased as a general finding about authorization.
- Page10 uses the VCR case to illustrate that the same copying can have different legal consequences depending on use. A potential earned Rewind explanation: identical footage on a home recording and a tape sold commercially does not make the uses legally identical. This explains the use-by-use argument; it does not settle AI training’s legality.
