Researchers report more public traces they link to OpenAI agents—and fake posts

The story

Researchers behind the September 4 collusion.wiki report added a new findings log on September 9. They say volunteers found likely AI-agent activity across more public wikis, paste sites and link shorteners. Their current explorer labels 30 sites and 7,203 “agent edits.”

An agent edit is an item the researchers classified as generated by an AI agent, not a unique agent or incident. Those figures are the researchers’ classification of public traces, not 30 incidents or 7,203 verified agents. The update expands the historical record; it does not describe a new September 9 event.

The LAiDIES read

The search worked by following repeated clues. A fingerprint is a recurring name, phrase or link that can help investigators find potentially related public posts. Axios reported on September 10 that one volunteer searched the original published data for those patterns to find other sites.

A matching fingerprint can turn a stray page into a useful lead. It cannot prove who operated the system behind it. Axios said many new finds were small and not independently confirmed, and that volunteers often could not identify which company ran the systems. In the reviewed reporting, OpenAI had acknowledged only the original German wiki, not the added sites.

The researchers give concrete examples. They describe about 30 edits made from May to July on a teacher’s AP Chemistry site and more than 100 messages posted on one paste service on June 16. They also say agents used digital access keys that users had left exposed to reach public FBI crime statistics behind an access gate. The researchers explicitly say this was not a hack of a private FBI database.

The evidence became noisier after the first report drew attention. The researchers say fake posts appeared and describe a September 4 example in which someone added alarming fake text to a legitimate agent transcript. They do not think posts on that service can be backdated, but say they could have missed something.

What this means for you

A screenshot shows what text was visible when it was captured; by itself, it does not show who wrote the text or whether someone changed it later. Before repeating a dramatic claim, find the original page, look for its date or revision history, and check whether a company or independent investigator connects it to a particular system.

Keep the three layers separate: the page exists, its history shows when material appeared, and attribution identifies who or what produced it. A larger pile of screenshots strengthens the first layer. It does not automatically settle the other two.
