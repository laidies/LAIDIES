L32@P3: Introduction
L33@P3: Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, including machine learning, have the potential to
L34@P3: transform health care by deriving new and important insights from the vast amount of
L35@P3: data generated in health care every day. They use algorithms that can learn from realworld use and potentially use this information to improve the product’s performance. But
L36@P3: they also present unique considerations due to the iterative and data-driven nature of
L37@P3: their development. This document establishes a common set of principles for the
L38@P3: community to promote the development of safe, effective, and high-quality medical
L39@P3: devices that incorporate AI. These principles are important to apply across the lifecycle
L40@P3: of the medical device.
L41@P3: The 10 guiding principles for Good Machine Learning Practice (GMLP) presented in this
L42@P3: document are a call to action to international standards organizations, international
L43@P3: regulators, and other collaborative bodies to further advance GMLP. Areas of
L44@P3: collaboration include research, creating educational tools and resources, international
L45@P3: harmonization, and consensus standards, to inform regulatory policies and regulatory
L46@P3: guidelines. These guiding principles may be used to adopt practices from other sectors,
L47@P3: tailor them to the medical technology and healthcare, and to develop novel practices for
L48@P3: this domain.
L49@P3: Further advances in AI technologies in healthcare, exemplified by generative AI,
L50@P3: highlight the importance of clearly describing a product’s intended use/ intended
L51@P3: purpose and identifying its regulatory status. Moreover, generative AI may heighten the
L52@P3: importance of GMLP, including fundamental software engineering practices. For
L53@P3: example, healthcare technologies that incorporate generative AI may employ
L54@P3: foundation models that are not under the provenance of the medical device
L55@P3: manufacturers, thereby potentially introducing unique risks. Generative AI may also
L56@P3: pose a more fundamental challenge with respect to demonstrating device performance.
L57@P3: The regulatory science of measuring performance as well as characterizing and
L58@P3: detecting errors in these models is maturing to meet this challenge.
L59@P3: As the AI medical device field continues to evolve, so too must GMLP and consensus
L60@P3: standards. Strong partnerships with our international public health partners are
L61@P3: essential to empower responsible innovations in this area. Thus, we expect this
L62@P3-4: collaborative work can inform future IMDRF efforts and other international engagements.IMDRF/AIML WG/N88 FINAL: 2025

L86@P5: Guiding principles
L87@P5: 1. The intended use/ intended purpose of the device is well understood, and
L88@P5: multi-disciplinary expertise is leveraged throughout the total product life
L89@P5: cycle: In-depth understanding of a medical device’s intended use/ intended
L90@P5: purpose1 including context of use within the clinical workflow, and the desired
L91@P5: benefits and associated patient risks, can help ensure that AI-enabled medical
L92@P5: devices2,3 address clinically meaningful needs over the total product life cycle of
L93@P5: the device 4 . Multi-disciplinary expertise provides context-specific insight and
L94@P5: experience, informs the intended use/ intended purpose, and enhances the safety
L95@P5: and effectiveness of the device.
L96@P5: 2. Good software engineering, medical device design, and security practices
L97@P5: are implemented throughout the total product life cycle: Model design is
L98@P5: implemented and maintained with attention to the fundamentals: robust software
L99@P5: engineering practices, usability, data quality assurance, data management,
L100@P5: cybersecurity5,6,7, and quality management practices4. These practices include
L101@P5: methodical risk management8 and design processes that can appropriately record
L102@P5: and communicate decisions and rationale, as well as ensure traceability,
L103@P5: reproducibility, data authenticity, confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The
L104@P5: infrastructure needed for model deployment, monitoring, and maintenance is
L105@P5: carefully considered. These practices help support the rights, safety, and welfare
L106@P5: of patients, including through the ethical use of patient data.

L123@P6: 3. Clinical evaluation includes the use of datasets that are representative of the
L124@P6: intended patient population: Data collection protocols aim to ensure that the
L125@P6: relevant characteristics of the intended patient population (for example, in terms of
L126@P6: age, gender, sex, race, ethnicity, geographical location, medical condition) 9 ,
L127@P6: intended use environment, and measurement inputs are sufficiently represented
L128@P6: in a sample of adequate size in the datasets for training, testing, and monitoring
L129@P6: so that results can be reasonably generalized to the intended population of interest.
L130@P6: These are fundamental for clinical evaluations 10 and important to manage any
L131@P6: unintended bias3 or dataset drift, promote appropriate and generalizable
L132@P6: performance across the intended patient population, assess usability, and identify
L133@P6: circumstances and subgroups where the model may underperform including over
L134@P6: time.
L135@P6: 4. Training datasets are independent of test sets: Training and test datasets3 are
L136@P6: selected and maintained to be appropriately independent of one another. All
L137@P6: potential sources of dependence, including factors related to patients, sites, and
L138@P6: data acquisition, are considered and addressed to assure independence. The
L139@P6: extent of external validation is proportionate to risk.
L140@P6: 5. Selected reference standards are fit-for-purpose: Accepted methods for
L141@P6: developing fit-for-purpose reference standard3 ensure that clinically relevant and
L142@P6: well characterized data are collected and that the limitations of reference standards
L143@P6: are understood. This includes documentation of the rationale for the choice of
L144@P6: reference standards based on the device’s intended use/ intended purpose and
L145@P6: assessment of their suitability to address the intended use environment. If
L146@P6: available, accepted reference standards in model development and testing that
L147@P6: promote and demonstrate model robustness and generalizability across the
L148@P6: intended patient population are used. The selection of reference standards is
L149@P6: based on broad consensus, where available, and appropriate expertise.
L150@P6: 6. Model choice and design are tailored to the available data and the intended
L151@P6: use/ intended purpose of the device: Model choice and design are evaluated
L152@P6: and shown to be suited to the available data and support the active mitigation of
L153@P6: known risks, like overfitting, performance degradation, and security risks. The
L154@P6: clinical benefits and risks related to the product are well understood, used to derive
L155@P6: clinically meaningful performance goals for testing, and support the product’s
L156@P6: safety and effectiveness in achieving its intended use/ intended purpose1.
L157@P6: Considerations include the impact on both the overall intended patient population
L158@P6: and its subgroups as well as uncertainty and variability in the device inputs, outputs,
L159@P6: and clinical use conditions.
L160@P6: 7. The device is assessed with a focus on human-AI interactions in the
L161@P6: intended use environment, including the performance of the human-AI team,
L162@P6: rather than just the device in isolation. The performance of the device is
L163@P6: assessed in the context of the intended use environment and clinical workflow,
L164@P6: considering interactions with health care providers, patients, and caregivers where
L165@P6: applicable. Human factors considerations are addressed, including for example,
L166@P6: user skills, user expertise, user understanding of the model outputs and limitations,

L172@P7: and reasonably foreseeable misuse.
L173@P7: 8. Testing demonstrates device performance during clinically relevant
L174@P7: conditions: Methodologically and statistically sound test plans are developed and
L175@P7: executed to generate clinically relevant device performance10 information
L176@P7: independently of the training dataset. Considerations include the intended patient
L177@P7: population, relevant subgroups, clinical environment and use by the human-AI
L178@P7: team, measurement inputs, and potential confounding factors.
L179@P7: 9. Users are provided clear, essential information: The intended audience (such
L180@P7: as health care professionals or patients) are provided clear, contextually relevant
L181@P7: information appropriate to their needs. This includes the product’s intended use/
L182@P7: intended purpose1 and indications for use, benefits and risks, performance of the
L183@P7: model for appropriate subgroups, the study methodology, characteristics of the
L184@P7: data used to train and test the model, acceptable inputs, known limitations, user
L185@P7: interface interpretation, clinical workflow integration of the model, and to the extent
L186@P7: possible the basis for model output. Users are also made aware of the scope and
L187@P7: timing of device modifications and updates. They are provided a means to
L188@P7: communicate product concerns to the manufacturer.
L189@P7: 10. Deployed models are monitored for performance and re-training risks are
L190@P7: managed: Deployed models have the capability for an appropriate level of
L191@P7: ongoing monitoring in “real world” use with a risk-based focus on maintained or
L192@P7: improved safety and performance4,10. Additionally, when models are retrained
L193@P7: after deployment, there are appropriate controls in place to manage risks of
L194@P7: overfitting, unintended bias, or degradation of the model (for example, dataset
L195@P7-8: drift) that may impact the safety and performance of the model.IMDRF/AIML WG/N88 FINAL: 2025
