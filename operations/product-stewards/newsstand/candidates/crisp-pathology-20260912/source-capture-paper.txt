Abstract 
70 
Intraoperative pathology is pivotal to precision surgery, yet its clinical impact is constrained by 
71 
diagnostic complexity and the limited availability of high-quality frozen-section data. While 
72 
computational pathology has made significant strides, the lack of large-scale, prospective 
73 
validation has impeded its routine adoption in surgical workflows. Here, we introduce CRISP, 
74 
a clinically oriented foundation model developed on over 100,000 frozen sections from ten 
75 
medical centers, specifically designed to provide Clinically-oriented Robust Intraoperative 
76 
Support for Pathology (CRISP). CRISP was comprehensively evaluated on more than 15,000 
77 
intraoperative slides across nearly 100 retrospective diagnostic tasks, including benign-
78 
malignant discrimination, key intraoperative decision-making, and pan-cancer detection, etc. 
79 
The model demonstrated robust generalization across 6 institutions, 14 tumor types, and 24 
80 
anatomical sites-including previously unseen sites and rare cancers. In a prospective cohort of 
81 
over 3,000 patients, CRISP sustained high diagnostic accuracy under real-world conditions, 
82 
directly informing surgical decisions in 92.6% of cases. Human-AI collaboration further 
83 
reduced diagnostic workload by 35%, avoided 105 ancillary tests and enhanced detection of 
84 
micrometastases with 87.5% accuracy. Together, these findings suggest that CRISP represents 
85 
a clinically oriented approach to AI-driven intraoperative pathology, with the potential to 
86 
support surgical decision-making and facilitate the translation of computational methods into 
87 
clinical practice.

Introduction 
97 
Rapid and accurate intraoperative diagnosis is a cornerstone of modern surgical practice, 
98 
directly influencing patient outcomes and guiding critical decisions in real time.1,2 For over a 
99 
century, frozen section examination has served as an indispensable modality for intraoperative 
100 
pathological assessment, enabling surgeons to make informed choices regarding tumor 
101 
resection, margin status, and differential diagnosis across a wide spectrum of surgical 
102 
specialties.3-6 The widespread adoption of frozen sections is driven by their expedited 
103 
preparation and diagnostic accuracy, which closely parallels that of formalin-fixed paraffin-
104 
embedded (FFPE) specimens in clinical practice.7,8 As a result, frozen section analysis has 
105 
become integral to precision surgical management, supporting timely interventions that can 
106 
profoundly improve patient outcomes. 
107 
Despite its clinical utility, frozen section diagnosis presents formidable challenges. The 
108 
inherent morphological complexity of frozen tissue, compounded by the urgency of 
109 
intraoperative decision-making, demands rapid and highly accurate interpretation. These 
110 
constraints introduce significant risks, including the potential for misdiagnosis and suboptimal 
111 
surgical outcomes.9,10 The need for robust, scalable support systems to assist pathologists and 
112 
surgeons in real-time intraoperative workflows is therefore both pressing and universal. 
113 
Recent advances in computational pathology and artificial intelligence (AI) have begun to 
114 
reshape the landscape of diagnostic medicine. Deep learning models have demonstrated 
115 
remarkable promise in automating and augmenting pathological assessment, offering the 
116 
potential to enhance diagnostic consistency and efficiency. However, the application of AI to 
117 
intraoperative frozen section analysis remains in its infancy.11 Prior studies have explored deep 
118 
learning approaches in specific disease contexts,12-14 Yet these efforts are frequently constrained 
119 
by small, homogeneous datasets that fail to capture the full spectrum of morphological and 
120 
technical variability encountered in clinical practice.15 As a result, the development of 
121 
generalizable and clinically practical AI models for intraoperative diagnosis has remained 
122 
elusive.  
123 
The emergence of large-scale AI models, or foundation models, has further fueled optimism for 
124 
the clinical translation of computational pathology.15-18 Pretrained on vast and diverse datasets, 
125 
these models have shown strong generalizability across a range of diagnostic tasks.19 However, 
126 

PAGE 12
most existing foundation models are built upon FFPE specimens,20 which differ fundamentally 
127 
from frozen sections in both preparation and tissue morphology. Direct application of FFPE-
128 
trained models to intraoperative frozen section risks compromising diagnostic accuracy as such 
129 
models may lack exposure to frozen section morphology,10,21 while attempts to bridge this gap 
130 
using virtual staining techniques raise concerns regarding reliability and clinical trustworthiness 
131 
of synthetic images.22-24 Additionally, the lack of prospective validation in most studies limits 
132 
their readiness for real-world deployment.25  
133 
Addressing these challenges requires the development of a robust and generalizable foundation 
134 
model specifically tailored to frozen section pathology, with demonstrated clinical validity in 
135 
diverse and realistic settings. In this study, we introduce CRISP, an AI-enabled comprehensive 
136 
deep learning framework designed to support a wide spectrum of intraoperative diagnostic and 
137 
predictive tasks. Leveraging the largest frozen section dataset assembled to date-comprising 
138 
over 100,000 slides from ten medical centers across different regions-CRISP was pretrained on 
139 
50 million image patches from 25 anatomical sites. The model’s performance was rigorously 
140 
evaluated across multiple downstream tasks commonly encountered in intraoperative pathology, 
141 
with external validation cohorts drawn from independent institutions. To further assess its real-
142 
world clinical utility, we conducted a registered prospective observational study involving more 
143 
than 3,000 patients in real-world surgical workflows. Through both rigorous retrospective and 
144 
prospective analyses, our findings demonstrate the practical value and broad applicability of a 
145 
frozen-specific foundation model, making a significant advance toward the integration of AI-
146 
driven decision support in precision surgery. 
147 
 
148 
Results 
149 
Patient cohorts and study design 
150 
The development of a clinically robust foundation model for intraoperative diagnosis 
151 
necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the biological and technical heterogeneity 
152 
inherent in real-world surgical pathology.26 To address this, we constructed the largest multi-
153 
center frozen section dataset to date, comprising 103,797 frozen section slides obtained 
154 
primarily from ten medical centers and spanning a broad spectrum of anatomical sites (Fig. 1a). 
155 
This dataset was specifically curated to reflect the diversity of clinical scenarios and laboratory 
156 

PAGE 13
conditions encountered during intraoperative consultations, as detailed in the Methods.  
157 
For model pre-training, we utilized 81,667 frozen section slides from three major centers and 
158 
the TCGA frozen section dataset.27 To rigorously assess the model’s robustness and 
159 
generalizability, we established 98 downstream diagnostic tasks designed to emulate critical 
160 
intraoperative decision-making scenarios. Validation was conducted across multiple centers, 
161 
including pan-cancer analyses, rare disease subtypes, and challenging conditions (Fig. 1b-e).  
162 
To evaluate cross-modality generalizability, we further constructed a paired dataset comprising 
163 
frozen section and corresponding FFPE slides from the same 685 patients across GZH, HZH, 
164 
and DGH cohorts, with one-to-one matching to minimize sampling bias (Supplementary Fig. 
165 
S1). 
166 
To further bridge the gap between proof-of-concept and clinical translation, we conducted a 
167 
large-scale prospective study (Fig. 1f). This study enrolled 3,064 consecutive patients with 
168 
intraoperative consultation at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, Fudan University 
169 
Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) and Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and 
170 
Hospital (TCIH) between November 11, 2024, and July 1, 2025. Based on the tissue site, 
171 
patients from SYCC were assigned to three prospective cohorts: SYCC-THYR (n=1,330), 
172 
SYCC-BREA (n=391), and SYCC-LUNG (n=350) (Supplementary Table S1s). To assess the 
173 
practical feasibility of CRISP within clinical workflows, we conducted a simulated deployment 
174 
analysis using an independent set of 100 cases underwent intraoperative consultation, which 
175 
were not part of any training or evaluation cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S2). 
176 
 
177 
Classification of common benign and malignant lesions 
178 
To evaluate CRISP’s performance in this critical context, we validated the model using datasets 
179 
from five independent centers (Fig. 2a,b), spanning six common anatomical sites (thyroid, lung, 
180 
breast, brain, ovary, and head and neck), as well as complex abdominal lesion differentiation 
181 
tasks (Supplementary Table S2.1).  
182 
CRISP was benchmarked against several state-of-the-art pathology foundation models15-17,28,29 
183 
across 21 downstream diagnostic tasks. In malignancy discrimination, CRISP consistently 
184 
achieved superior performance, with an average AUROC of 0.970, an average accuracy of 0.945, 
185 
and an average F1 score of 0.924-significantly surpassing the next-best model (Fig. 2c; P < 
186 

PAGE 14
0.001, effect size r = 0.62). Notably, CRISP was the only model to achieve a median F1 score 
187 
above 0.9 (0.902; Fig. 2d).  
188 
CRISP achieved consistently higher performance across the 21 downstream tasks, with modest 
189 
but consistent improvements over other approaches (Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 1a). In the 
190 
subset of 14 tasks derived from previously unseen institutions, CRISP was the only model to 
191 
achieve an average AUC above 0.95 (0.967±0.018), maintaining AUC values greater than 0.9 
192 
across all tasks. Even in particularly challenging datasets—such as NFH-Abdomen and ZSH-
193 
Abdomen—or in heterogeneous multi-institutional data (ZHDG-Breast, ZHG-H&N, ZSGZ-
194 
Brain), CRISP sustained an average AUC of 0.957, outperforming Virchow2 by 3.5% (P < 
195 
0.001) (Supplementary Tables S2.2-2.9). 
196 
To further evaluate diagnostic sensitivity, we analyzed CRISP’s performance in detecting 
197 
malignant lesions across 1,729 cancer cases spanning 21 downstream tasks. The model 
198 
correctly identified 1,634 cases, yielding an overall sensitivity of 94.5% (Extended Data Fig. 
199 
1b). At sensitivity thresholds of 90% and 95%, CRISP achieved median specificities of 0.917 
200 
and 0.790, respectively, again significantly outperforming the second-best model (+8.0% and 
201 
+7.7% over Virchow2, P = 0.004 and P = 0.013; Fig. 2f). Comparative experiments showed 
202 
that slide-level and case-level aggregation achieved similar performance across cohorts, with 
203 
no consistent advantage observed for either strategy (Supplementary Table S2.10). 
204 
Visualization analyses further demonstrated CRISP’s ability to accurately localize malignant 
205 
regions within frozen tissue sections, supporting its capacity for sensitive and precise cancer 
206 
detection across a wide array of anatomical sites, institutions, and clinical contexts (Fig. 2g, 
207 
Supplementary Fig S3). 
208 
 
209 
Model evaluation in key intraoperative decision-making tasks 
210 
To rigorously evaluate CRISP’s generalizability and clinical utility, we established 20 multi-
211 
center downstream tasks spanning the entire surgical continuum: early planning, mid-procedure 
212 
guidance, and late intraoperative assessment (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table S3.1). These tasks 
213 
were designed to reflect the diversity and complexity of real-world clinical decisions, with 
214 
detailed clinical significance described in the Methods. Across both binary and multiclass tasks, 
215 
CRISP consistently achieved the highest performance among all evaluated models 
216 

PAGE 15
(Supplementary Table S3.2-3.3).  
217 
Early intraoperative decision: In both the SYCC and NFH cohorts (Fig. 3b, Extended Data 
218 
Fig. 2a), CRISP achieved AUCs of 0.998 and 0.987, respectively-representing numerical 
219 
improvements over the second-performing comparator (both P < 0.001; Fig. 3b-3c, Extended 
220 
Data Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table S3.4). Notably, CRISP correctly identified 64 of 66 patients 
221 
with PCNSL, whereas alternative models missed up to 2.5 times as many cases, underscoring 
222 
the clinical impact of enhanced diagnostic precision (Fig. 3d). In fine-grained tumor 
223 
classification, CRISP demonstrated a pronounced 20.4% gain in top-1 accuracy in the external 
224 
cohort (Fig. 3e, Extended Data Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table S3.5), highlighting its potential to 
225 
support nuanced early intraoperative decision-making. 
226 
Mid-procedure decision: CRISP was evaluated across eight cohorts spanning ovarian 
227 
(Ovarian Cancer Coarse/Fine-Grained Subtyping, OCCGS/OCFGS), thyroid (Thyroid Nodule 
228 
Differential Diagnosis, TNDD), and parathyroid tissue (Parathyroid Gland Detection, PGD), 
229 
consistently achieving the highest performance in all tasks, with a mean macro-AUC of 0.954, 
230 
compared to 0.934 for the second-best baseline (P < 0.001; Fig. 3f-3g, Extended Data Fig. 2d, 
231 
Supplementary Table S3.6-3.9). Confusion matrix analyses underscored CRISP's robust 
232 
discrimination between distinct tissue types (Extended Data Fig. 3). For ovarian and thyroid 
233 
tumors, where accurate subtype classification directly informs surgical strategy, CRISP showed 
234 
the highest top-k (k = 1, 2, 3) accuracies in external cohorts, with top-1 accuracy improving by 
235 
13.6% over CONCH (Fig. 3h-j). In PGD, a particularly challenging task, CRISP was the only 
236 
model to maintain AUCs consistently above 0.9. In external validation, it reached a specificity 
237 
of 0.559 at 95% sensitivity, outperforming all other models and demonstrating potential to 
238 
mitigate inadvertent parathyroid resection (Fig. 3k). Similar results were observed in internal 
239 
cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 2e-h), further supporting CRISP’s robustness in fine-grained 
240 
intraoperative decision-making. 
241 
Late intraoperative assessment: CRISP’s performance was evaluated across eight cohorts 
242 
focused on sentinel and cervical lymph node metastasis detection (SLMD, CLMD) and breast 
243 
cancer surgical margin assessment (BSMA) (Fig. 4a).30-32 The model achieved mean AUCs of 
244 
0.984, 0.992, and 0.992 across these tasks, outperforming all other evaluated models (Fig. 4b, 
245 
Supplementary Table S3.10-12). Under high-sensitivity conditions, CRISP demonstrated 
246 

PAGE 16
remarkable specificity. Among 263 positive patients, CRISP correctly identified 254 (96.6%), 
247 
representing a 40% reduction in missed diagnoses compared to the second-best model (Fig. 4c, 
248 
Extended Data Fig. 4). Visualization analyses further confirmed its ability to detect small 
249 
clusters of residual tumor cells (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. S4), supporting its utility in 
250 
postoperative assessment. 
251 
Across the complete set of 20 downstream tasks, CRISP consistently outperformed all 
252 
comparative models, which exhibited variable and dispersed performance (Fig. 4e). These 
253 
findings collectively validate CRISP’s generalizability and robust predictive power throughout 
254 
the entire surgical decision-making process, establishing it as a highly effective tool for 
255 
supporting precision intraoperative pathology in diverse clinical settings. 
256 
 
257 
Robustness under clinical heterogeneity 
258 
A defining measure of clinically-oriented foundation model is their capacity to generalize under 
259 
data-scarce conditions, particularly when confronted with unobserved samples and rare cancer 
260 
types-scenarios that frequently challenge conventional diagnostic approaches.15,33 To rigorously 
261 
evaluate this aspect, we conducted a large-scale pan-cancer detection study encompassing 
262 
13,851 intraoperative frozen slides from six independent centers (Supplementary Table S4.1). 
263 
The CRISP model was deployed to extract patch embeddings from these slides, with the 
264 
downstream aggregator trained exclusively on 1,573 slides from the SYCC cohort, representing 
265 
six common anatomical sites: lung, breast, thyroid, head and neck, brain, and ovary 
266 
(Supplementary Table S4.2).  
267 
Generalizability was systematically assessed across three external cohorts, which included both 
268 
six observed and more than eleven previously unseen cancer types (Fig. 5a, b; Supplementary 
269 
Table S4.3-4.5). Across these diverse datasets, CRISP consistently achieved high diagnostic 
270 
accuracy, with AUCs of 0.953, 0.973 and 0.960-each exceeding the 0.95 threshold and 
271 
outperforming all comparators (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Table S4.6). Notably, CRISP showed 
272 
pronounced advantages in the ZSH and HDGZ cohorts, with AUC improvements of 7.7% and 
273 
6.0% over the next-best model (P < 0.001), respectively, underscoring its robust cross-
274 
institutional generalizability. To assess the impact of adaptation strategies, we additionally 
275 
compared LoRA, full fine-tuning, IA³, and training from scratch under the same experimental 
276 

PAGE 17
settings (Supplementary Table S4.7). The results indicated that full fine-tuning did not 
277 
outperform LoRA and in some cases showed inferior generalization across cohorts, while LoRA 
278 
consistently achieved the best overall performance. 
279 
To further dissect model performance, we analyzed results across 17 site-specific subgroups, 
280 
encompassing both observed and unseen anatomical subtypes. CRISP consistently delivered 
281 
the highest AUCs, ranging from 0.914 to 1.0 (Fig. 5d). While all models exhibited some decline 
282 
in performance on unseen subgroups, CRISP maintained a distinct relative advantage, 
283 
outperforming the next-best model by 10.4% in lymph nodes, 9.1% in cervix, 9.7% in bile duct, 
284 
and 9.2% in pancreas. This suggests enhanced recognition and adaptability to unfamiliar cancer 
285 
morphologies. Across 51 subgroup analyses from three unseen cohorts, CRISP achieved the top 
286 
AUC in 47 and ranked within the top two in all, whereas Virchow2 led in only 16 unseen 
287 
subgroups (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Table S4.8-4.24), highlighting CRISP’s superior 
288 
adaptability to novel clinical scenarios. 
289 
CRISP’s performance in rare tumors was equally compelling (Fig. 5f). According to the 
290 
National Cancer Institute, rare cancers are defined as those with an annual incidence below 
291 
40,000 cases in the United States.34,35 Across seven rare tumor types, CRISP achieved an overall 
292 
AUC of 0.983, with cohort AUCs ranging from 0.963 to 0.993, consistently outperforming 
293 
Virchow2. Furthermore, CRISP exhibited remarkable stability, maintaining AUCs above 0.9 in 
294 
nearly all categories. In the ZSH-Else cohort, where all models performed less optimally (< 
295 
0.9), the results likely reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of tissue sources.  
296 
We further investigated the impact of unseen and rare samples on model performance (Fig. 5g, 
297 
h). CRISP’s diagnostic accuracy was minimally affected by either factor. Specifically, AUROC 
298 
improved by 0.023, 0.017, and 0.003 for rare versus common tumor types, while decreasing by 
299 
0.025, 0.022, and 0.036 for unseen versus seen tumor types. Even when analysis was restricted 
300 
to rare tumor subgroups from previously unseen anatomical sites, CRISP maintained an AUC 
301 
exceeding 0.95. Under high-sensitivity conditions, CRISP also achieved markedly higher 
302 
median specificity than the second-best model across both rare and unseen subgroups (Fig. 5i, 
303 
j). Comprehensive performance metrics are detailed in Supplementary Tables S4.25-4.29. To 
304 
evaluate cross-modality generalizability, we tested CRISP on matched FFPE slides from the 
305 
HDGZ cohort. Without additional fine-tuning, CRISP achieved an AUC of 0.961 on frozen 
306 

PAGE 18
sections and 0.972 on FFPE slides, indicating robust performance across different histological 
307 
preparation modalities (Supplementary Table S4.30). Across 98 retrospective evaluations, 
308 
CRISP achieved the highest AUROC in 92 tasks (93.9%), indicating a consistent performance 
309 
advantage across diverse settings (Supplementary Table S4.31). At fixed sensitivities of 90% 
310 
and 95%, CRISP consistently yielded higher specificities than competing foundation models, 
311 
with relative improvements reaching up to 63.4% in representative tasks (Supplementary Tables 
312 
S2.2, S3.2 and S4.6). Collectively, these findings demonstrate CRISP’s robust adaptability to 
313 
complex and heterogeneous clinical data sources, including data-scarce scenarios and rare 
314 
cancer types. This level of generalization is essential for real-world deployment, ensuring 
315 
reliable diagnostic support across the full spectrum of intraoperative pathology.

317 
Prospective validation of clinical utility 
318 
Prospective validation in real-world clinical environments is the definitive benchmark for 
319 
assessing the practical utility of diagnostic AI models.36 Building on retrospective findings, we 
320 
conducted a large-scale prospective observational study at SYCC, FUSCC and TCIH to 
321 
rigorously evaluate CRISP’s ability to support intraoperative decision-making under authentic 
322 
surgical conditions. A total of 3,064 consecutive patients were enrolled across multiple 
323 
clinically relevant frozen-section scenarios and anatomical sites. The prospective evaluation 
324 
included benign-malignant discrimination of pulmonary, thyroid, and breast nodules (PNBM, 
325 
TNBM, BNBM), cervical and sentinel lymph node metastasis detection (CLMD, SLMD), and 
326 
breast surgical margin assessment (BSMA), together with additional multi-organ frozen-section 
327 
cases from head and neck, ovary, and brain tissues. Importantly, the model’s aggregators-
328 
trained exclusively on retrospective data-were deployed directly, without task-specific fine-
329 
tuning, providing an unbiased assessment of CRISP’s robustness and translational potential 
330 
(Fig. 6a). 
331 
Crucially, CRISP demonstrated consistent superiority over competing foundation models 
332 
across independent prospective external cohorts at both FUSCC (AUROC = 0.966) and TCIH 
333 
(AUROC = 0.984), confirming its robustness against site bias (Supplementary Tables S5.1-2). 
334 
Across all six real-world tasks combined with the SYCC cohort, CRISP achieved an overall 
335 
AUC of 0.975 and outperforming all comparator models in every task (Fig. 6b, Supplementary 
336 

PAGE 19
Table S5.3). Compared to Virchow2, the second-best baseline, CRISP delivered AUC 
337 
improvements ranging from 3.2% to 5.9% and elevated the specificity at 95% sensitivity from 
338 
0.495 to 0.809 (+63.4%), underscoring its clinical relevance in high-sensitivity scenarios. 
339 
Similar performance trends were observed for sensitivity- and specificity-related metrics across 
340 
external cohorts. 
341 
In linear probing experiments, CRISP showed improved performance compared to competing 
342 
models, with a similar trend to aggregation-based evaluations (Supplementary Table S5.4). 
343 
CRISP maintained stable performance across varying perturbation settings, with only limited 
344 
degradation observed under increasing levels of image corruption (Supplementary Table S5.5). 
345 
In a reader study conducted on the prospective SYCC cohort, the operating point of the senior 
346 
pathologist fell on the ROC curve of CRISP, indicating comparable overall performance under 
347 
the evaluated conditions (Fig. 6c, Extended Data Fig. 5a-f). In contrast, the performance of two 
348 
junior pathologists fell below the ROC curve of CRISP, suggesting lower diagnostic accuracy 
349 
under the same evaluation setting. Similar diagnostic trends were observed in the sub-analysis 
350 
of 214 inter-pathologist discordant cases (Extended Data Fig. 5g). To further test resilience to 
351 
laboratory variability, the cohort was stratified into 27 subgroups based on histotechnologists 
352 
(Fig. 6d, Supplementary Table S5.6). CRISP maintained robust generalization, with 21 
353 
subgroups exceeding the overall AUC, 7 reaching perfect accuracy (AUC = 1.0), and all 
354 
others—except the multi-doctor “Else” subgroup—remaining above 0.95. Representative 
355 
histological examples and detailed subgroup compositions are provided in the Supplementary 
356 
Fig. S5 and Supplementary Table S5.7. These findings highlight CRISP’s ability to sustain high 
357 
diagnostic precision across diverse, prospective, and heterogeneous clinical conditions. 
358 
Extended evaluation incorporating additional recently proposed models (e.g., ProvGigaPath) 
359 
within the same benchmarking framework further confirmed the model's efficacy 
360 
(Supplementary Tables S2.2, 3.2–3.3, 4.6, 5.1, and 5.3).37 
361 
We further evaluated CRISP’s predictive accuracy in clinically challenging cases, defined by 
362 
high diagnostic uncertainty in intraoperative frozen reports (n = 371). CRISP achieved an 
363 
overall AUC of 0.936 in this subset (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Table S5.8), with performance 
364 
largely maintained in PNBM, BNBM, and CLMD tasks. Modest decreases were observed in 
365 
the TNBM (− 0.031), SLMD (− 0.041), and BSMA (− 0.047) tasks, yet score distributions 
366 

PAGE 20
remained well separated between benign and malignant cases (Fig. 6f). In diagnostically elusive 
367 
cases, such as sentinel lymph node micrometastases, CRISP correctly identified 7 of 8 
368 
micrometastatic and 40 of 41 macrometastatic cases (Fig. 6g), with predicted values showing 
369 
clear separation from negative controls (Fig. 6h). Visualization analyses revealed that CRISP 
370 
focused attention on peripheral zones-regions prone to metastatic involvement-and precisely 
371 
highlighted minute malignant areas (Fig. 6i, Supplementary Fig. S6). Error analysis showed 
372 
that false-positive predictions were mainly associated with rare benign tumors and frozen 
373 
section artifacts, while false-negative cases were primarily observed in rare malignant tumors 
374 
with ambiguous morphology (Supplementary Fig. S7).  
375 
To explore CRISP’s potential to alleviate clinical workload, we examined its utility in triaging 
376 
cases requiring immunohistochemistry (IHC), a common but resource-intensive diagnostic 
377 
adjunct. Among 510 patients requiring additional IHC evaluation, CRISP achieved an overall 
378 
AUC of 0.963, with task-specific AUCs ranging from 0.943 to 1.000 (Fig. 6j, Supplementary 
379 
Table S5.9). At 95% sensitivity, CRISP correctly identified 74.5% of IHC-negative patients, 
380 
underscoring its value as a screening tool to reduce unnecessary testing (Fig. 6k). We further 
381 
assessed whether CRISP could safely triage typical cases without compromising pathologists’ 
382 
diagnostic accuracy.38 When patients with predicted scores > 0.9956 or < 0.0044 were managed 
383 
directly by CRISP, the combined CRISP-pathologist workflow reduced diagnostic workload by 
384 
34.9%, with 79.0% of positive cases included (Fig. 6l). Under this strategy, sensitivity and 
385 
specificity remained comparable to conventional frozen-section reports (sensitivity, P = 0.60; 
386 
specificity, P = 1.00; Fig. 6m), highlighting CRISP’s potential as a clinical prescreening tool 
387 
for streamlining the diagnosis of typical positive cases. The workload reduction and 
388 
corresponding decrease in immunohistochemistry usage were further analyzed separately 
389 
across all subtasks and multi-region prospective cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 6,7,8a-b). In a 
390 
simulated deployment experiment conducted on 100 cases from SYCC, the average end-to-end 
391 
processing time for CRISP was 196.4 seconds per case, compared to 292.6 seconds for 
392 
pathologists (Extended Data Fig. 9). The pre-screening strategy identified 65 typical cases, and 
393 
this approach did not result in any degradation in sensitivity or specificity (Extended Data Fig. 
394 
8c). Notably, CRISP assistance increased pathologists’ diagnostic confidence by 6.0% in typical 
395 
cases and 42.9% in diagnostically uncertain cases, suggesting its potential to support decision-
396 

PAGE 21
making particularly in challenging scenarios (Extended Data Fig. 10).

398 
Discussion 
399 
Foundation models have redefined the landscape of artificial intelligence, but their translation 
400 
into high-stakes clinical environments has remained elusive.39,40 Nowhere is this gap more 
401 
consequential than in surgery, where more than 310 million major surgical procedures are 
402 
performed worldwide each year, and intraoperative decisions must balance speed with 
403 
diagnostic precision.41,42 The consequences of diagnostic uncertainty are profound, leading to 
404 
unnecessary reoperations, overtreatment, or undertreatment, and imposing substantial 
405 
economic burdens that exceed one billion dollars annually in the United States alone.43 Against 
406 
this backdrop, the development of a clinically reliable AI platform capable of guiding 
407 
intraoperative decisions represents a paradigm shift-not only for surgical pathology, but also 
408 
for the broader clinical adoption of foundation models in medicine. 
409 
In this study, we present CRISP, the first clinically oriented foundation model specifically 
410 
dedicated to intraoperative pathology. Distinct from existing pathological foundation models 
411 
trained on FFPE specimens, CRISP leverages large-scale frozen section data for pre-training. 
412 
This strategic focus directly addresses a critical clinical gap: FFPE-trained models often falter 
413 
in intraoperative settings due to frozen section-specific artifacts such as ice crystal formation 
414 
and tissue compression. By constructing a large and diverse dataset sourced from ten 
415 
independent medical centers and reserving data from seven centers exclusively for external 
416 
validation, we ensured rigorous assessment of CRISP’s diagnostic performance under authentic, 
417 
heterogeneous clinical conditions. The inclusion of public frozen section data increases 
418 
morphological diversity during pretraining, with potential to improve robustness to 
419 
heterogeneous data. Furthermore, prospective real-time deployment provided a robust test of 
420 
the model’s reliability in actual surgical workflows. 
421 
CRISP’s robust performance across a spectrum of intraoperative diagnostic tasks highlights its 
422 
potential as a generalizable paradigm for frozen section-based decision support. Beyond 
423 
achieving superior accuracy in benchmark comparisons, CRISP demonstrated the ability to 
424 
address clinically critical scenarios-such as the detection of subtle malignant foci in surgical 
425 
margins or lymph nodes-where diagnostic sensitivity is paramount to avoid undertreatment.44,45 
426 

PAGE 22
Importantly, CRISP maintained consistent advantages across the entire surgical workflow, from 
427 
early intraoperative differential diagnosis to mid-procedure tumor classification and late  
428 
assessment of resection completeness. CRISP demonstrates stable performance across FFPE 
429 
data and common perturbations, indicating robustness to variations in staining and image 
430 
quality encountered in clinical practice. Furthermore, CRISP’s transferability to routine FFPE 
431 
slides shows it captures cross-modality morphological invariants, offering potential to unify 
432 
intraoperative guidance and post-operative pathology under a single framework. Given that 
433 
misinterpretation of intraoperative frozen sections can lead to inappropriate surgical decisions 
434 
and increased patient risk,46,47 CRISP’s breadth of applicability suggests it could substantially 
435 
reduce variability and time constraints, offering a scalable and clinically meaningful 
436 
complement to routine pathology practice. While absolute improvements in AUROC are 
437 
modest in high-performance regimes, such differences are expected due to ceiling effects and 
438 
should be interpreted in the context of already strong baselines. Moreover, the consistent 
439 
performance advantage across diverse organs, institutions, and downstream tasks suggests that 
440 
the observed improvements are unlikely to arise from optimization for individual benchmarks 
441 
alone, but instead reflect enhanced generalization of the pretrained representation. Importantly, 
442 
these advantages were further amplified under clinically relevant high-sensitivity operating 
443 
conditions. CRISP operates on standard frozen section slides and supports multi-cancer 
444 
scenarios, enabling broader generalizability and easier integration into routine clinical 
445 
workflows compared with prior approaches.48 
446 
A key strength of CRISP lies in its capacity to learn generalized morphological features, 
447 
enabling robust generalization even under previously unseen laboratory conditions.49 The 
448 
model maintained high predictive accuracy for tissue types with scarce or absent training 
449 
samples-a crucial benchmark for foundation model generalizability. When applied to 
450 
previously unseen institutional data and novel samples, CRISP-based pan-cancer detection 
451 
models demonstrated stable and reliable performance, outperforming alternative models by up 
452 
to 11% on challenging datasets. This provides compelling evidence that CRISP has effectively 
453 
internalized universal tissue morphology from large-scale frozen sections, equipping it to 
454 
navigate the complexity of diverse clinical scenarios. Notably, CRISP also excelled in the 
455 
diagnosis of rare diseases, addressing a common challenge in clinical practice where labeled 
456 

PAGE 23
data are limited and expertise may be lacking.50-52 Such capabilities position CRISP as a 
457 
valuable tool for resource-limited settings, potentially mitigating disparities in healthcare 
458 
access and supporting expert-level pathology where it is most needed.53 
459 
Prospective evaluation further reinforced CRISP’s feasibility as a clinically oriented tool. When 
460 
deployed in real-time intraoperative settings, CRISP demonstrated robust performance across 
461 
diverse patient cohorts and laboratory conditions, bridging the gap between experimental 
462 
development and practical implementation. The reader study further supports the clinical 
463 
relevance of CRISP, demonstrating performance comparable to an experienced pathologist 
464 
under time-constrained intraoperative conditions. Remarkably, CRISP alone enabled precise 
465 
surgical intervention in 92.6% of patients. The diagnostic errors are concentrated in 
466 
diagnostically challenging scenarios, which are also known to exhibit substantial inter-observer 
467 
variability, highlighting the importance of cautious interpretation in such cases. Future work 
468 
may address these limitations by incorporating more diverse training data and integrating 
469 
uncertainty-aware or human-in-the-loop strategies. In contexts where clinical resources are 
470 
limited, the ability to optimize resource allocation becomes essential.54,55 In routine cases, 
471 
CRISP-based triage reduced pathologists’ workload by 35% and prevented 75% of unnecessary 
472 
ancillary testing, while in complex cases, it provided a standardized reference to support 
473 
diagnostic reconsideration and reduce missed diagnoses. Additionally, CRISP demonstrates 
474 
comparable end-to-end processing time to pathologists in simulated intraoperative settings, 
475 
indicating its potential to provide timely support. These results highlight CRISP’s potential for 
476 
seamless integration into intraoperative diagnostic workflows, with tangible benefits for both 
477 
clinical efficiency and patient safety. Beyond pre-screening, CRISP could be integrated into 
478 
hybrid workflows through direct presentation of model predictions, fusion of algorithmic 
479 
outputs with expert assessments, or incorporation into agent-based diagnostic systems. Given 
480 
its robustness and generalizability, CRISP is well-positioned to be compatible with and readily 
481 
integrated into these collaborative paradigms. 
482 
Despite these promising findings, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while 
483 
extensive retrospective and prospective validation was performed on nearly 20,000 multi-center 
484 
intraoperative frozen sections and over 3,000 prospective multi-site cases, a fully prospective 
485 
interventional study is still necessary to assess its real-world deployment and impact on clinical 
486 

PAGE 24
outcomes.56,57 Second, although CRISP demonstrated strong generalization across multiple 
487 
centers, further validation in more diverse ethnic and geographic populations is essential to 
488 
account for potential population-level bias and ensure global applicability.58,59 In addition, 
489 
broader long-term multi-institutional deployment may further support continuous monitoring 
490 
of real-world clinical performance. Finally, large-scale deployment and productization will 
491 
require addressing technical and operational challenges common to AI clinical translation, 
492 
including integration with existing workflows, regulatory compliance, and user training.60 
493 
In summary, CRISP establishes a versatile and clinically relevant AI paradigm for 
494 
intraoperative pathology, illustrating the transformative potential of large-scale, data-driven 
495 
approaches to advance precision surgery. By bridging the gap between technological innovation 
496 
and clinical practice, CRISP paves the way for the next generation of AI-enabled surgical 
497 
decision support, with broad implications for improving patient outcomes and healthcare 
498 
delivery worldwide.

810 
Large-scale intraoperative frozen section dataset 
811 
To develop a clinically-oriented foundation model for intraoperative pathology, we constructed 
812 
a large-scale dataset of frozen section whole-slide images (WSIs), comprising 103,797 slides 
813 
from 51,186 patients. Retrospective data were collected from eight independent medical centers, 
814 
including Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYCC), Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical 
815 
University (ZJH), Jiangmen Central Hospital (JMH), Zhongshan City People’s Hospital (ZSH), 
816 
Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (NFH), Affiliated Cancer Hospital & Institute 
817 
of Guangzhou Medical University (GZH), Huizhou Central People’s Hospital (HZH), and The 
818 
Tenth Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University, Dongguan People’s Hospital (DGH). 
819 
All slides collected from the participating centers were obtained from routine clinical 
820 
intraoperative consultations. In total, these cohorts encompassed 38,253 patients and 81,678 
821 
intraoperative frozen WSIs collected between May 2017 and July 2024. The following 
822 
inclusion criteria were applied to all cases: (1) at least one available intraoperative frozen 
823 
section; (2) presence of identifiable tissue on the slide; (3) absence of scanning artifacts such 
824 
as defocus or blurring; and (4) availability of complete diagnostic information, including 
825 
intraoperative frozen section and corresponding postoperative paraffin section reports, as well 
826 
as basic clinical characteristics (e.g., age and sex). Slides were manually reviewed by two 
827 

PAGE 34
experienced pathologists based on thumbnail inspection to assess tissue presence and image 
828 
quality, and only those with severe scanning-related artifacts (e.g., out-of-focus regions or 
829 
extensive image degradation) that precluded tissue recognition were excluded, while intrinsic 
830 
artifacts inherent to frozen section preparation were retained. The detailed inclusion period, 
831 
number of patients, number of slides, and baseline characteristics of each cohort are 
832 
summarized in Supplementary Tables S1.1-1.8. To further increase histological diversity, we 
833 
additionally curated 15,975 frozen WSIs from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). The detailed 
834 
selection process was as follows: diagnostic slides prepared from formalin-fixed paraffin-
835 
embedded (FFPE) tissues were excluded, resulting in 21,094 slides. These were subsequently 
836 
reviewed by two experienced pathologists, and immunohistochemistry as well as other non-
837 
frozen section slides were removed. To ensure consistency, all patients were categorized into 
838 
24 major anatomical sites according to the definitions provided by the Genomic Data Commons 
839 
(GDC) system.61 For model development, WSIs from ZJH, JMH, TCGA, and a subset of SYCC 
840 
(43,013 slides) were used for CRISP pretraining. The remaining SYCC slides (5,178) were 
841 
reserved for downstream task development and internal evaluation, ensuring no data leakage 
842 
between pretraining and testing. External validation was performed using ZSH, NFH, GZH, 
843 
HZH, and DGH cohorts.  
844 
For the prospective real-world deployment study of CRISP, patients undergoing thyroid, breast, 
845 
or lung surgery with intraoperative consultation at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center were 
846 
consecutively recruited between November 2024 and July 2025, representing the most common 
847 
sites for intraoperative consultation. Inclusion criteria were: (1) at least one available 
848 
intraoperative frozen section; (2) presence of identifiable tissue on the slide; (3) absence of 
849 
scanning artifacts such as defocus or blurring; (4) complete diagnostic and clinical information; 
850 
(5) age ≥18 years at the time of surgery; and (6) documented informed consent. Following 
851 
screening, a total of 2,071 patients with 4,143 intraoperative frozen sections were included. To 
852 
further assess geographical generalizability, two additional prospective cohorts from FUSCC 
853 
and TCIH were included, with the same inclusion criteria applied. To simulate real-world 
854 
application, 100 consecutive cases from SYCC were included for simulated clinical deployment 
855 
analysis. Recruitment periods and detailed clinical characteristics for each cohort are provided 
856 
in Supplementary Tables S1.9-1.11.

Data digitization and preprocessing  
859 
All clinical intraoperative frozen sections included in this study were digitized at 40× 
860 
magnification (0.25 μm/pixel). Slides from the SYCC cohort were primarily scanned using an 
861 
Aperio AT2 scanner (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and stored in SVS format. Data 
862 
from the ZJH, GZH, HZH, and DGH cohorts were scanned with a PHILIPS Ultra-Fast Scanner 
863 
(Philips Electronics N.V., Amsterdam, Netherlands) and saved in iSyntax format. NFH and 
864 
ZSH cohort slides were mainly scanned using a KF-PRO-120-HI scanner (Jiangfeng Biotech, 
865 
Ningbo, China) and stored in KFB format. JMH cohort slides were scanned using an SQS-600P 
866 
scanner (Shengqiang Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and saved in SDPC format. 
867 
Slides from the prospective SYCC cohort were mainly scanned with a PHILIPS Ultra-Fast 
868 
Scanner (Philips Electronics N.V., Amsterdam, Netherlands) and saved in iSyntax format. 
869 
Slides from the FUSCC and TCIH cohorts were mainly scanned using an Aperio AT2 scanner 
870 
(Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and stored in SVS format. 
871 
WSIs were processed using vendor-provided API tools to extract full-slide thumbnail images. 
872 
Given the extensive presence of non-informative background areas, tissue regions were 
873 
automatically segmented using the CLAM toolkit to identify and isolate relevant histological 
874 
content for subsequent analysis.52 Segmented tissue regions were partitioned into a series of 
875 
non-overlapping patches of 512×512 pixels at a resolution of 0.25 microns per pixel (MPP). 
876 
To mitigate center-specific biases introduced by variations in optical resolution, slide 
877 
preparation, and scanning settings, redundant patches from higher-resolution images were 
878 
downsampled. This approach ensured a balanced representation of patches from each center, 
879 
reducing the risk of model overfitting to specific institutional characteristics. In total, 
880 
approximately 50 million patches were extracted from 81,667 slides for model pretraining. 
881 
No color normalization (e.g., Vahadane or Macenko) was applied in order to preserve inter-
882 
institutional variability and reflect real-world clinical conditions.

884 
Network architecture 
885 
Building on previous studies of FFPE-based pathology foundation models, we focus here on 
886 
intraoperative frozen sections and introduce CRISP, a specialized pathology foundation model 
887 

PAGE 36
for their analysis. CRISP is built upon Virchow2, a state-of-the-art pathology foundation model 
888 
pretrained on 1 million hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained WSIs.15,29 Virchow2 was selected 
889 
as the backbone model based on prior large-scale benchmarking studies demonstrating its 
890 
strong overall performance across multiple pathology tasks.18 To adapt this general-purpose 
891 
model to the frozen section domain while preserving its learned representations and 
892 
generalizability, we employed low-rank adaptation (LoRA).62 LoRA is a parameter-efficient 
893 
fine-tuning (PEFT) method that injects trainable rank decomposition matrices into the self-
894 
attention layers of the transformer architecture, keeping the remaining model weights frozen 
895 
during training. This approach drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters, 
896 
computational overhead, and risk of catastrophic forgetting. In this study, LoRA modules were 
897 
integrated into the query and value projection matrices of each attention layer, initialized with 
898 
a rank of 8 and a scaling factor of 16. This configuration resulted in only 2 million trainable 
899 
parameters, constituting merely 0.3% of the model’s total 633 million parameters. 
900 
 
901 
Model training 
902 
For pretraining, we utilized DINO, a self-supervised learning strategy effective for learning 
903 
visual representations without labeled data.63 Specifically, DINO operates on a teacher-student 
904 
paradigm in which two different augmented views of the same image are processed by the 
905 
student and teacher networks, respectively. The student network learns to predict the output 
906 
distribution of the teacher network, thereby encouraging the learning of semantically 
907 
meaningful features without manual annotation. The teacher network’s weights are updated via 
908 
an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student weights, with a momentum coefficient 
909 
set to 0.9995. The model was trained using the AdamW optimizer (𝛽1= 0.9, 𝛽2= 0.999) with 
910 
mixed-precision (FP16) arithmetic to accelerate training and reduce memory consumption. 
911 
Pretraining was conducted using 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs with a batch size of 384 for 640,000 
912 
iterations. 
913 
For downstream intraoperative tasks, a slide-level prediction pipeline was implemented. In 
914 
computational pathology, WSIs are typically too large to be processed directly by deep learning 
915 
models. Therefore, the analysis pipeline was divided into three steps: patch-level feature 
916 
extraction, feature aggregation, and slide-level prediction. In this study, each 512×512 patch 
917 

PAGE 37
(at 0.25 MPP) was resized to 224×224 pixels to serve as input to the frozen CRISP encoder; 
918 
this corresponds to the same field of view as 256×256 patches at 0.5 MPP, a commonly adopted 
919 
setting in baseline models, thereby ensuring consistent input resolution and fair comparison 
920 
across models.16,37 From the final layer of the transformer, the [CLS] token embedding and the 
921 
mean-pooled embeddings of all patch tokens were concatenated, forming a comprehensive 
922 
2,560-dimensional feature vector for each patch. To identify diagnostically critical regions, we 
923 
employed an attention-based multiple instance learning (ABMIL) model as the feature 
924 
aggregator.64 The ABMIL module learns to assign an attention weight to each patch feature 
925 
vector, effectively highlighting salient patches and generating a weighted slide-level feature 
926 
representation. This aggregated feature vector was then passed through a single fully connected 
927 
classification layer to produce the final slide-level prediction. For supervised downstream tasks, 
928 
the cross-entropy loss function was used. Only the ABMIL aggregator and the classification 
929 
layer were trained from scratch, while the CRISP encoder remained frozen to leverage the 
930 
pretrained features. The model was optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 
931 
2×10-4 and weight decay of 1×10⁻⁵. Early stopping based on validation loss was implemented 
932 
to halt training upon performance saturation and prevent overfitting. In all downstream task 
933 
comparisons, identical strategies were employed across comparator models to guarantee 
934 
fairness in performance assessment. 
935 
 
936 
Downstream task  
937 
To comprehensively evaluate CRISP, 98 retrospective downstream tasks were established. The 
938 
reference labels for all tasks were based on postoperative FFPE morphology integrated with 
939 
immunohistochemical and molecular analyses, and were independently verified by a 
940 
pathologist. Aggregator development for all downstream tasks was conducted using the SYCC 
941 
cohort, with patients in the internal test sets kept independent from the training and validation 
942 
sets to prevent data leakage. Data were randomly split at the patient level into training, 
943 
validation, and test sets to prevent data leakage, with stratification by outcome labels; identical 
944 
splits were used for all models to ensure fair comparison. No explicit rebalancing strategies 
945 
were applied in order to preserve the original class distribution. A shared set of hyperparameters 
946 
was used across all tasks and models to ensure a fair comparison. Baseline models were selected 
947 

PAGE 38
based on their relevance to histopathology foundation modeling, as well as the availability of 
948 
publicly accessible implementations or pretrained weights, to ensure reproducibility and fair 
949 
comparison. 
950 
Classification of common benign and malignant lesions: Each benign-malignant 
951 
classification task was formulated as an independent slide-level binary problem, with slides 
952 
containing malignant regions designated as positive. Six prevalent tissue sites were included: 
953 
lung, breast, thyroid, head and neck, brain, and ovary. The task encompassed commonly 
954 
encountered benign and malignant lesions, such as pulmonary adenocarcinoma and 
955 
inflammatory nodules, to replicate large-scale clinical pre-screening scenarios. The abdominal 
956 
metastasis classification task was incorporated to evaluate CRISP’s capability in distinguishing 
957 
complex metastatic lesions. Abdominal metastases represent a frequent dissemination route for 
958 
multiple cancers and often signal disease progression; accurate identification can therefore 
959 
inform patient management and improve clinical outcomes.65,66 Malignant abdominal nodules 
960 
comprised diverse metastatic cancers, including ovarian, intestinal, and gastric origins 
961 
(Supplementary Table S6.1). Model performance was assessed on 2,771 slides across five 
962 
external cohorts, with dataset partitions detailed in Supplementary Table S2.1. For comparison, 
963 
a case-level aggregation strategy was implemented by concatenating slide-level features from 
964 
the same case to form case-level representations. Identical data splits and evaluation protocols 
965 
were used to ensure fair comparison. 
966 
Early intraoperative decision: The primary central nervous system lymphoma differentiation 
967 
(PCNSLD) task was defined as a slide-level binary classification, aimed at accurately 
968 
distinguishing primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) from other brain lesions. 
969 
PCNSL is recognized as one of the most common primary malignant brain tumors, exhibiting 
970 
poor prognosis, presenting diagnostic challenges, and being primarily managed with 
971 
radiotherapy and chemotherapy, in contrast to other brain lesions.67 Accurate intraoperative 
972 
identification of PCNSL can prevent unnecessary surgical interventions and facilitate precision 
973 
treatment. Slides containing PCNSL were labeled positive, whereas slides of other common 
974 
brain lesions—including gliomas, medulloblastomas, central neurocytomas, metastases, and 
975 
inflammatory lesions—were labeled negative. External validation was performed using 461 
976 
slides from the NFH cohort. The central nervous system lesion classification (CNSLC) task 
977 

PAGE 39
comprised the same dataset but was formulated as a fine-grained multi-class classification to 
978 
distinguish among the aforementioned lesion types. Detailed dataset partitions for 
979 
intraoperative decision tasks are provided in Supplementary Table S3.1. 
980 
Mid-procedure decision: The ovarian cancer coarse-grained subtyping (OCCGS) task 
981 
involved slide-level three-class classification to differentiate primary ovarian carcinomas, 
982 
metastatic ovarian tumors, and benign ovarian lesions. As the ovary is a common site of 
983 
metastasis from extragenital malignancies, distinguishing primary from metastatic tumors 
984 
remains a critical diagnostic challenge during surgery.68,69 Accurate classification informs the 
985 
surgical extent and guides additional treatment directed at the primary site. The ovarian cancer 
986 
fine-grained subtyping (OCCFS) task shared the same dataset but focused on fine-grained 
987 
classification of primary ovarian carcinoma subtypes, including serous, clear cell, and 
988 
endometrioid adenocarcinomas. 
989 
The thyroid nodule differential diagnosis (TNDD) task involved a slide-level three-class 
990 
classification of thyroid nodules into papillary thyroid carcinoma, follicular-patterned tumors, 
991 
and benign lesions such as nodular goiter. Frozen section diagnosis of thyroid nodules is critical 
992 
for intraoperative surgical decision-making, as papillary carcinoma and nodular goiter often 
993 
dictate different surgical extents, while follicular-patterned tumors typically require diagnostic 
994 
surgery pending definitive FFPE evaluation.70  
995 
The parathyroid gland detection (PGD) task was a slide-level binary classification for detecting 
996 
parathyroid tissue in thyroidectomy frozen sections. Inadvertent parathyroid resection, 
997 
occurring in up to 16% of thyroidectomies, is the primary cause of postoperative 
998 
hypocalcemia.71,72 Accurate frozen-section recognition of parathyroid tissue is therefore 
999 
essential for preventing misresection and optimizing surgical outcomes. Labels were assigned 
1000 
solely based on the presence of parathyroid tissue, with all other tissues—including thyroid, 
1001 
lymph nodes, and thyroid carcinoma—classified as negative, reflecting real-world 
1002 
intraoperative settings. 
1003 
Late intraoperative assessment: Sentinel lymph node metastasis detection (SLMD), cervical 
1004 
lymph node metastasis detection (CLMD), and breast cancer surgical margin assessment 
1005 
(BSMA) are three slide-level binary classification tasks designed to detect residual tumor cells 
1006 
in lymph nodes and surgical margins. Sentinel lymph node assessment and margin evaluation 
1007 

PAGE 40
are critical indicators of surgical completeness and directly influence patient prognosis and risk 
1008 
of recurrence.73,74 In thyroid cancer, accurate intraoperative identification of central lymph node 
1009 
metastasis can help avoid unnecessary dissections, whereas in breast cancer, margin and 
1010 
sentinel lymph node assessment form the basis of individualized treatment.13,75,76 
1011 
Pan-cancer detection study: The pan-cancer detection task was designed to develop a 
1012 
universal model capable of accurately identifying malignant lesions across intraoperative 
1013 
frozen samples of any source or type, thereby evaluating CRISP performance under data-
1014 
limited conditions, particularly in unseen samples and rare cancers. Model development was 
1015 
based on 3,071 benign and malignant WSIs from six common frozen tissue sites in the SYCC 
1016 
cohort. Given that clinical decision-making is case-based, external validation was conducted at 
1017 
the case level. For each case, positivity was defined as the presence of at least one malignant 
1018 
slide. All slide-level features from a case were concatenated and directly input into the 
1019 
aggregator as case-level features. The model was validated across five external cohorts 
1020 
comprising 5,867 cases: ZSH and NFH served as two independent cohorts, whereas GZH, HZH, 
1021 
and DGH were combined into a single cohort. Detailed dataset partitions are provided in 
1022 
Supplementary Tables S4.2-4.5. For subgroup analyses, eight tissue sites (stomach, bone, 
1023 
esophagus, kidney, bladder, adrenal gland, testis, and thymus) were combined as “Else”,  
1024 
owing to limited sample sizes or unidirectional labels in external cohorts, which precluded 
1025 
reliable calculation of metrics such as AUROC.  
1026 
 
1027 
Prospective cohort study 
1028 
In the prospective cohort, the diagnostic reference standard for each patient was established 
1029 
based on the postoperative FFPE pathology report in combination with immunohistochemistry 
1030 
and molecular test results, and independently verified by a board-certified pathologist blinded 
1031 
to the study. Patients from SYCC were assigned to one of six intraoperative decision tasks 
1032 
(PNBM, TNBM, BNBM, CLMD, SLMD, BSMA) according to the surgical indication and 
1033 
sample type. Detailed dataset partitions are provided in Figure 2b. Aggregators used for 
1034 
validation in these six tasks were directly transferred from retrospective downstream task 
1035 
construction without any task-specific fine-tuning. For clinical applicability, validation was 
1036 
performed at the patient level. 
1037 

PAGE 41
Information on frozen-section slide preparation personnel was obtained from intraoperative 
1038 
reports, encompassing a total of 30 histotechnologists; four with single-class labels were not 
1039 
suitable for AUROC-based evaluation and were therefore grouped into an “Else” category for 
1040 
analysis. Diagnostic outcomes and confidence levels were derived from the content of the 
1041 
intraoperative frozen-section reports. Confidence assessment was conducted according to a 
1042 
prespecified rule set: reports indicating “consistent with”, “consider”, or “favor” were 
1043 
categorized as relatively definitive, whereas reports stating “not excluded”, “suspicious for”, or 
1044 
“requires differential diagnosis” were categorized as indeterminate (Supplementary Table S6.2). 
1045 
All classifications were independently reviewed and confirmed by a pathologist blinded to the 
1046 
study. Immunohistochemistry requirements were similarly inferred from the report content. 
1047 
The upper and lower thresholds used for coarse screening were defined based on the range of 
1048 
prediction scores observed in misclassified cases, such that samples within the retained range 
1049 
were classified with near-perfect accuracy by the model. 
1050 
To evaluate clinical performance, a reader study was conducted using the entire prospective 
1051 
SYCC cohort. One senior pathologist (Pathologist 1; 10 years of diagnostic experience) and 
1052 
two junior pathologists (Pathologist 2, 3; 2 years of experience) independently reviewed all 
1053 
cases, blinded to model predictions and study design. Readers were asked to perform diagnostic 
1054 
classification across multiple clinically relevant tasks, consistent with the study endpoints. All 
1055 
reader predictions were recorded for subsequent analysis and comparison with model outputs. 
1056 
To simulate intraoperative conditions during the reader study, each case was evaluated under a 
1057 
time constraint of 5 minutes. Linear probing experiments were conducted on multi-organ 
1058 
benign-versus-malignant classification tasks (lung, breast, and thyroid) to assess the intrinsic 
1059 
discriminative capacity and transferability of pretrained features. A standardized benchmark 
1060 
subset comprising 75 frozen-section slides from an independent SYCC cohort, not used during 
1061 
foundation model pretraining, was constructed for evaluation, with regions of interest annotated 
1062 
by an independent pathologist. For the linear probing experiments, fixed feature representations 
1063 
were extracted from each foundation model, and logistic regression classifiers were trained on 
1064 
top without updating the backbone parameters, applying identical data splits and 
1065 
hyperparameters across all models to ensure fair comparison. To evaluate robustness, we further 
1066 
assessed model performance under common perturbations, including color shifts, image blur, 
1067 

PAGE 42
additive noise, and reduced image resolution, based on the linear probing setting. The entire 
1068 
process was designed as a largely automated pipeline (slide scanning → data upload → model 
1069 
inference → result output), with minimal manual intervention required. Regarding the 
1070 
simulated deployment analysis, whole-slide images were digitized using six PHILIPS Ultra-
1071 
Fast Scanners in parallel and automatically uploaded to a local server via the SYCC intranet. 
1072 
Model inference was performed on a server equipped with two NVIDIA A100 GPUs, and 
1073 
results were returned via an internal web-based interface. For the pre-screening strategy, the 
1074 
same threshold and decision criteria used in the prospective SYCC cohort were applied to this 
1075 
independent dataset. 
1076 
Micro-metastases and macro-metastases were defined according to AJCC guidelines and 
1077 
determined based on both intraoperative frozen-section and postoperative FFPE pathology 
1078 
reports, and were independently morphologically reviewed by a pathologist blinded to the study. 
1079 
Micro-metastases were defined as malignant foci within sentinel lymph nodes measuring 
1080 
greater than 0.2 mm but not exceeding 2 mm, whereas macro-metastases were defined as 
1081 
malignant foci larger than 2 mm.77

1083 
Statistical analysis 
1084 
For binary classification tasks, model performance was evaluated using the area under the 
1085 
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), F1 score, accuracy, and specificity at 90% 
1086 
and 95% sensitivity thresholds. For multi-class classification tasks, performance was assessed 
1087 
using macro-averaged AUROC and top-k accuracy. For each case, logits from multiple classes 
1088 
were combined using a weighted summation, followed by a sigmoid transformation to rescale 
1089 
the aggregated value into a unified CRISP score between 0 and 1. For binary classification tasks, 
1090 
optimal thresholds were determined using the Youden index on the internal validation set and 
1091 
subsequently applied to external test cohorts; for multi-class tasks, predictions were assigned 
1092 
based on the class with the highest logit. To compare overall model performance across multiple 
1093 
tasks, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed, and p-values were adjusted for 
1094 
multiple comparisons using the Holm method.78 The effect size r is derived from the formula r 
1095 
= Z / sqrt(N), with Z representing the standard normal deviate corresponding to U, and N 
1096 
denoting the combined sample size. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed 
1097 

PAGE 43
via bootstrap with 1,000 iterations. Differences in predicted value distributions for multi-class 
1098 
tasks were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s 
1099 
honestly significant difference (HSD) test for pairwise comparisons. For binary tasks, group 
1100 
differences in predicted score distributions were evaluated using two-sided Student’s t-tests; 
1101 
non-parametric alternatives were considered when normality assumptions were not met. 
1102 
Statistical significance was defined as not significant (P > 0.05), * (P ≤ 0.05), ** (P ≤ 0.01) 
1103 
and *** (P ≤ 0.001). Boxplots depict the median (50th percentile), interquartile range (25th-
1104 
75th percentile); whiskers extend to the non-outlier range; outliers are plotted individually. Data 
1105 
analysis was performed using R version 4.2.1 (2022-06-23) and Python 3.13.5 using the 
1106 
numpy (version 2.2.3), scipy (version 1.15.3) and scikit-learn (version 1.7.0) packages, and 
1107 
deep learning models were implemented in PyTorch 2.7.1.
