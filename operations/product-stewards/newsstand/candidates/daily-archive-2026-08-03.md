## THE LEAD

# Europe’s AI transparency rules started August 2. Here’s when you should expect a label.

If you use AI in Europe, or encounter material made with AI, you may now receive clearer information about what you are dealing with. Article 50 of the European Union’s AI Act began applying on August 2, 2026.

That does **not** mean every use of AI must carry the same sticker. The duty depends on who is providing or using the system, what the system does, what kind of material it produces and which exceptions apply.

The useful part for an ordinary reader is simpler: in covered situations, you should have more help recognizing when you are interacting with AI or encountering particular AI-generated or manipulated material.

## What changed

The European Commission’s current guidance separates two jobs.

**Providers** of certain AI systems must design them so that people are informed when they are interacting directly with AI. Providers also have marking duties for certain AI-generated or manipulated text, images, video and audio so that the material’s origin can be detected reliably by machines.

**Professional deployers**—the organizations using the systems—have separate disclosure duties in specified situations. Those include emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems, deepfakes, and some AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public when it has not received human review or editorial control.

There are exceptions and transition rules. For example, the Commission describes a grace period to December 2026 for the marking obligation for certain generative AI systems placed on the market before August 2. That is one reason a professional user should consult the current guidance for her exact system and role rather than turn a newspaper article into a compliance checklist.

## What a label can tell you

A disclosure can tell you that AI is involved. A machine-readable mark can help systems trace synthetic or manipulated material. A visible label can give you useful origin context before you decide what to do next.

Think of it as caller ID. Knowing where the call appears to come from can be valuable.

## What a label cannot tell you

Caller ID cannot prove everything the caller says. In the same way, an AI label does not prove that the labelled claim is accurate, fair, safe or lawful.

It also works in the other direction: the absence of a label does not prove that a person made the item or that every applicable duty was followed. A label is one piece of evidence about origin—not a complete verdict about the content.

The next questions belong to you:

1. What exact claim is this item asking me to believe?
2. What kind of evidence would support that claim?
3. Can I find the original study, record, data or official source?
4. Is it current, and does its scope actually match the claim?
5. After checking, should I use it, share it, qualify it or leave it alone?

At work, imagine a campaign image carrying an AI-origin mark beside a polished statistic. The mark helps identify how the image was made. It does not verify the statistic, confirm that your organization approved the image or make the campaign accurate. Those remain separate checks.

At home, imagine a labelled AI-generated health graphic arriving in the family group chat. The label tells you something important about the graphic’s origin. Before forwarding it, you still trace the health claim to a current authoritative source. The label begins the check; it does not finish it.

**The LAiDIES read:** expect more useful disclosure in the situations the rules cover. Keep checking the claim, source and date separately. If you publish or deploy AI professionally, use the Commission’s current guidance and qualified advice for your exact role and system.
