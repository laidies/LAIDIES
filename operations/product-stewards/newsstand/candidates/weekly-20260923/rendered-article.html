This week’s AI news: what changed, and what is still a proposal

California ordered a study and enacted reporting duties; Congress advanced a proposal; investigators reported a card-skimming campaign; Claude and OpenAI released product changes. The verb tells you what changed — and what did not.

  • California accelerated AI-oversight planning, but did not install a universal kill switch.
  • Two California data-centre laws create assessment and reporting duties; neither guarantees a lower household bill.
  • A House ratepayer bill remains a proposal, while a card-skimmer investigation does not establish that any particular shopper was exposed.
  • Claude merged work modes, and GPT-6 Sol and Luna reached specified plans and products with clear access limits.

Last week’s Weekly asked how to tell a promise from proof. This week needs a sharper tool: read the verb.

An agency can study a safeguard. A legislature can enact a reporting duty. One chamber can pass a proposal. Investigators can report a campaign. A company can release a product. All are real developments, but they do not create the same protection, evidence or access.

California: study the switch, start the reporting

California’s September 18 executive order took effect immediately. It accelerated existing oversight work and set November 16 for recommendations on possible legal amendments, including independently checked shutoffs for advanced AI systems, onsite verifiers and broader incident reporting. It did not install a universal AI kill switch. The Electronic Frontier Foundation supports broader reporting and third-party investigations while warning that shutoff effectiveness remains active research and government control could threaten protected speech.

On September 21, California also made SB 1168 and AB 1577 law. They are two of seven data-centre bills signed that day; this Weekly does not claim to explain the other five. SB 1168 tells the utilities commission to assess opportunities for rate structures — the rules used to divide utility charges among customer groups — including data centres’ share of grid and procurement costs. An assessment is not an adopted tariff or a calculated household saving.

AB 1577 creates local and state reporting for covered data centres with at least 10 megawatts of electrical capacity. Its public releases must be anonymized and aggregated — combined so a public release does not expose one protected customer record — and its first specified statewide load-trend assessment belongs in the 2029 energy-policy report. Named public, research, safety, national-security, publicly owned and utility facilities are excluded from the cited reporting provisions.

Washington: a House vote is still a proposal

The US House passed H.R. 9340 by 417–3 on September 16. If enacted, it would require state regulators and nonregulated utilities to consider a standard for qualifying computing sites with at least 100 megawatts of peak demand. Under that standard, a large customer’s rate would recover the additional grid-upgrade cost needed to serve it.

The bill would require consideration and a determination; it would not itself set one nationwide retail rate. A September 17 unanimous-consent attempt in the Senate drew an objection. The current official sources in this packet do not establish a Senate vote or enactment. Your bill still depends on the relevant utility, regulator, project and approved rate design.

Card skimmers: an investigation, not your exposure notice

BleepingComputer reported on September 23 that security firm Gambit had investigated AI-assisted agents used in a retail card-skimming campaign. Gambit calls its underlying publication an interim report and separates recovered material and observed checks from estimates about scale and cost.

The consumer consequence is serious: card skimmers steal payment details from online stores. The evidence supplied here does not identify a complete victim list, establish that a particular reader’s card was taken or support a made-up instruction to cancel every card. Quantities and claims about the AI role remain attributed to the investigators until affected parties or independent evidence narrow the scope.

Product changes you may actually encounter

Anthropic said on September 16 that Claude Cowork and chat are merging into one Claude. The practical change is that longer work can begin in an ordinary conversation rather than a separately chosen mode. The rollout starts with Pro and Max over several weeks; Team and Free are to follow, while Enterprise administrators get advance notice. Claude Docs and Slides are beta features on paid plans.

OpenAI also announced GPT-6 Sol and Luna in ChatGPT Work and Codex for Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Edu users. Free and Go users can access Luna in the desktop app. The models were not yet available in Chat, and the company said rollout through the day could delay visibility. OpenAI says its performance and reliability comparisons come from its own evaluations, not independent proof that everyone should switch. For ordinary work, access and the task matter before a benchmark table does.

The LAiDIES Read

The week is not one story about AI speeding up or government catching up. It is a set of different decision chains. The California order leads to recommendations. The state laws lead to proceedings, reports and later public data. The House bill still needs enactment and local rate decisions. The security report needs corroboration or affected-party notices. Product releases still depend on plan, app and rollout.

When a headline sounds finished, replace its loudest noun with the operative verb. “Kill switch” becomes “recommend.” “Ratepayer protection” becomes “consider.” “AI card theft” becomes “investigators report.” That small edit often reveals the next evidence you actually need.

What This Means for You

For an electricity claim, look for the commission proceeding, local permit or approved rate. For a security claim, look for an affected retailer, issuer notice or updated investigation before assuming personal exposure. For a product announcement, check your plan and the surface named in the source: Work, Codex, Chat, desktop or another app are not interchangeable.

No new routine is compulsory because six developments happened. The useful habit is narrower: ask what changed now, who is covered and what still requires a later decision.
