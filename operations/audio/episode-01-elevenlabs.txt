It's 4:52 on a Tuesday afternoon, and a man named Steve is being called a visionary.

Steve works in revenue operations. Steve has never once refilled the printer. And Steve has just presented a competitive analysis — clean, confident, suspiciously well-formatted — that everyone in that room knows took him about an hour, because at lunch he was playing pickleball. I know because he told us. Twice.

And me? I had a version of that same analysis. Mine took two weekends. Mine had footnotes. Mine was still sitting in my drafts folder, waiting until it felt... ready.

Somewhere between Steve's standing ovation and my fourth coffee, it hit me. He isn't smarter than me. He isn't better informed. He just stopped doing it the hard way. And I couldn't help but wonder... when did everyone learn to do that? And when, exactly, was I supposed to?

So. Let me tell you what I did after the Steve meeting. Nothing. For eight months. Well — not nothing. I did what every competent woman does with a problem she doesn't have time for: I put it on a list.

And in my defense, every time I tried to start, the on-ramp was terrible. Everything I found was either written by men in fleece vests — say no more — or so surface-level it basically amounted to "AI is transformative!" AI is transformative? Groundbreaking. 

Here's the thing. I have a full-time job. I manage a team. My calendar is a Tetris game I am perpetually losing. So the idea of adding "become AI-literate" to that pile felt about as realistic as Miranda Priestly asking me to fetch the unpublished Harry Potter manuscript. Technically possible — but at what personal cost?

Then one night — eleven p.m., glass of wine, everyone else asleep — I went looking for a different answer. Not "how do I learn AI." A different question entirely: was it just me? It was not just me.

I found a Harvard study. A hundred and forty thousand people, around the world. For every one hundred men using generative AI tools... seventy-eight women. And that gap holds even when you control for job type, age, education, and access. Same tools. Same desks. Different adoption rates.

And the deeper I read, the worse it got. Men are twenty-three percent more likely to be encouraged by their managers to use AI. Twenty-seven percent more likely to be praised when they do. Steve got called a visionary, remember. And women? We're thirty-two percent more likely to worry that using AI looks like cutting corners.

That one hit me like Samantha Jones delivering an uncomfortable truth over brunch. Because of course we worry about that. We've spent entire careers building credibility through preparation, thoroughness, visible effort. And now there's a tool that makes hard things look easy — and some part of our brain calculates, correctly, based on historical data, that "easy" might get held against us. That's not imposter syndrome. That's pattern recognition. Which is ironic, given what AI actually is — but we'll get there.

You know how there's always one person who preps the deck before the meeting, remembers the feedback from last time, follows up on the action items nobody else tracked — and still delivers her own work on time? That person is usually not named Steve. No offense to the Steves out there fighting the good fight. So no — this was never a confidence problem. It's a physics problem. You cannot add hours to a day that's already over-subscribed. The tool that could give you your time back requires time you don't have — to learn. So you don't start. And the gap compounds, week over week, until the distance between you and the colleague who started six months ago isn't a gap anymore. It's a canyon.

One more thing I found that night, from Fei-Fei Li — the Stanford professor they call the Godmother of AI. She said it plainly:

If we don't get women involved in AI, we're going to have a future built by half the population — for all of the population.

And it's not just the principle of the thing. When women don't use these tools, the tools learn from a skewed pool of users — they literally get worse for women. And around the cycle goes. So this was never just about my career, or yours. As Dolly would say...

You'd better get to building your own bridge, honey — because ain't nobody building it for you.

So here's what finally got me to start. It wasn't inspiration. It was spite. Fine — it was mostly spite, plus a Sunday morning, fifteen quiet minutes, and nothing to lose.

There was an email I'd been avoiding for four days. You know the kind — the delicate one, to the stakeholder who reads tone into line breaks. I opened one of the AI tools, and instead of asking it something grand, I just told it the truth: who the email was for, what I needed from them, and what I couldn't say out loud. It gave me a draft in nine seconds. And it was... eighty percent right. The other twenty percent was wrong in ways only I could see — which, it turns out, is the good news. I fixed it with my own judgment. I hit send. And then I looked at the clock. Four days of dread. Eleven minutes of work. And that's when it landed: oh. I can do this. Because the thing stopping me was never ability. It was that nobody had explained it in a way that made me want to start.

Now — I want to be clear. I am no AI slayer. There's no Watcher guiding me through some prophecy, no training montage that happened off-screen. I'm still learning, still getting things wrong, still googling things mid-conversation. But here's my favourite stat I've found so far, and it's the one that keeps me going. BCG looked at senior women who push through that first awkward phase. They don't just catch up to the men. They lead them — by fourteen percentage points. Fourteen. Because the women who get past "when would I even do this" bring the one thing AI cannot replicate: fifteen years of professional judgment. The instinct that something is off in a document before you can even articulate why. That twenty percent of my email only I could see? That's the job now.

Which brings me to my favourite part of every episode. We call it the cocktail party explanation: the answer you'll give the next time somebody at a dinner table says "AI" like they know what they're talking about. This week's question is the big one — what is this thing, actually? Here's the version I wish somebody had handed me at the start.

Imagine someone who has read an almost unimaginable amount of human writing. Books, articles, websites, forums, manuals — billions of documents. But this someone has never lived a single day of real life. No job. No relationships. No consequences. Not one awkward moment at a holiday party that taught them something about human nature. They can sound incredibly knowledgeable, because they've absorbed how language works, how ideas connect, how arguments are built. But they don't understand any of it the way you do — through experience, through getting things wrong, through building judgment one decision at a time, over a career.

So when you type something into ChatGPT, or Claude, or Gemini — you're not talking to an intelligence. You're prompting a very sophisticated pattern-completion engine, predicting, word by word, what should come next. It's autocomplete at a scale that feels like thought. But isn't.

Which is why it can be brilliant and useless in the same breath. It's Cher's closet computer from Clueless: endless outfit combinations, all day long — and absolutely no idea that you can't wear white after Labour Day to a conservative client meeting where you're already fighting to be taken seriously. That contextual judgment is yours. AI will never have it. And it can be confidently, spectacularly wrong — because it doesn't check whether what it says is true. Only whether it's plausible. It's the Burn Book from Mean Girls: some entries accurate, some completely fabricated, all delivered with the same unbothered confidence. "Made out with a hot dog"? Same certainty as an actual fact — it has no way of telling the two apart. Regina George energy. But make it software.

Three words before you go, so you don't pull a Cher — confidently arguing it doesn't say R.S.V.P. on the Statue of Liberty. Generative AI: the kind that makes new things — text, images, first drafts. A Carrie Bradshaw in your laptop — not one that finds you articles to read, that's Google — one that writes the column for you. Model: the trained brain behind the app. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are the magazines; the models are their editors-in-chief — different taste, different judgment, different style. Same brief, wildly different results. And hallucination: when the answer is polished, confident... and completely made up. It's not lying — lying requires intent. It just has no built-in "wait, do we have receipts for this?" mechanism. Think of your most confident friend, who will answer absolutely any question with total authority, whether or not she actually knows.

I'm not a regular mom — I'm a cool mom.

That's AI trying to be helpful and completely misreading the room. Every single time. And your job, in all of this, is knowing which parts to trust and which to push back on. Which — good news — is exactly what you've been doing with other people's work your entire career.
