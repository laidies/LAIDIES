# Comic / Graphic-Novel Visual Storytelling — Team Playbook

A practical reference for a weekly illustrated series that teaches a concept through a recurring heroine in a small town, with occasional historical-figure flashbacks. Every technique below is grounded in established comic theory (Scott McCloud, Will Eisner) or standard film/comic practice. Where a claim is our own synthesis or is contested, it says so.

**Running example convention:** "the heroine" = your weekly lead; "the pioneer" = a historical figure shown in flashback (e.g. an early-computing pioneer). Examples are light-touch — don't treat them as canon episode facts.

---

## 1. Panel-to-Panel Transitions (McCloud's six)

From Scott McCloud, *Understanding Comics* (1993). Comics work through **closure** — "observing the parts, but perceiving the whole" — where the reader mentally fills the **gutter** (the gap between panels) to supply the time and motion the drawings only imply. Well-established; these six categories are McCloud's, widely taught.

| Transition | What it does | Use it when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Moment-to-moment** | Tiny increment of a single action; almost no time passes. Minimal reader work. | You want to slow time down and dwell — a held beat, a dawning realization on the heroine's face. |
| **Action-to-action** | A single subject advances through one distinct movement. The workhorse of comics (McCloud notes it's the most common). | Straightforward "and then she did X" beats — the heroine picks up a tool, then uses it. |
| **Subject-to-subject** | Stays within one scene/idea but jumps between subjects; asks more of the reader. | Cutting between the heroine and what she's reacting to (her → the screen → her friend). |
| **Scene-to-scene** | Jumps across significant time and/or space; high closure demand. | Moving from town to the pioneer's era, or "later that day." Often paired with a caption box. |
| **Aspect-to-aspect** | Time barely moves; wanders across different aspects of one place/mood (details, textures). | Setting a mood or establishing a location — quiet beats of the town, the workshop, the machine. |
| **Non-sequitur** | No logical relationship between panels. | Rarely — deliberate absurdity or a jarring joke. Use with intent; it can read as an error. |

*Note:* some summaries online list a "movement-to-movement" transition — that is **not** one of McCloud's six and appears to be a paraphrase error. The canonical set is the six above.

## 2. "Camera": Shot Types & Angles

Comics have no camera, but the **framing** choice does the camera's job. Eisner (*Comics and Sequential Art*, 1985) treats framing and "camera angle" as the language of comics much as film theory treats shot selection. Shot-distance and angle vocabulary below is borrowed from standard film practice — well-established and cross-medium.

**Shot distance (how close we are):**
- **Establishing / wide** — where we are, who's present. Use to open a scene or after a location jump.
- **Full shot** — whole figure head-to-toe; body language and action.
- **Medium shot** — waist-up; the default conversation shot, balances face and gesture.
- **Close-up** — face fills the frame; emotion, a key line, a decision.
- **Extreme close-up** — eyes, a hand, one detail; maximum intensity or a significant object.
- **Splash** — one image filling the page (see §5); a big beat, not a "distance" per se.

**Camera angle (where we look from) and what it typically conveys:**
- **Eye-level** — neutral, equal footing. The everyday default.
- **High angle / bird's-eye (looking down)** — makes the subject look small, vulnerable, or overwhelmed; or gives a map-like overview.
- **Low angle / worm's-eye (looking up)** — makes the subject look powerful, heroic, or intimidating.
- **Dutch / canted (tilted horizon)** — unease, disorientation, tension, things "off."
- **Over-the-shoulder** — anchors a conversation; puts us inside the exchange, hints at whose side we're on.
- **POV (first-person)** — we see through a character's eyes; immersion, or hiding who's looking.

**Use it when…** the *feeling* should carry the beat: shrink the heroine with a high angle when she's daunted by a huge machine; low-angle the pioneer at her moment of triumph to make her monumental.

## 3. Simulating Camera *Movement* Across Static Panels

Comics can't literally zoom or pan, but a sequence of stills implies the move the way a movie would. Standard practice; the specific mappings below are widely used craft advice (our framing of it is synthesis).

- **Push-in (zoom in):** successive panels of the same subject, each closer than the last → we "move toward" it. Great for building to a realization. *(Some artists add inward-pointing corner arrows as a notation — that's a niche convention, not universal.)*
- **Pull-out (zoom out):** the reverse — widen across panels to reveal context or isolate a figure in a big space. Usually needs at least two panels.
- **Pan:** one wide/long panel that the eye travels across left-to-right does the work of a pan; the reader *is* the moving camera.
- **Tracking shot:** the subject stays framed the same size while the background changes behind them across panels → camera "travels with" them (distinct from push/pull, where distance changes).
- **Whip / flash cut:** an abrupt hard cut between very different panels (often scene-to-scene) for speed or shock.
- **Montage:** several small panels compressing a process or passage of time into one glance.

**Use it when…** you want cinematic energy: push in over three shrinking frames as the heroine realizes the answer; pull out to a wide to show how small the problem was all along.

## 4. Depicting Motion & Time *Within* a Panel

- **Speed / motion lines** (a.k.a. action lines, zip ribbons): abstract lines trailing a moving object, parallel to its direction. Well-established; manga uses them heavily, sometimes breaking the panel border for extra speed. Use for fast movement and impact.
- **Motion blur:** smeared/streaked rendering of the fast-moving part; reads as high speed.
- **Multiple / overlapping images:** the same figure drawn several times in one panel (a limb repeated, a figure echoed across the frame) to show a fast or continuous action. (Academic term: "polymorphic" imagery — optional jargon.)
- **The gutter + closure controls time:** a wide gutter or a scene-to-scene jump = more time passed; a thin gutter between near-identical panels = a held instant. (McCloud — well-established.)
- **Panel width/shape affects perceived duration:** a wide panel tends to read as a longer, lingering beat; a thin panel reads as a quick one. This is common craft guidance and broadly accepted, though not a hard rule.
- **Decompression vs. compression (pacing):** *decompression* = spreading one moment across many panels to slow down and dwell; *compression* = packing a lot of story into few panels to move fast. Choose per beat.

**Use it when…** teaching a step-by-step idea: decompress the key "aha" (many small panels), compress the routine setup (few panels), and add speed lines only where something literally moves fast.

## 5. Page & Sequence Composition / Reading Flow

- **Z-path reading order:** Western readers scan left-to-right across a tier, then down — a "Z." A tall panel or an odd layout can break this; empirical work (Cohn et al.) found readers follow the Z-path ~95% of the time on a standard grid but far less when layout fights it. **Takeaway:** on a regular grid, order is safe; if you break the grid, make the intended path unmistakable.
- **Panel size = emphasis:** the biggest panel on the page is read as the most important beat. A run of small panels building to one large panel is a classic buildup-and-pause. Well-established craft.
- **Splash page = a big beat:** one full-page image for a reveal, an arrival, or an impact. Expensive in space — use sparingly.
- **Bleed / borderless panels:** art running off the page edge (bleed) or with no border feels open-ended, timeless, or immersive; removing borders can also make two panels blur together as near-simultaneous. Use for scale, mood, or dreamlike beats.
- **Inset panel:** a small bordered panel sitting on top of a larger one — for a detail, a reaction, or simultaneous action without spending a full panel slot.

**Use it when…** you want the eye guided: keep teaching sequences on a clean grid so nobody gets lost, then spend a splash or bleed on the single emotional/"wow" moment of the episode.

## 6. Flashback / Time-Jump Conventions

These are widely used reader-recognized signals (documented across comics; see TV Tropes "Flashback Effects" and craft guides). No single one is a "rule" — pick a consistent house style and stick to it.

- **Color/tone shift:** sepia or desaturated wash = the past or a memory; full/brighter color = the present. Very common and instantly readable.
- **Border treatment:** wavy, scalloped, or rounded borders signal memory/flashback; borderless or soft-edged panels can read as dream. Deteriorated/rougher borders can imply the *distant* past vs. clean borders for recent past.
- **Caption boxes:** a captioned "Years earlier…" or a distinct nar: caption color anchors the jump cleanly (often the simplest, clearest tool).
- **Present ↔ past transitions:** cue the switch with a scene-to-scene transition *plus* at least one signal above (tone or border), then reverse the signals to return. Consistency is what makes it legible.

**Use it when…** cutting from the heroine's present-day town to the pioneer's era: desaturate/sepia the flashback + a "Years earlier…" caption on the way in; snap back to full color to return. Our recommended house style: **color is the primary cue, caption is the backup** — cheapest to produce, hardest to misread.

## 7. Emotional / Tonal Tools

- **Color & lighting for mood:** dark/cool/low-key = tension, past, danger; bright/warm/high-key = safety, present, hope. Contrasting a dark past against a bright present is a standard, readable device.
- **Sound-effect lettering (onomatopoeia):** big, styled SFX ("CLICK," "WHIRR") add energy and sound; size/weight/color imply loudness and texture.
- **Balloon type carries a different voice** (standard comics grammar):
  - **Speech balloon** — spoken aloud; tail points to the speaker.
  - **Thought balloon** — interior thought; traditionally cloud-shaped with bubble tail.
  - **Caption box** — narration, time/place, or an off-panel voice ("Meanwhile…," "Years earlier…").
  - Balloon *shape* signals delivery: jagged/spiky = shouting or electronic; wobbly = weak/scared; icy or colored = a distinct voice.

**Use it when…** you want tone without exposition: warm palette for the heroine's town, cooler/sepia for the pioneer's workshop; a jagged balloon when the machine "speaks," a caption box to timestamp every jump.

## CHEAT SHEET — Emotion / Goal → Reach for…

- **Show someone small / powerless** → high angle looking down on them.
- **Show someone powerful / heroic** → low angle looking up at them.
- **Create unease / "something's wrong"** → Dutch (canted) angle.
- **Land emotion or a key line** → close-up / extreme close-up.
- **Set the scene / say where we are** → establishing wide shot.
- **Slow time down, dwell on a beat** → moment-to-moment + decompression (many small panels); wide panel.
- **Speed up / cover ground fast** → compression (few panels), action-to-action, montage.
- **Build to a realization** → push-in (successive closer panels).
- **Reveal scale or isolate a figure** → pull-out to wide, or a bleed panel.
- **Make THE big moment** → splash page (use sparingly).
- **Show fast motion in one image** → speed lines + motion blur (+ multiple images for continuous action).
- **Signal a flashback / past** → sepia or desaturated wash + wavy/scalloped border + "Years earlier…" caption.
- **Return to the present** → snap back to full color, clean borders.
- **Guide the eye reliably** → clean left-to-right Z-path grid; save layout-breaking for intentional emphasis.
- **Emphasize importance** → make that panel the biggest on the page.
- **Distinguish thought / narration from speech** → thought balloon vs. caption box vs. speech balloon.
- **Convey a non-human or shouted voice** → jagged / colored balloon + styled SFX lettering.
