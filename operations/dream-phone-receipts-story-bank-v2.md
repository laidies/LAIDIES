# Dream Phone · "Is That Even True?" — story bank v2 (locked assertion-packet model)

Model + design rules live in `dream-phone-receipts-story-packet-example.md` (Sky Dancers = round 0, the template). These are 5 more, built on it.
**Balance:** 3 For Real (resist reflex-skepticism), 3 As If incl. Sky Dancers. Every real fact verified + sourced; every made-up uses *scatter*, every inflated uses *missing denominator*.

## 1 · Furby & the NSA — **FOR REAL** (wild-but-true; the trap is wanting to say "As If")

**☎️ AI:** *"The NSA actually **[banned Furbies from its headquarters]** — staff were **[scared the toys were recording classified conversations]** and would repeat them later."*

- **A — "NSA banned Furbies from HQ"** → **TRUE** (Fort Meade, early 1999, the internal "Furby Alert").
- **B — "because they feared the toys were recording"** → **TRUE** (that was the stated fear).
- **Overall: FOR REAL.**

**Why it holds (convergence, not scatter):** the credible confirmers all land on the *same specifics.* 📚 Jeeves → the Washington Post write-up: *"Fort Meade, early '99, banned over recording fears."* ☕ Barista → *"my cousin's dad was on a base — there really was a memo."* 🙄 Skeptic → *"sounds fake… but it's genuinely documented. Ugh, fine."* Everyone who actually knows agrees. (A made-up one would fall apart on the details; this one doesn't.)

**Reveal:** **For Real.** The NSA really did it — Fort Meade, 1999. *(They were wrong that Furbies could record; Hasbro proved the toys just ran ~200 words on a timer. But the ban absolutely happened.)* **Lesson: don't call the absurd fake on reflex — check first. Truth's often weirder than the lie.**
**Sources:** Snopes (https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/nasa-furby-ban/), Mental Floss (https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/55136/did-pentagon-really-ban-furbys). Verified 2026-07-14.

One says Toonami, one says 4Kids, one says a bootleg — nobody who *actually watched it* would fumble that, because **it never aired outside Japan.** Made up. **Lesson: a pile of confident "I saw it" that can't agree on the *where* is nobody seeing it.**

## 3 · The Beanie Baby divorce — **AS IF** (real core + an **inflated** number → missing denominator)

## 8 · The "Good Times" virus — **AS IF** (**made-up** → the effects scatter)

**☎️ AI:** *"Back in '94 there was a computer virus called **[Good Times]** that would **[wipe your whole hard drive if you so much as opened the email]**."*

- **A — "a virus called Good Times"** → **MADE UP** (no such virus ever existed — it was a chain-email *hoax*).
- **B — "it wiped your drive if you opened the email"** → **MADE UP** (the hoax's imaginary payload).
- **Overall: AS IF.**

**How it's caught (scatter — nobody can agree what it *did*, because it never existed):**
- 📧 Deb → chain email: *"GOOD TIMES DELETES YOUR HARD DRIVE — forward to everyone!!"*
- ☕ Barista → *"I heard it **fried your processor**, some infinite loop?"*
- 🗣️ Office Gossip → *"no, it **emailed itself** to your whole address book"*
- 📻 DJ SunnyV → *"a caller said it just **erased your files**"*
- 📚 Jeeves → *"the Dept. of Energy put out an official notice in Dec '94: **there is no such virus.** The only thing spreading was the warning."*

Four "victims," four totally different symptoms → nobody had it → **made up.**

**Reveal:** **As If.** There *was* no Good Times virus. It was a **hoax** — a chain email warning about a virus that never existed, and the warning was the only thing that ever spread. The government officially debunked it in '94. Notice nobody could agree what it even *did*? A real virus has real symptoms; a fake one has as many as there are people forwarding the panic. **The chain-email panic *was* the whole "virus."** **Lesson: this is *literally* the thing this game is about.**

## 12 · The Pentium chip bug — **AS IF** (**inflated** → a real recall, an overblown scope)

**☎️ AI:** *"That Pentium chip bug was so bad **[every Pentium computer was getting basic math wrong]**, and **[Intel had to recall them all]**."*

- **A — "Intel recalled the chips"** → **TRUE** ($475 million recall, Dec 1994 — the first full recall of a computer chip).
- **B — "every Pentium was getting basic math wrong"** → **INFLATED** (only certain high-precision divisions; ~1 in 9 billion random divisions — a normal user would essentially never hit it).
- **Overall: AS IF.**

**How B is caught (the *frequency* is missing — panic says "everything's broken," nobody says how rare):**
- 📧 Deb → *"my computer was LYING, nothing added up!!"* · 🗣️ Office Gossip → *"everyone's Pentium couldn't do math"*
- 📚 Jeeves → *"the flaw only showed in specific high-precision divisions — odds on a random calc were about **1 in 9 billion.** A regular person would never notice. A math professor doing prime-number research is the one who caught it."*

"Every computer, basic math" collapses the moment you ask *how often does it actually happen.* → **inflated.**

**Reveal:** **As If.** The bug and the $475M recall (first-ever for a chip) were completely real. But "every Pentium getting basic math wrong"? The flaw only surfaced in rare high-precision divisions — roughly **1 in 9 billion** random calculations. Normal users never hit it; it took a mathematician hunting primes to even find it. The recall was real; "your PC can't add" was the panic. **Lesson: "there was a recall" ≠ "everything was broken" — check how *often* it actually happened.**
**Source:** Wikipedia — Pentium FDIV bug (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentium_FDIV_bug). Verified 2026-07-14.
