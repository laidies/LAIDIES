# The Before & After beat — the format every class uses

**Ali, 2026-07-22:** *"i also want to show what bad looks like, and what good looks like. why what
is bad is bad. and why what you put in gets bad output by the tool. and how good changes that…
i want them to understand how it works and what it means, they will be better able to remember
and really get it rather than just memorizing things."*

This is a **format decision, not a topic.** It is not a class called "good prompts". Every class
gets this beat.

---

## Why this is the differentiator
Every generic guide hands out prompts to copy. Copying is memorising, and memorising dies the
moment the situation is slightly different. Showing the *cause* means she can work out the next
one herself.

It is also **the most durable material we make.** A menu path rots when a vendor renames it. The
reason vague input produces vague output does not rot — it is how the machinery works. Put the
effort here and it keeps paying.

---

## 🔴 THE ONE RULE: change ONE thing

**Same task. Same tool. One variable different. That is the entire discipline.**

This is where every other guide fails: they put a three-word prompt next to a two-hundred-word
prompt and call it a lesson. All she learns is "longer is better," which is not true and not
what happened. If bad→good changes five things at once, nothing causal has been taught.

If you cannot name the single variable in one short phrase, the demonstration is not ready.

---

## The four parts, in this order

1. **The bad take.** A real attempt on a real screen. It must be a *reasonable* thing a smart
   person would actually type — never a strawman, never deliberately stupid. If she would not
   have typed it, she learns nothing from watching it fail.
2. **Why it did that.** The mechanism, in one plain sentence. **This is the part she keeps.**
   Not "be more specific" — *why* being unspecific produces that particular kind of bad.
3. **The fix.** The one thing that changes. Named out loud, in a phrase.
4. **The good take.** Same task, same screen, one variable changed. The improvement has to be
   visible without narration — if you have to explain why the second one is better, it isn't.

---

## The mechanisms — the durable "why"s
Nearly all bad output comes from one of these. They are stable, non-vendor-specific, and they
are the actual content of the curriculum.

### 1. Nothing narrowed it, so you got the average
A model produces the most likely continuation of what is in front of it. Constraints change what
"likely" means. Give it almost nothing and the likeliest output is the most *average* one —
bland, hedged, could-be-anyone. **That flat corporate voice is not laziness. It is what "most
likely" looks like when nothing has narrowed the field.**

### 2. It will not stop and ask
Where something is missing, it fills the gap with the most plausible guess and carries on. It
does not know it guessed. This is why an audience, a tone, a format or a fact you never
specified turns up in the answer anyway — and why it can be confidently, fluently wrong.

### 3. It only sees what is in front of it
Everything it can use is on the board right now — this conversation, whatever it stored about
you, whatever files or access you gave it. Anything else does not exist to it. Most "it ignored
my instruction" is really "that instruction was not on the board any more."

### 4. Plausible is cheap
Producing something that *looks* right costs it exactly as much as producing something that *is*
right. A convincing-looking source is as easy to generate as a real one. This is why the check
has to be external — which is Episode 3's receipts, and Basics Period 9.

## What the variable is, class by class
The beat generalises because every class has one: **the same task, run with and without the
thing the class is about.**

| Class | The one variable |
|---|---|
| What It's Actually Doing | how much has been narrowed |
| Why It Doesn't Know About Last Month | whether it looked it up or answered from the edition |
| What a Session Is | whether the constraint is still on the board |
| What It Keeps | whether memory carries your context |
| Telling It Who You Are | whether the standing instruction is set |
| Giving It Your Stuff | whether the material is in the container |
| When It Does the Work | whether it has access to do it, or is only describing it |
| Bolting On Extra Powers | whether the capability is switched on |
| How to Tell When It's Wrong | whether you clicked the source |

## Runtime — Ali 2026-07-22
**Classes run 5–10 minutes.** Two minutes is too short for most of them.
**But never pad to hit a number.** If a topic is honestly covered in less, it ends there.

⚠ **Correction to earlier reasoning of mine.** I argued for two-minute classes on the grounds
that "eight two-minute classes survive a redesign; one sixteen-minute class does not." That
conflated *duration* with *scope*. The re-shoot economics come entirely from **one topic per
class** — a seven-minute single-topic class costs exactly one re-shoot when that topic's menu
changes, same as a two-minute one. Short means narrow, not brief.

## Filming notes
- **Show the bad output in full and let it sit.** The bad take is not a joke to rush past — she
  has to recognise it as something she has received, and probably sent.
- **Do not caption the picture.** The voiceover carries the *why*; the screen carries the what.
- **Never mock the reader.** The bad take is the sensible first attempt, not a mistake only an
  idiot would make. Ali's audience is smart and new, not careless.
- **Same account, same day, back to back.** If the two takes come from different sessions the
  comparison is not honest and someone will spot it.
- ⛔ Do not tidy the good output. If the good take is still a bit off, say so on camera. A
  demonstration that overclaims teaches her to distrust the next one.
