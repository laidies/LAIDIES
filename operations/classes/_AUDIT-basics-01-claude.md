# Audit — Basics Class 1 (intro) · independent Claude pass

## Overall verdict
Worth making, with changes — the spine is strong and her seven minutes would be well spent. The featured letter is genuinely taught (why she can't read it, exactly what comes back, and the honest "where the letter is silent, it says so"), the Grace Hopper bridge lands the core reframe, and it closes on a real Tuesday task. The single biggest reason it isn't ship-as-is: it assigns her to paste an insurance letter, a client pricing email, and two job offers and says **nothing** about where that material goes — the one omission most likely to stop a cautious professional from ever trying it.

## Findings (ranked most-severe first, across all dimensions)

**1. [usefulness + explanation, HIGH] The confidential-paste question is never answered — and the homework steers her into it.**
Quotes: "how it gets your stuff. There are three ways…" and the closing "The email you're dreading." A whole section explains the input mechanic but says nothing about what happens to her material once it's in, and then the homework actively points her at work-confidential material as today's task. For a reader who runs things, "and then where does that go?" is the instant reaction to pasting a client email — leaving it out of the section literally titled "how it gets your stuff" reads as a half-explained mechanic and creates real day-one exposure.
**Fix (two parts):** (a) Keep the work examples as capability illustrations but do NOT frame them as day-one homework — steer today's task to what's just hers. Replace the nudge with: *"Pick something real off your list — and keep today's to something that's just yours: the letter, the toast, a week of dinners. Anything your job would call confidential — a client's email, an internal report — hold on that for now. For today, one thing that's yours."* (b) Add one honest, light caveat rather than a data-handling how-to: *"One thing before you paste anything personal: whatever you hand it, you're handing to a company's computers. When it's a client email or a benefits letter, you can black out the names and account numbers first — it reads the shape of it just as well."* Do not point at a "where your words go" class unless one actually gets added to the roadmap; the current roadmap (screen tour, then phrasing) contains no such class.

**2. [usefulness, MEDIUM] Only one thing is actually shown working, so the watching-budget is thin.**
Quote: "And that same move works on a lot of what's already on your plate." The premise is that runtime past the ~7-min script is spent WATCHING it work — but there's one demo (the letter), and the file-upload input path is described yet never seen. Highest-value proof for this audience is watching one real high-stakes task end to end, not hearing six outputs recited too fast to hold.
**Fix:** Promote a SECOND example to a full on-screen demo that shows the upload path (file dragged in → one-screen briefing building). To avoid re-teaching the confidential-paste behavior Finding 1 warns against, make the demo document non-confidential-but-high-stakes — a dense contract or policy she's personally signing, a benefits handbook before open enrollment, a lease — and keep the internal 34-page report as a NAMED example only. The on-screen action carries it, so VO stays sparse; no other narration needs cutting. If runtime is tight, the wedding toast is the first cut from the named list.

**3. [sense, MEDIUM] "You just watched all three" contradicts the script's own next sentence.**
Quote: "There are three ways, and you just watched all three." The six examples used only paste/type and upload; the third way (connect your email) is described one breath later as something you do "later on" and that is "its own class too" — explicitly NOT shown. An attentive listener catches the false claim at the exact moment the mental model is being taught.
**Fix:** "There are three ways. You just watched two of them; the third comes a little later." The existing "And later on, you can connect things you already use, like your email…" line then agrees with it.

**4. [explanation/usefulness, LOW→MEDIUM] The five examples run as five identical nested lists back-to-back — dense in audio.**
Same paragraph as Finding 2; addressed largely by promoting a second demo. Additionally, break the identical "paste X, back comes Y" cadence: let one example run in full (keep the job-offer one whole so it carries the honesty beat — *"like the letter, back comes a plain comparison with 'not stated' wherever an offer goes quiet instead of a guess — the decision stays yours"*) and trim the rest to single clauses (dreaded email → two warm drafts that hold your line; report → a one-screen briefing with page numbers to check; a week of dinners → five thirty-minute meals and a grocery list by aisle; toast → a three-minute toast built from your details).

**5. [sense, LOW] Wording-doesn't-matter vs. wording-is-a-whole-class is never bridged.**
Quote: "And how you word the ask, so that what comes back is actually this good — that's its own class." Sits in tension with the earlier reassurances ("no syntax to memorize, no magic words"). It already pins wording to answer *quality*, so it doesn't land as a flat takeback — but the understood-vs-quality line is never drawn, and killing the fear of a "secret correct way" is a stated job of this opener.
**Fix:** "It always understands you in plain words — there's no wording you have to get right for it to work. How you ask does change how good the answer comes back, though, and that's its own class."

**6. [sense, LOW] The wedding-toast verb names the output as the input.**
Quote: "Type the wedding toast you keep putting off, and back comes a three-minute toast…" You don't have the toast to type — it's what you want back — and "landing right on the toast" reads circularly. The four prior examples train the ear on "[verb] the thing you already have," so this inverts the pattern.
**Fix:** "Give it the details for the wedding toast you keep putting off, and back comes a three-minute toast, built from what you told it, warm and a little funny, landing right where it should."

**7. [sense/explanation, LOW] Garden-path on the report's "moved figures" clause.**
Quote: "the figures the report itself says moved since last quarter." The reduced relative clause makes the ear parse "the report itself says" as a fresh statement before it snaps back to modifying "figures" — a snag on the one clause that quietly makes the reliability point (the tool is citing the report's numbers, not inventing movement).
**Fix:** insert the overt relativizer, keeping the report as named source: "the figures **that** the report says moved since last quarter." (Do not swap to "it says" — that drops the attribution the clause exists to carry.)

**8. [accuracy, LOW] "The reason programming isn't all code today" is a one-hero flattening.**
Grace Hopper's verified first is the plain-English lineage specifically (A-0, FLOW-MATIC→COBOL); the broader move off raw code had multiple independent parents (FORTRAN, ALGOL, LISP), none downstream of her. "The reason" overcredits against LAiDIES' own verify-every-stat bar.
**Fix:** "— a big reason you don't have to write everything in raw code today." Keeps her real claim to fame, drops the sole-cause "the."

**9. [brand, LOW] "Text a friend" is off-era for a world set permanently in 1999.**
Quote: "If you can text a friend, you already know how to work it." Routine texting as shorthand for an effortless everyday task is a mid-2000s reference; in 1999 people paged and left voicemails. It's the one visible seam in an otherwise meticulous 1999 voice. (The line is *not* condescending — that's ordinary reassurance — so this rests only on the era slip.)
**Fix:** "If you can jot a quick note in your own words, you already know how to work it." (Matches the typing capability the line introduces.)

## Most useful thing / what's missing
**Most useful takeaway:** the reframe-plus-proof that she can hand the tool the exact dreaded paperwork she can't parse and get back a plain read AND the right questions to ask, with judgment left to her — "the questions-to-ask are the part that earns its keep… leaves the judgment with you," and it flags where the letter is silent instead of bluffing. That's genuine capability-awareness, not filler.

**Missing — and where it belongs:**
- **Safety of pasting (belongs HERE, non-negotiable):** covered in Finding 1. The class tells her to paste an insurance letter, a client pricing email, and two job offers with zero word on where that goes, then makes "the email you're dreading" the homework. One honest caveat + steering day-one to personal material fixes it without turning the orientation into a data-handling tutorial.
- **Does trying it cost money? (belongs HERE, cheap):** she's told to pick a tool and do a real task today, but nothing tells her whether she'll hit a card wall. Add after "Pick one.": *"And they're all free to start — you won't need a card to do today's task."* Keep it to that durable half; do NOT claim the free tier covers upload/voice (that rots per tool).
- **Screen tour, phrasing, connecting email (belongs LATER, correctly deferred):** the script already defers these to named future classes. Good.

## Runtime & what to SHOW
~1000 words is honestly ~7 minutes of talking, and the design intent — script carries the mental model, screen carries the proof — is sound. But right now the "watching" budget is over-promised: only the letter is shown, so the back half of the runtime has little to actually watch. Show **two** examples working end to end, not one:
- **Show in full:** the letter (paste path — keep as-is) AND a second, non-confidential high-stakes document on the **upload** path (a contract/policy she's signing, a benefits handbook, a lease) so she SEES the file-drag mechanic that's currently only described.
- **Name only:** job offers, week of dinners, wedding toast — and keep the internal 34-page report as a named example, not the shown one (showing it would re-teach the confidential-paste habit Finding 1 warns against). Vary the cadence on the named run so it doesn't flatten into "it does a lot of stuff."

## Make it more LAiDIES
- **Fix the era slip (Finding 9):** "If you can jot a quick note in your own words, you already know how to work it." — the only visible non-1999 seam; on-era swap costs nothing.
- **Keep, don't touch:** the "different magazines on the same rack — same sections, a different voice" line nails the locked umbrella-analogy without forcing it; AI is correctly "it" throughout with clean verbs; "written to be filed, not to be read" is exactly the anti-condescension this audience needs. Do **not** bolt a town / FAiRY Godmother / building plug onto this class — none is warranted and it would read as a forced cross-sell in an orientation whose whole job is to make her unafraid.

The Grace Hopper passage is the strongest prose in the piece and earns its ~40 seconds — trim only the "the reason" overclaim (Finding 8), not the beat itself.
