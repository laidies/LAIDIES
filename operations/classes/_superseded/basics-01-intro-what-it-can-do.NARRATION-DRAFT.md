[warm] You know the letter. It comes in a windowed envelope, on paper a little thicker than it needs to be. Across the top, in bold, it says "This is not a bill." Underneath: a column of codes, a few dollar amounts, and a sentence that starts "in accordance with your plan." You read it once. You read it again. And you still can't answer the one question you opened it for — did they pay this, or didn't they. It isn't that you're missing something everyone else understands. That letter was written to be filed, not to be read.

There's a tool that can read it back to you, in plain words. It's a tool you talk to in plain English: you type what you want, it writes back. No code to learn, no special words, no wall of buttons to get past first. You already have the part that matters — you can describe the job, and you can tell whether what comes back is any use.

And you don't only have to type. On your phone or your computer you can talk to it out loud, in your actual voice, and it answers. Hands full in the kitchen, walking out to the car, or you just don't feel like typing it all out — you say what you want, plainly, and the answer comes back. For a lot of people that's the difference between meaning to try one of these and actually trying it.

[warm] And you don't have to phrase it the "right" way. There's no secret syntax, no magic words — think an AIM message, not a computer spell: you say the job in ordinary words. The details you give it do shape how good the answer is, and getting the hang of that is its own class.

If this feels like a room you arrived late to, it isn't. For most of computing history, telling a machine to do anything meant writing in its language — raw code, numbers. Then, in the nineteen-fifties, a Navy mathematician named Grace Hopper built FLOW-MATIC: the first language you could write in something close to plain English instead. It became the basis for COBOL, which ran business machines for decades. She pushed computers toward the way people actually talk. These tools just take it further — now you ask in your own words.

You've probably heard the names by now: Chat G-P-T, Claude, Gemini. They're broadly the same kind of thing, and they mostly do the same jobs. Once you can use one, the others feel familiar — like different magazines on the same rack. Same sections, a different voice. There are real differences, but none that matter on your first day. So pick one. They're all free to start — you won't need a card to do today's task.

Now — back to that letter. You paste it in — black out your name and policy number first; it reads the shape of it just as well — codes, jargon and all, and you ask, in plain words, what it actually says.

[measured] What comes back is a plain read. What the letter is telling you. Whether they paid it, denied it, or billed you — and for how much. Any deadline, quoted the way the letter put it. A short list of questions to ask when you call them. And where the letter is silent, it says so — it flags what the letter doesn't tell you, instead of handing you something that only sounds right.

[thoughtful] It won't turn you into a claims expert, and it can still read a line wrong. That's why the questions-to-ask are the part that earns its keep — it gets you oriented enough to make the call, and leaves the judgment with you.

And carry that into all of it, not just this letter. It builds its answer from patterns in language — it isn't checking whether what it says is true. That's exactly what lets it make a baffling page clear, and it's why you check the amounts, the dates, the details against what you gave it. A sharp second read, never the last word.

And that same second read works across what's already on your plate. Take the email you've been dreading — paste it in with your terms, that hundred-and-fifty-a-round line you need to hold, and back come two warm drafts that hold it and still offer a call. Upload a thirty-four-page report and get a one-screen briefing: the three things that matter, the figures that the report says moved since last quarter, a page number to check each against. Paste two job offers side by side and, like the letter, back comes a plain comparison — "not stated" wherever an offer goes quiet, and the trade-offs laid out, the decision still yours. Give it your week, your allergy and what you actually like, and out come five thirty-minute dinners with a grocery list by aisle. Give it the names and a couple of true stories, and the wedding toast you keep putting off comes back in three minutes, landing where it should.

All three — Chat G-P-T, Claude, Gemini — can help with every one of these; the features and the limits just vary a little.

Notice that your own material was in every one of those, because that's the next piece: how you get it in. You've just seen two ways. You type or paste it straight in — the letter, the toast, the week of dinners. Or you upload a file — a Word document, a PDF, a spreadsheet — the way you'd hand over that thirty-four-page report. There's a third, for later: you can connect something you already use — your email, say — and grant it access, so you're not pasting every time. That one gets its own class, and its own privacy walkthrough.

[warm] So — here's your first assignment at SUNNYVAiLE High. Pick one real thing off your list — something that's just yours, not work-confidential: the letter you can't parse, a trip to plan, a toast to write. Black out any names or account numbers, hand it to one tool, and read what comes back. (What's safe to share from work, and how, is its own class.) A real, low-stakes task will show you far more than a test ever will.
