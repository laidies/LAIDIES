# PART 1 — THE CONCEPT

## Where this sits
**The first class anyone takes.** She has signed up, opened it, and is looking at a blank box
with a cursor blinking in it. Nothing on that screen tells her what it is or what to do.

Everything else at SUNNYVAiLE High assumes this class. Nothing here assumes anything.

## The one idea
**It is two things, not one — the tool you click and the model that writes — and the model works
by producing the most likely next words for whatever is in front of it.**

That single sentence explains almost everything that will confuse her later: why a vague
question gets a bland answer, why memory is a setting rather than the machine getting to know
her, why a new version does not wipe her preferences.

## Why she should care
Because she has been handed an interface with no map, and the shape of the thing is genuinely
not obvious. People who "get" these tools are not smarter — they know what the parts are. That is
learnable in eight minutes and then it never has to be learned again.

## What she will be able to do afterwards
1. Name every part of the screen she was handed.
2. Tell the tool from the model, and say what each one does.
3. Describe, in one sentence, what happens when she presses enter.
4. Have typed one real thing and got something usable back.

## The one mistake this class exists to prevent
**Opening with "what can you do?" and judging the tool on the answer.** It is the natural first
move and it produces a brochure — a generic capability list that answers nothing she came for, and
makes the tool feel like a toy. The fix is not a cleverer question. It is giving it one real, small job.

## The analogy
⚠ **Needs Ali's Mall ruling before scripting is final.** Proposed: describing the model's output
as **the average answer** — ask a hundred people to describe a chair and you get four legs,
wooden, nothing memorable. Not one of them is wrong; the average is just unmemorable.

This is the only new analogy in the class. The two-parts idea is taught plainly, with no
metaphor, because it is the thing she must hold precisely.

## Deliberately NOT in this class
- **How to write a good prompt.** That is Episode 2. This class shows one contrast, in one beat,
  to prove the mechanism — then stops.
- **Any settings walkthrough.** She is pointed at the door; the rooms are the later classes.
- **Model names, versions, cutoff dates.** They rot, and she does not need them today.
- **Which tool is best.** Not this class, and arguably not ours.

## Structure, and why — evidence-based
| Chapter | Runs | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · What am I looking at | 0:00–3:06 | The screen tour first, because she cannot follow anything else until the parts have names. |
| 2 · What is actually happening | 3:06–6:11 | The two nouns, defined before they are named, then the mechanism — plainly, once. |
| 3 · So what do I type | 6:11–8:51 | She does one real thing and gets something usable. Ends with the recap and one instruction. |
Chapters because engagement drops sharply past ~6 minutes *(Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014, ACM L@S,
6.9M viewing sessions)*. Task previewed in plain language before any screen appears *(Van der
Meij 2013/2015)*. Steps recapped aloud at the end — the most corroborated finding in the
teaching research.

# PART 2 — THE NARRATION SCRIPT

## CHAPTER ONE — What am I looking at · 0:00–3:06

**0:00–0:10** · Lesson slide, held. No voiceover.
The overhead transparency: *Class One · What You're Looking At.* Under TODAY YOU WILL — name
every part of the screen · tell the tool from the model · type one real thing and get something
back.

**0:10–0:34**
> So you signed up, and it opened, and it is a box. That is it. A box with a cursor in it and no
> instructions anywhere. If your first thought was "and now what" — that is not you missing
> something. There genuinely is nothing there telling you.

*On screen: chatgpt.com as it opens in a browser. Blank. Let it sit before speaking.*
⚠ WEB layout to be confirmed on chatgpt.com before capture — the screenshots I have are the Mac
desktop app, which differs.

**0:34–1:14**
> Let us name the furniture, because it is hard to use a room when nothing in it has a name.
> Down here in the middle is where you type. Over on the left, at the top, is a button that says
> New chat — that starts a fresh one. Under it, your past conversations stack up under Chats, and
> you can open any of them again. And your own name, at the very bottom of that left strip, is the
> way into your settings, where nearly everything else in this term lives.

*On screen: each highlighted as named, held — the composer ("Ask ChatGPT"), the "New chat" button
(top-left), the Chats history below it, the account name (bottom-left) that opens Settings.
Confirmed on chatgpt.com logged in, 2026-07-22.*

**1:14–1:58**
> Four things are the spine. Type here, your history on the left, one control for a new chat,
> your name for the settings. There is more on that left side — a search, saved projects, a
> couple of things we will meet later — but those four are the ones you need on day one.
>
> And here is the useful part. On your phone these same four things exist, just tucked in
> slightly different places — the settings behind a little gear, and so on. So learn them as
> four things, not four positions, and you will find them on anything.

*On screen: pull back to the whole window with the four parts labelled together. A single still
of the phone layout may flash under the last line to make the point — optional, not a tour.*

**1:58–2:30**
> One last thing to notice up top, so it does not throw you later. There is a little switch that
> says Chat on one side and Work on the other. For everything in this term, you stay on Chat.
> Work is a different, more advanced mode we are not touching yet. If you ever land there by
> accident, that switch is how you come back.

*On screen: cursor rests on the Chat/Work toggle at the top; Chat stays selected.
Confirmed on chatgpt.com logged in, 2026-07-22.*

**2:30–2:48**
> Now the thing that surprised me. There is more behind that settings door than you would
> expect. Not just your name and your billing — sections for your memory, for connecting other
> things, for controls most people never open.

*On screen: the Settings screen, scrolled slowly — the Account section.* ⚠ show only the
cross-platform Account items; the desktop-only sections are out of scope for a web-taught class.

**2:48–3:06**
> You do not need any of it today. I am showing you so you know it is there. That is most of the
> difference between people who find these tools easy and people who do not. Not cleverness.
> Knowing there is a menu.

*On screen: close settings, return to the blank box.*

## CHAPTER TWO — What is actually happening · 3:06–6:11

**3:06–3:56**
> Before you type anything, there are two things here and they are not the same thing. This
> matters more than it sounds like it does.
>
> Everything you can click is one of them. The box, the list, the settings, your account, the
> files you put in. All of that is one thing.
>
> And somewhere inside it there is a part that does nothing at all except produce words, one
> after another. That is the other one.

*On screen: split. Left, the interface with everything clickable dimly highlighted. Right, only
streaming text. No labels yet.*

**3:56–4:34**
> The clickable part is the tool. The part that produces the words is called the model. You will
> meet that word constantly — a model picker, which model are you on, this model is better at that
> — and it is worth knowing exactly what it means. The tool is what you set up. The model is what
> writes. Keep those two apart and nearly everything else stops being confusing.

*On screen: NOW the labels appear — THE TOOL, then THE MODEL. Late, after each is described.*
⚠ Do NOT point at a model name "at the top" — on logged-in web the top shows a Chat/Work toggle,
not a model name. The model picker's location is NOT YET VERIFIED; teach the concept without
anchoring to a spot until it is confirmed on screen.

**4:34–5:24**
> So what does the model actually do. When you press enter, it looks at everything in front of
> it and produces the words most likely to come next. That is the mechanism. Not looking
> anything up, not thinking it over — producing the most likely continuation of what it was
> given.

*On screen: a simple sentence being completed word by word, deliberately slowly.*

**5:24–6:11**
> Which explains something you will run into constantly. Ask a hundred people to describe a
> chair and you get four legs, wooden, nothing you would remember. Not one of them is wrong. The
> average is just unmemorable. If you give it almost nothing to go on, the most likely answer is
> the most average one — and that is what lands in front of you. It is not being lazy. That flat
> answer is what most-likely looks like when nothing has narrowed it down.

*On screen: hold on a plain, generic paragraph of output.*

---

## CHAPTER THREE — So what do I type · 6:05–8:40

**6:11–6:36**
> Which brings us to the first thing everybody types, and it is the one I would skip.

*On screen: type "what can you do?" — let the answer come.*

**6:36–7:06**
> A list. A perfectly accurate, completely useless list. You have learned nothing about whether
> this is any good, because you did not give it anything to be good at. This is the moment most
> people decide it is overrated.

*On screen: hold on the capability list. Let it be as dull as it is.*

**7:06–7:51**
> So try this instead. Take one real thing off your actual plate today — something small, and
> something you would not mind getting back a bit wrong. Then tell it who it is for and what it
> needs to do.

*On screen: a real small task typed out in full. The answer streams. ⚠ the exact example task to
be chosen with Ali — it must be genuinely ordinary, not a showcase.*

**7:51–8:21**
> That is usable. Not perfect — you would still change it — but that is a starting point rather
> than a blank page. Same tool. Same minute. The only difference is that this one had something
> to work with.

*On screen: the two answers side by side.*

**8:21–8:41** · Recap
> So: the box, the list, the new chat, the settings. The tool is what you click; the model is
> what writes. And it produces the most likely next words for whatever you put in front of it.

*On screen: the four labelled parts, then the two nouns, as stills.*

**8:41–8:51**
> Give it one real thing today. Not a test. Something off your actual list.

*On screen: final card — Give it one real thing.*
