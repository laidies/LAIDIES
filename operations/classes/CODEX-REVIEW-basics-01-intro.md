# PART 1 — What LAiDIES is (so you can judge fit)

**The thing.** LAiDIES teaches AI to smart women who were not invited into the tech conversation.
Not a blog, not a course — a **town, SUNNYVAiLE, permanently set in 1999**, with a weekly episode,
a radio station, a high school (where these classes live), a library, characters. Tagline: *girl
power meets machine power.*

**The reader.** A busy, intelligent woman, **no computer-science background**. She runs things,
manages people, has judgment; she is new to this *one* subject, not to competence. **Talking down
to her is the number-one failure.** Assuming she already knows the jargon is number two.

**The voice.**
- **1999, always.** No "nowadays", no "the future". Modern software exists — it lives *inside the
  television*. Real history from before 1999 (e.g. Grace Hopper, 1950s) is fair game.
- **Insider, not nostalgia-bait.** Humour comes from what this reader lived (AIM away messages,
  Seventeen quizzes, Trapper Keepers, butterfly clips, Delia*s, Blockbuster, the chick-flick canon)
  and always lands **on the AI point**, never on her.
- **Two cringes to catch:** influencer-hustle ("stop scrolling", "link in bio", hype) and tech-bro
  ("disrupt", "10x", "ship it", "game-changer"). Heuristic: *if a man in a quarter-zip would post
  it, it's wrong.*
- **AI is "it"** — never "she/her". It does not think, want, understand, decide or remember in the
  human sense; say what it mechanically does (produces, stores, re-reads, fills in). **"AI" is
  always both capitals**; lowercase `Ai` belongs ONLY to brand words (LAiDIES, SUNNYVAiLE, etc.).
- **Plain teaching carries; the analogy garnishes.** The explanation must read exactly as plainly
  as you'd say it to a smart friend. **Test:** if a plain chat-style sentence would be clearer than
  the drafted line, the line is wrong — flag it.

**What makes LAiDIES different from a googleable guide:** a *place* she returns to, owned
interactive tools (above all the **FAiRY Godmother Prompt Glow-Up** — paste your real prompt, watch
it get rewritten and see what was missing), teaching through story and a real voice, and **fluency
and judgment over syntax**. A script that could point at something we own beats a generic tip — but
a forced plug is worse than none.

# PART 2 — What THIS class is (judge it as this, not as something else)

**Basics · Class 1 — the intro-to-AI opener.** The very first class anyone takes. It is **general
and tool-agnostic** (true of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini). Its job is **capability awareness + a truer
mental model** — for someone who doesn't yet know what these tools are, that they're broadly
interchangeable, or what one could do for *her*.

**It is deliberately NOT:**
- Not a prompting lesson (that's Episode 2 and a later class — it may point there, not teach it).
- Not a deep how-to / screen walkthrough (that's the per-tool subjects and a later screen class).
- Not a "name the furniture / here's the box you type in" tour — that approach was **retired** for
  being condescending. Do not ask it to add one.

So judge it as an **orientation that makes her unafraid and shows her it's for her real life** —
not as a tutorial. But "orientation" is not an excuse for empty. It must still teach something
true and usable.

**The six real jobs it draws on** (these were independently vetted; each example's prompt is
specific on purpose, and none asks the tool to know something it can't):
1. Draft the client email you're dreading (paste her email; your terms are in the prompt).
2. Digest a 34-page report before a meeting → one-screen briefing with page numbers.
3. Compare two job offers side by side → table + trade-offs; decision stays yours.
4. Plan a week of dinners + grocery list (allergy + preferences supplied).
5. Write a wedding toast from details you give it.
6. Make sense of a health-insurance letter → plain read that flags what the letter *doesn't* say.

**Known tension the reader will feel and the script currently does NOT address:** it tells her to
paste sensitive documents (an insurance letter, two job offers, a client email) and says **nothing
about whether that's safe to do**. Weigh whether that's a real gap.

**Runtime target 5–10 min.** The script is ~1,000 words (~7 min of narration). The rest of the
time is meant to be *watching it work on screen*. The script currently shows ONE example in full
(the letter) and merely *names* the other five.

# PART 3 — What to review (these are the exact questions)

Answer every one, in this order, about the script inlined in PART 5.

### 1. Accuracy — is every factual claim true?
- The **Grace Hopper** beat: is what it claims accurate and not overstated? (She built the A-0
  compiler, ~1951–52, and FLOW-MATIC, documented as the first English-like programming language,
  which fed into COBOL, 1959.) Flag any overclaim or wrong attribution.
- "**You can talk to it with your voice**": true across the category as stated? The script keeps
  this general on purpose — flag if it accidentally overclaims *talk-back* parity or a specific
  tool/screen.
- "**All three tools — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — will do every one of these**": fair, or too
  strong for any of the six?
- Each **example's described result**: is it something the tool can actually produce from only what
  the prompt supplies (plus a pasted/uploaded doc)? Flag any that would need knowledge it can't
  have, or that overclaims (legal/medical/financial advice; reliable arithmetic on a document).
- Anything that **bakes in an expiry** (a model version, a stated cutoff date) — there should be
  none.

### 2. Sense — does anything not hold together?
- Any sentence that **doesn't make sense**, is a non-sequitur, or is confusing on first listen.
- Any **internal contradiction** (e.g. "you don't have to phrase it right" vs the pointer that how
  you ask matters — does the script reconcile these or trip over them?).
- Any place a first-time listener would **have to pause and re-listen**.

### 3. Explanation — is it explained *well*?
- Where does it **assert instead of explain**? The five rapid-fire examples ("paste X → get Y") —
  do they teach, or are they a dressed list? Say which.
- Where would a **plainer sentence** be clearer than the drafted line? (Garnish-not-carry test.)
- Is the featured **letter example** explained clearly enough that she believes it and could copy
  the move?

### 4. Usefulness — would she actually find this useful?
- Name the **single most useful thing** she genuinely gains. If you can't name one, say so — that's
  the finding.
- Is there a **"I could do that Tuesday" takeaway**, concretely?
- Is any beat **decoration without payoff**? Specifically weigh the **Grace Hopper history** (~45s):
  does it earn its time by dismantling "I'm not technical enough", or is it a tangent that delays
  the useful part? Recommend keep / trim / cut, with reasoning.
- What's **missing** that this reader would want on day one? (Consider at least: is it *safe* to
  paste private documents; does it cost money.) Recommend whether each belongs here or in a later
  class.
- **Runtime honesty:** is ~1,000 words really 5–8 *useful* minutes, or is it thin? Which of the six
  examples should be **shown working on screen** (not just named) to earn the time and the
  usefulness? Give a specific recommendation.

### 5. LAiDIES brand — does it sound like us, and how to make it *more* us?
- Flag any voice break: talking down · a joke at her expense · tech-bro/influencer register ·
  personifying the AI · "Ai" outside a brand word · exclamation marks in narration · hype phrasing.
- Then, constructively: **where could it be more LAiDIES** without forcing it? Give **concrete line
  suggestions** — an insider 1999 reference that lands on the AI point, a tie to town canon or the
  FAiRY Godmother, a warmer turn. Mark anything that would be a *forced* plug (worse than none).

=== HOST VOICE (Jessica) ===

[warm] You know the letter. It comes in a windowed envelope, on paper a little thicker than it needs to be. Across the top, in bold, it says "This is not a bill." Underneath: a column of codes, a few dollar amounts, and a sentence that starts "in accordance with your plan." You read it once. You read it again. And you still can't answer the one question you opened it for — did they pay this, or didn't they. It isn't that you're missing something everyone else understands. That letter was written to be filed, not to be read.

There's a tool that can read it back to you, in plain words. It's a tool you talk to in plain English. You type what you want; it writes back. There's no code to learn, no special words, no wall of buttons to figure out first. If you can text a friend, you already know how to work it.

And you don't only have to type. You can talk to it — out loud, with your actual voice — and have it answer. Hands full in the kitchen, walking out to the car, or you just don't feel like typing it all out: you say what you want, plainly, the way you'd say it to a friend, and it takes it from there. For a lot of people that's the difference between meaning to try one of these and actually trying it.

And you don't have to phrase it the "right" way — no syntax to memorize, no magic words. For most of computing history, getting a machine to do anything meant speaking its language: raw code, numbers. In the nineteen-fifties, a Navy mathematician named Grace Hopper built the first programming language you could write in something close to plain English — the reason programming isn't all code today. But even then, you had to learn that language and meet the machine halfway. Now you don't: you use your own words, and it comes to you. Grace got us most of the way there; AI closes the gap.

You've probably heard the names by now: Chat G-P-T, Claude, Gemini. They're broadly the same kind of thing, and they mostly do the same jobs. Once you can use one, the others feel familiar — like different magazines on the same rack. Same sections, a different voice. There are real differences between them, but none that matter on your first day. So which one you pick barely matters right now. Pick one.

Now — back to that letter. You paste it in, all of it, codes and all, and you ask, in plain words, what it actually says.

[measured] What comes back is a plain read. What the letter is telling you. Whether they paid it, denied it, or billed you — and for how much. Any deadline, quoted exactly the way the letter put it. A short list of questions to ask when you call them. And where the letter is silent, it says so — it flags what the letter doesn't tell you, instead of handing you an answer that only sounds right.

[thoughtful] It won't turn you into a claims expert, and it can still read a line wrong. That's why the questions-to-ask are the part that earns its keep — it gets you oriented enough to make the call, and leaves the judgment with you.

And that same move works on a lot of what's already on your plate. Paste the email you've been dreading, with your terms — a hundred and fifty dollars per extra revision round — and back come two warm drafts that hold that line and offer a call. Upload the thirty-four-page report before your meeting, and back comes a one-screen briefing: three plain takeaways, the figures the report itself says moved since last quarter, two risks, and three questions to ask, each with a page number to check. Paste two job offers side by side, and back comes a comparison table, with "not stated" wherever a letter goes quiet, and the real trade-offs laid out — the decision stays yours. Type out a week of dinners and your rules, and back come five thirty-minute dinners that skip the shellfish and go easy on the red meat, two of them sharing ingredients, with a grocery list sorted by aisle. Type the wedding toast you keep putting off, and back comes a three-minute toast, built from the details you gave it, warm and a little funny, landing right on the toast.

All three tools — Chat G-P-T, Claude, Gemini — will do every one of these. And how you word the ask, so that what comes back is actually this good — that's its own class.

Notice that your own material was in every one of those, because that's the next piece: how it gets your stuff. There are three ways, and you just watched all three. You type or paste it straight in — the letter, the toast, the week of dinners. You upload a file — a Word document, a PDF, a spreadsheet — the way you'd hand over that thirty-four-page report. And later on, you can connect things you already use, like your email, so it reaches them without you pasting a thing. That last one is its own class too.

[warm] So — one thing to do today. Pick something real off your actual list. Not a test, not a poke to see if it works — a real thing. The letter you can't parse. The email you're dreading. Give it to one tool, and read what comes back. A real task is the only thing that shows you what it's genuinely good at.

Next class, we'll walk the screen together — what each part of it is for. The one after that gets into how to phrase an ask so the answer comes back worth having. For today, though: one real thing, one tool. See what it does.
