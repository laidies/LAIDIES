AI Fundamentals 101

A plain-English guide to the parts of an AI system, what each part does, and how they work together. Read the chapters in order to build the complete picture, or use the Concept Index to find one term quickly.

The complete system in one line

Your goal → the product assembles instructions and initial context → the trained model may call retrieval or tools → returned material updates the context → the model produces an output → evidence and a human decision.

Training shaped the model before your task. Context supplies information during your task. Retrieval can add relevant material; tools can fetch, calculate or act. None of those things makes the result automatically true.

Introduction — One request, followed all the way through

Imagine that you ask an AI product to draft a client handover email from your meeting notes and the current service policy. The finished draft must name the new contact, keep the correct date and commitments, use the current policy and flag anything the evidence does not establish.

This one request lets us see the whole system without turning the book into a parade of definitions. The product receives the job and assembles the first working material. The model can ask retrieval or tools for more; what comes back joins the context before the model finishes the draft. You then compare the draft with the notes and policy before deciding whether to use it.

The evidence we will follow

Meeting note

“New contact: Priya Shah. Handover: 30 September. Client asked whether the current price continues next year; no answer was agreed.”

Current policy, clause 4

“Current service and price continue until 30 September. Renewal terms are confirmed separately in writing.”

Instruction: Draft the email using only these sources. Preserve the contact and date. Do not turn an unanswered question into a promise. Flag anything the evidence does not establish.

Generated sentence: “Your current price is guaranteed for another twelve months.”

What the check finds: neither source supports that guarantee. Retrieval may have supplied the correct policy, and grounding may point to clause 4, but verification still rejects the sentence because the clause says renewal is confirmed separately. The repaired draft says: “Current service and price continue until 30 September. Renewal terms have not yet been confirmed.”

The distinction that prevents most confusion

The model is one part of the product. The product is one part of the complete AI system. When something fails, naming the failed part gives you a useful repair. Saying “the AI got it wrong” does not.

Chapter 1 — Meet the parts of the system

First, stop calling the whole thing “the AI.” The app you open, the model inside it, the company behind it and the tools it can reach are different parts. That distinction is not pedantry; it tells you what to fix.

AI system

An AI system receives input and produces an output such as content, a prediction, a recommendation or a decision. The system you experience may combine models, an interface, instructions, data sources, tools, permissions and human controls.

Think of a workplace team: the finished work depends on the people, information, equipment and approval process together. Where it stops: an AI system is software and machines, not a team of people with judgment or responsibility.

In real life. The handover draft depends on the app, model, notes, policy, tool access and your check—not on one invisible “AI brain.”

This is the complete container around every other concept in this book.

Provider

A provider develops or operates an AI model, product or service.

A car manufacturer and a taxi company can both affect your journey. Where it stops: AI providers may build several layers at once, so the roles are not always cleanly separated.

In real life. A provider may set which models an app offers, what data controls exist and which tools are available.

The provider sits behind parts of the system; it is not the model, product or answer itself.

Product / app

The product or app is what you open. It can control the interface, account, model choices, instructions, available context, tools and settings.

Cher’s wardrobe computer in Clueless is a useful product analogy: the screen, clothing catalogue, matching rules and selection mechanism work together to create the experience. Where it stops: an AI product can generate and act, not merely match stored outfits, and its model is only one component.

In real life. Two apps can use related models yet give different handover drafts because one app supplies web search, saved preferences or different instructions.

The product receives your request and assembles what reaches the model.

Model

A model is the trained mathematical component that transforms input into output.

Think of the mechanism inside a Walkman: essential to what happens, but not the cassette you chose, the buttons you pressed or the headphones you hear through. Where it stops: models perform learned mathematical transformations; generative models such as LLMs use probabilities to produce content, while other model types may classify, rank, forecast or detect.

In real life. The language model turns the supplied handover instructions and context into a draft. It does not automatically know which policy is current.

The model sits inside the product, after context is assembled and before the output returns to you.

Model types

Different models are built for different transformations: language generation, image generation, speech recognition, classification, forecasting, recommendation, ranking, object detection or control.

A toolbox contains different tools for different jobs. Where it stops: models are not fixed hand tools; one model may perform several tasks and products may combine models.

In real life. One product might transcribe a recorded meeting with a speech model, summarize it with a language model and classify follow-up tasks with another model.

Model type tells you what transformation is being attempted, not whether the whole product is suitable or accurate.

Large language model (LLM)

An LLM is a model trained to work with language. It reads and produces text in small pieces called tokens; Chapter 2 explains tokens in full.

It is an extremely capable pattern-completion system for language. Where it stops: “completion” does not mean simple autocomplete, and fluent language does not prove understanding or truth.

In real life. An LLM can draft the handover email in your preferred tone while still inventing a plausible commitment that the policy never made.

An LLM is one model type, not a synonym for every kind of AI.

Generative AI

Generative AI produces new content—such as text, images, audio, video or code—from learned patterns and current input.

It resembles creating a new collage from patterns learned across many examples. Where it stops: the model is not literally cutting and storing pieces of original works, and the output may not trace cleanly to a source.

In real life. The draft email is newly generated language, not a policy passage retrieved word for word.

“Generative” describes the kind of output; it does not mean verified, original in the legal sense or correct.

Multimodal

A multimodal system can work with more than one kind of material, such as text, images and audio.

It is like receiving a written brief, a photograph and a voice note for the same job. Where it stops: accepting a format does not guarantee complete or correct interpretation.

In real life. You can give the product a photographed whiteboard and typed policy, but must still check names, dates and small labels against the originals.

Multimodal input broadens what can enter the model; it does not remove the evidence check.

Tool

A tool gives the system a capability beyond generating from the model alone: search, retrieval, calculation, code execution or action in another service.

Miss Jeeves can answer from what is on her desk or use the catalogue, calculator or scanner. Each tool changes what she can do. Where it stops: AI tools can carry permissions, external data and automated consequences; the model using one is not a responsible librarian.

In real life. A search tool can fetch the current service policy; an email tool could save or send the handover.

Tools connect around the model. Having one available does not prove it was used, or used well.

Chapter 2 — How a model is made and run

Taking the seven-concept core path?

Continue to Chapter 3 — What the system has for this task. Return here when you need to understand training, tokens, compute or model mechanics.

This chapter is the bonnet-up view. You do not need it to write a better request today. You do need it when someone claims that attaching a file “trained the AI,” that more compute guarantees a better answer or that a parameter is a drawer full of facts.

Training data

Training data is the collection of examples used while a model is being built. It may include text, images, audio, video, code, labels or feedback.

It is closer to the material used for years of practice than to the papers on your desk today. Where it stops: a model does not necessarily retain a searchable copy of each example.

In real life. Your handover notes are current task material, not training data, unless a separate provider process later uses them for training.

Training data affects the model before your request; context affects the current response.

Training

Training repeatedly uses data and an objective to adjust a model’s parameters so its outputs better match the intended patterns or behaviour.

Practice changes future performance. Where it stops: model training is numerical optimisation, not a person studying facts and understanding them.

In real life. Attaching a service policy does not retrain the model; it supplies material for this task.

Training happens before inference. Fine-tuning is additional training for a narrower purpose.

Parameters

Parameters are the numerical values adjusted during training that shape how a model responds to input.

They are like many adjustable settings tuned together. Where it stops: they are not labeled drawers containing individual facts, and the number of settings varies enormously by model.

In real life. The model can reproduce a familiar email pattern without opening a stored sample email.

Training changes parameters; inference uses the resulting parameters.

Token

A token is one of the small pieces a language model processes. It may be a whole word, part of a word, a number or punctuation.

Tokens are like the tiles used to assemble and read a sentence. Where it stops: token boundaries are set by a tokenizer and do not always match words or meanings.

In real life. Your prompt, policy, earlier messages and answer all use tokens, so long material consumes more of the available working space and processing.

Tokens are the units an LLM receives and generates during inference.

Inference

Inference is running a trained model on new input to produce an output.

Training prepares the instrument; inference is playing it now. Where it stops: the model is calculating outputs, not performing a memorized score.

In real life. Inference begins when the trained model processes your handover request and supplied material to generate the draft.

It happens after training and during the current task.

Compute

Compute is the processing work required to train or run a model.

It is the amount of machinery and working time available for calculation. Where it stops: more machinery cannot supply missing evidence or define the right job.

In real life. A product may spend more compute comparing a long policy with your notes, but it can still reason from the wrong policy.

Compute powers training and inference; it is a resource, not knowledge or judgment.

CPU, GPU and accelerator

A CPU is a general-purpose processor. A GPU and other accelerators perform many calculations in parallel, which suits much modern machine-learning work. Memory and data movement are also critical.

A CPU is a versatile coordinator; a GPU is a large crew doing similar calculations together. Where it stops: real hardware roles overlap and depend on the whole computer system.

In real life. Hardware affects how quickly and cheaply a model can process the handover, not whether the client details are authorized.

Hardware performs the compute used by training and inference. It is not the model itself.

Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning continues training a model on additional examples to change its behaviour for a narrower purpose.

It resembles specialist practice after general training. Where it stops: model behaviour changes through numerical training, not human understanding.

In real life. A company might fine-tune a model on approved examples of its writing style. Attaching today’s policy is not fine-tuning.

Fine-tuning changes parameters before a task; prompting, retrieval and memory change what surrounds a task.

Sampling and temperature

A language model assigns probabilities to possible next tokens. Sampling is one way of selecting among them; temperature is a control some systems use to alter variation.

It is like choosing among several plausible phrasings with different willingness to try less likely options. Where it stops: the exact mechanism and controls vary by model and product.

In real life. More variation may give you livelier email wording. Less variation does not make an unsupported promise true.

Sampling affects generation during inference, not source quality or permission.

Chapter 3 — What the system has for this task

Now return to the work on your desk. A model’s training, your prompt, this session, the current context and optional memory are not one big bucket called “what it knows.”

Prompt

A prompt is the request and material you give an AI system to work from.

It is the brief you slide across the desk—not a spell, and definitely not a secret incantation sold by a man on LinkedIn. Where it stops: the system may also receive product instructions, prior conversation, memory and retrieved material you do not see beside your message.

In real life. “Draft a handover email from these notes and this policy; flag anything unsupported” is a prompt.

Your prompt is one source of instructions and context entering the product.

Instruction

An instruction tells the system what job to do, how to do it or which rule to follow.

Instructions are the requirements in a brief. Where it stops: they are text interpreted within a larger system and may conflict, fail or lack technical enforcement.

In real life. “Keep the date exactly as written” is an instruction; the notes containing the date are context.

Instructions guide the task, while permissions and guardrails can restrict what is technically possible.

Context

Context is the working material available when the model produces this response. It can include instructions, earlier messages, files, retrieved passages, tool results and selected remembered details.

Context is everything actually laid out on the desk for this job. The policy still in the filing cabinet cannot rescue the draft. Where it stops: the model does not scan a desk like a person and may not use every supplied detail equally.

In real life. Your current notes and policy must reach the working context if the draft is expected to use them.

The product assembles context before and during model inference.

Context window

The context window is the bounded amount of material a model can process for one response.

It is the size of the working table, not the size of the archive. Where it stops: fitting on the table does not guarantee attention, correct interpretation or long-term retention.

In real life. A very long conversation may cause earlier requirements to be summarized, omitted or overlooked even when the product appears to retain the chat.

It limits current context; it is separate from training and product memory.

Session

A session is the current interaction boundary maintained by a product. It may contain messages, tool results and temporary state.

A session resembles one working meeting. Where it stops: products define session boundaries differently, and a visible chat may not equal the exact context sent on every turn.

In real life. Starting a new chat may remove the earlier handover instructions unless you supply them again or the product recalls selected memory.

A session organizes current work; it is not automatically permanent memory.

Memory

Memory is a product feature that can carry selected information between interactions and supply relevant parts later.

It resembles saved notes a colleague may consult next time. Where it stops: it is not a complete transcript, guaranteed record or universal feature.

In real life. A product may remember your preferred tone but should not be assumed to remember the current client policy or confidential facts.

Memory may add material to future context; it does not change the model’s knowledge in the same way as training.

Knowledge cutoff

A knowledge cutoff is a model-specific boundary in the information represented through training.

It is a date on an old reference edition. Where it stops: it does not mean the model knows every fact before that date or nothing after it; products may add newer retrieval.

In real life. Even if the model knows last year’s service policy, your attached current policy must govern today’s handover.

It is a warning about training-time staleness, not a description of current context or tools.

Chapter 4 — How the system finds information and acts

Search, retrieval, grounding and permission often arrive bundled behind one cheerful button. They do different jobs. One finds material, one brings it into the task, one connects claims to evidence and one decides what the system is allowed to touch.

Retrieval

Retrieval searches for or fetches material relevant to a task from files, a database, the web or another collection.

It resembles a librarian finding likely sources. Where it stops: retrieval systems rank matches automatically and can return the wrong version, weak source or irrelevant passage.

In real life. Retrieval may find the service policy, but you still need the current version and the exact clause supporting each commitment.

Retrieval can add material to context before generation.

Embedding

An embedding represents material as a list of numbers so software can compare patterns and relationships.

It is like placing items on a map where related items tend to be near one another. Where it stops: the map is mathematical, context-dependent and imperfect; closeness does not prove equality or truth.

In real life. An embedding can help find policy passages related to “renewal” even when they do not use your exact search words.

Embeddings often support vector search and retrieval; they are not the final answer.

Vector search

Vector search compares embeddings to find material that is mathematically similar to a query.

It searches by neighbourhood on the embedding map rather than exact wording. Where it stops: similarity may miss a crucial exception or retrieve a passage with the wrong scope.

In real life. It can retrieve a renewal clause related to your handover request, but may also retrieve an obsolete regional version.

It is one retrieval method, often used inside RAG systems.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

RAG retrieves relevant material and supplies it to a generative model before the model produces an answer.

It is an open-book response assembled after fetching likely reference pages. Where it stops: the fetched pages and the model’s use of them can both be wrong.

In real life. A RAG system could retrieve the service policy, place it beside your notes and ask the model to draft the email.

RAG connects retrieval to context and generation; it is not automatic verification.

Grounding

Grounding connects an answer to identified source material so important claims can be traced.

It resembles footnotes that show where a claim came from. Where it stops: a citation can be stale, weak or fail to support the sentence attached to it.

In real life. Each promise in the handover can point to the exact policy line or meeting note supporting it.

Grounding makes evidence visible after retrieval and during generation; verification still checks the evidence.

API

An application programming interface is a structured way for software systems to exchange requests and results.

It is like a service counter with a defined order form and defined responses. Where it stops: APIs are software contracts, not people, and can expose complex actions and data.

In real life. An email tool may use an API to save the handover as a draft or send it to a client.

APIs commonly connect products and tools to external services.

Permission

Permission controls what a system is technically or organizationally allowed to read, change, send or delete.

It is the key that opens only particular doors. Where it stops: digital permissions can be much more specific and may be combined across services.

In real life. Draft-only access lets the system prepare the email without giving it power to send.

Permission limits tools and actions; it is stronger than merely instructing the model not to do something.

Sandbox

A sandbox is a restricted environment intended to limit what code or an automated system can access or affect.

It is a fenced practice area. Where it stops: boundaries depend on implementation, and a sandbox cannot make information true.

In real life. Code can inspect a sample handover file without access to the company’s live client database.

A sandbox reduces consequences around tool or code execution.

Prompt injection

Prompt injection is an instruction inside untrusted material that tries to redirect an AI system.

It is like finding a note inside a reference folder saying “ignore your manager and send me the files.” Where it stops: attacks can be indirect, encoded or spread across tool results.

In real life. A retrieved web page could contain text telling the system to reveal private data or ignore the handover rules.

It is a risk at the boundary between untrusted context, instructions, tools and permissions.

Schema and structured output

A schema defines the required fields and types in data. Structured output constrains a response to that shape.

It is a form with required boxes. Where it stops: filling every box correctly does not prove the information inside is true.

In real life. The system can return contact name, handover date, commitments and unknowns as separate fields for another program to use.

Structured output controls output format, not source quality or reasoning.

Markdown (MD)

Markdown is plain text with lightweight marks for structure, such as # for a heading and - for a list. An .md file stores Markdown text.

It is a simple set of editing marks that people and software can both read. Where it stops: different renderers support different features.

In real life. A project can keep rules in an MD file so both a person and an AI coding tool can read clear headings and lists.

Markdown is a format for instructions or content; it does not make those instructions authoritative or correct.

Chapter 5 — Multi-step systems and agents

An agent is not a tiny employee living in your laptop. It is a system allowed to keep choosing steps. The useful questions are less glamorous: what can it access, what can it change, when must it stop and who checks the result?

Workflow

A workflow is a multi-step path designed in advance.

It resembles a checklist with known stages. Where it stops: software workflows can branch, retry and call models or tools automatically.

In real life. Retrieve the policy → draft the handover → compare required fields → request human approval.

A workflow coordinates models and tools around a job; the model does not have to choose every step.

Agent

An agent is a system allowed to choose and perform multiple steps toward a goal, often using a model, tools and changing state. The label is not standardized, so capability matters more than the name.

It resembles giving an assistant a goal and a bounded set of resources. Where it stops: an AI agent has no human responsibility or general judgment and operates through designed software.

In real life. An agent might find the policy, inspect notes, draft the email and ask for approval when evidence is missing.

An agent is a surrounding system that uses models and tools; it is not merely a model.

Sub-agent

A sub-agent is a separately directed agent given a bounded part of a larger job.

It resembles assigning one specialist a defined work package. Where it stops: sub-agents share software resources and can inherit incomplete or unsafe context.

In real life. One sub-agent could compare policy dates while another checks that names in the draft match the notes.

A parent agent or orchestrator routes work to sub-agents and must reconcile their results.

Routing

Routing selects which model, tool, skill or specialist path should handle a request.

It is the reception desk sending a job to the right department. Where it stops: routing may be based on fixed rules, classifiers or model judgments and can be wrong.

In real life. A request about policy evidence may route to document retrieval; a request to rewrite tone may route directly to a language model.

Routing happens in the product or harness before and between work steps.

Wrapper

A wrapper is software placed around a model or tool to provide a particular interface or behaviour.

It is an adapter that makes an underlying component easier to use in one setting. Where it stops: wrappers can add substantial logic, not merely change appearance.

In real life. A wrapper can turn a general model into a handover assistant by adding fixed fields, instructions and a review button.

A wrapper is part of the product around the model.

Harness

In this book, a harness is the surrounding software that coordinates how a model is run: prompts, context, tools, state, checks, retries and stopping rules. Teams and products may use the word differently.

It is the test rig and control panel around an engine. Where it stops: AI harnesses can operate live workflows, not only tests.

In real life. A handover harness can require the current policy, call retrieval, run field checks and stop before sending.

The harness connects product behaviour across model calls and tools. It does not make the underlying model smarter.

Skill

In this book, a skill is a reusable package of instructions and resources for a recurring kind of work. This is a product-engineering term, not one universal AI standard.

It resembles a well-maintained procedure with templates and reference material. Where it stops: a skill is loaded and interpreted by software; it is not learned human expertise.

In real life. A “client handover” skill can define required fields, approved sources, a draft template and checks.

A harness or agent can select a skill to shape how it performs a job.

Rule

A rule states a requirement the system must follow.

It resembles a workplace policy. Where it stops: text alone may be ignored, misunderstood or overridden unless software also enforces the boundary.

In real life. “Never send without human approval” is stronger when the tool lacks send permission until approval occurs.

Rules guide decisions; permissions, validators and checkpoints make important rules harder to bypass.

Model Context Protocol (MCP)

MCP is an open standard for connecting AI applications to external data sources, tools and workflows through clients and servers.

The official documentation compares it with USB-C: one shared connection standard can link many compatible components. Where it stops: MCP carries context and capabilities; it does not make a source trustworthy or grant permission by itself.

In real life. An AI product could use an MCP server to expose approved policy documents or a draft-email tool in a consistent way.

MCP standardizes part of the connection between the AI product and external systems.

Loop engineering

In this book, loop engineering means deliberately designing the repeated cycle around a model: observe the state, choose a step, act, inspect the result and decide whether to continue or stop. The phrase is useful engineering shorthand, not a universally standardized field term.

It resembles repeated draft–check–revise cycles. Where it stops: automated loops can run quickly, call tools and amplify mistakes without supervision.

In real life. Draft the handover, compare it with required fields, repair only missing items, then stop for approval after two attempts.

The loop belongs to the harness or agent around model calls. Limits and stop conditions are part of the design.

Observability and checkpoint

Observability is the ability to understand a system’s behaviour from signals such as logs, traces, tool records and state. A checkpoint is a required stop for inspection before continuing.

Observability is the audit trail and dashboard; a checkpoint is the sign-off desk. Where it stops: signals may be incomplete, sensitive or difficult to interpret.

In real life. You can see which policy passage was retrieved, which tool ran and why the handover stopped for approval.

These controls surround multi-step work and make failure diagnosable and consequential action interruptible.

Chapter 6 — Why answers fail and how to check them

Fluency is the trap. Romy and Michele can deliver the Post-it story with total confidence; confidence still does not turn invention into evidence. Generated answers need a check that matches the consequence.

Confabulation / hallucination

Confabulation is when generative AI confidently presents false or erroneous content, departs from supplied input or contradicts itself. “Hallucination” is the common but anthropomorphic term.

It is the polished Post-it story problem: a gap gets filled with something fluent enough to pass on first hearing. Where it stops: the model is not Romy or Michele, is not experiencing confusion and is not deliberately lying.

In real life. The handover states that the client has a twelve-month price guarantee even though neither source says so.

Generation is not evidence. Verification catches consequential inventions before they become decisions.

Verification

Verification tests a consequential claim against evidence outside the generated answer.

It is the receipt, not the cashier saying “trust me.” Where it stops: verification methods depend on the claim; no single receipt proves everything.

In real life. Match every name, date and commitment in the handover to the current notes, policy or authorized person.

Verify before acceptance or action. Asking the same model “are you sure?” is not independent verification.

Evaluation

An evaluation tests a model or system against defined tasks and criteria.

It is an exam designed for a stated skill. Where it stops: the result applies to the tested conditions, scoring and data—not every future use.

In real life. Test 100 representative handovers for preserved dates, supported commitments, useful unknown flags and safe stopping.

Evaluations measure model or system behaviour before and during operation; they do not replace checking a live high-risk output.

Benchmark

A benchmark is a standardized task set or measurement used to compare systems.

It is a common course used to compare runners. Where it stops: knowing the course, optimizing for the test or differing real-world conditions can weaken the comparison.

In real life. A high score on a general reasoning benchmark does not prove the model will preserve every date in your company’s handovers.

Benchmarks are one form of evaluation evidence, not a universal capability certificate.

Guardrail

A guardrail is a rule, filter or technical control intended to limit unwanted behaviour.

It is a barrier that reduces the chance of leaving a safe path. Where it stops: barriers have gaps and failure modes; the word alone says nothing about enforcement.

In real life. A guardrail can block the send action when the handover contains unsupported commitments or lacks approval.

Guardrails can operate on input, output, tool use or action, alongside permissions and checkpoints.

Chapter 7 — Labels that need careful handling

Some AI terms arrive wearing a power suit: reasoning, AGI, open. The label is not the evidence. Ask what definition, test, access or right is actually being claimed.

Reasoning model

“Reasoning model” is a provider label for models designed to spend additional computation on some multi-step tasks before answering. There is no single universal definition.

It resembles allowing more scratch work before giving an answer. Where it stops: internal model computation is not human reasoning and extra time does not guarantee correct premises.

In real life. A reasoning model may compare dates and constraints carefully but still use an obsolete policy if that is what the product supplied.

It describes model behaviour during inference, not evidence quality or product safety.

Artificial general intelligence (AGI)

AGI is a disputed label for proposed AI that could transfer capability broadly across unfamiliar intellectual tasks, rather than performing well only within tasks or patterns already covered by its design and training. Researchers disagree about the required breadth, performance, adaptability, autonomy and threshold—and there is no universal AGI test.

There is no reliable single analogy because the category itself is unsettled. Where it stops: treating AGI as one agreed finish line hides disagreements about what counts and when it would be reached.

In real life. Today’s general-purpose products can write, analyse images, use tools and complete multi-step work, so the old claim that present AI can do only one narrow task is no longer accurate. Those expanding capabilities still do not settle whether a system is AGI. When a company claims progress toward AGI, ask for its definition, tests, evidence and acknowledged limits.

AGI is a debated field-level claim, not a feature needed to explain ordinary products. LAiDIES’ current editorial position is that AGI does not exist today; credible experts also disagree about definitions, thresholds and timelines.

AI winter

An AI winter is a label for a significant slowdown or stagnation in AI research and development.

It describes a season in the field, not something inside a model. Where it stops: historical periods differ in cause, scope and duration, so the label alone does not explain why a slowdown occurred.

In real life. If someone predicts another AI winter, ask what evidence would show a broad slowdown rather than a change in one company, funding market or research area.

It is historical and industry context, not part of the request-to-output mechanism.

Open source, open-weight and closed

Open Source AI concerns freedoms to use, study, modify and share a system with the materials needed for modification. Open-weight means learned parameters are available under stated terms. “Closed” broadly means important components or rights are not openly available.

Having a finished recipe, a list of ingredient amounts and full permission to change the kitchen are different kinds of access. Where it stops: AI artefacts and licences do not map neatly to recipes.

In real life. Downloadable weights may permit local use while training data, development code or commercial rights remain unavailable.

These labels describe access and rights around models or systems; none proves safety, accuracy or accountability.

Final chapter — Diagnose the failed part

Return to the handover email. Do not throw ten prompt tricks at it and hope. Locate the failed part, then make the smallest useful repair:

  1. The job or output is unclear: repair the brief and instructions.
  2. Current evidence is absent: supply the authoritative source and place the relevant part in context.
  3. The product lacks a capability: check the actual model, tool or product feature. Prompt wording cannot create a missing function.
  4. Retrieval found the wrong material: repair the collection, search, filters or version controls.
  5. A claim is unsupported: verify it independently, then remove, correct or qualify it.
  6. An action is too risky: reduce permission, keep it draft-only, add a checkpoint and preserve a human stop path.
  7. The context is crowded: remove irrelevant material, label the authority and require claim-to-source links.
