That's where you come in. You onboard it, you manage it, you review its work. And you've done this all before.

Here is what is happening underneath the draft. During training, a language model learns patterns from large amounts of material. When you make a request, it uses those learned patterns and the context available for that answer to generate a response. Your request helps shape what it writes; it does not turn the draft into a checked fact. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are products around models. ### The Vocab

Generative AI The kind of AI that can make something you can work with. +

Generative AI produces content from your request and the material you give it—an email, image, summary, slide, song, video or piece of code. It works from patterns learned during training plus the context available now. If the product searches, those sources become material for the answer; they do not make every claim true. It can make the draft. It cannot make it true.

Model The trained component that turns an input into an output. +

A model is the trained component of an AI system that turns an input into an output. It may produce words or images, interpret material, make a prediction or choose a next action. You may reach one through an app, API or coding tool; the product around it can add instructions, memory, search, files and other models. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are whole products—not one permanent model apiece. A model is part of the experience—not the whole thing. Episode 05 gives this relationship its full fashion-house fitting.

Hallucination A made-up detail that arrives dressed like a fact. +

A hallucination is false or unsupported content delivered as part of an AI-generated answer. It can be one invented citation, wrong date or made-up detail inside otherwise useful work—not only a completely fictional response. Think of the Burn Book from Mean Girls : the tone supplies no warning label when an allegation is false. Polished is a style. Evidence is a standard.

## Study pack — the Cheat Sheet

# On Wednesdays We Do AI
## The lesson to keep

Start with one small task you can judge. AI can get a draft moving; your experience helps decide what is worth keeping. Steve does not get a monopoly on the useful tools. Dolly's common-sense rule: a draft still has to fit your actual life.

## What happened between your request and the answer?

**Learned patterns + the context available now → a generated draft → your check and edit.**

Training shapes the model before you arrive. Your request supplies material for this answer. The product may also supply earlier messages, saved preferences, files or search results. You cannot assume every relevant detail reached it—or that every detail it returns is true.

## Four things worth remembering

**Generative AI** produces content, such as text or images, from learned patterns and the input available to it. A convincing draft can still contain a mistake.

**A model** is the trained component that processes input and produces output. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are products around models, with their own features and settings. You are comparing the experience you can use today.

**A hallucination** is false or unsupported content in an AI answer. One made-up reservation inside an otherwise useful invitation counts. It does not have to be an entire fictional universe.

**The participation gap** describes differences in how often groups use AI. It measures use, not ability. More participation creates opportunities to practise and question the tools; it does not make every user's chat an automatic training update.

## Your first useful comparison

Give the products the same small, permitted request. Then ask:

- Did it understand what I needed?
- What could I actually use?
- What would I check or change?

If the invitation sounds like you but says a table is booked when it isn't, keep the useful wording and remove the invented plan. Confidence is not a reservation.

Use a personal-life task or an invented example. For work, check that both the tool and the information are allowed. Removing a name alone may leave sensitive details behind.

**Only one tool available?** Start there. Save the result and add another comparison when you can. A useful first attempt does not require three subscriptions.

**Keep in your binder:** the request, your comparison and the version you chose to keep. Next episode, David Rose helps you make the ask more specific.

## Quiz — questions and feedback

Ten scored questions and two optional bonus questions. This review copy includes the answer key and the feedback for every option; the interactive quiz reveals feedback after submission.


### 1. You have fifteen minutes and no AI experience. What fits this episode?

- **A. Try a small task whose result you can judge.** **Correct.**
  A familiar task gives you a way to judge the draft, even on your first try.

- **B. Finish a technical course before opening a tool.**
  You can learn by trying one manageable task; a technical course is not a prerequisite.

- **C. Hand over a high-stakes decision to save time.**
  Start where a mistake is easy to catch and repair. A consequential decision needs more than a quick first try.

- **D. Find the best model for every possible task.**
  You only need a useful tool for this task. One attempt cannot settle every future choice.

### 2. You want to try the invitation exercise in a personal AI tool. Which input fits?

- **A. A copied conversation with everyone’s private details.**
  The other people’s private details are unnecessary for this practice. Invent the situation instead.

- **B. An invented situation with the choices the message needs.** **Correct.**
  You keep the useful constraints while leaving real people’s private information out.

- **C. A client email with only the client’s name removed.**
  Names are not the only identifying or sensitive details. An invented example avoids relying on name removal.

- **D. A colleague’s document because it is on your laptop.**
  Being able to open a document does not establish permission to share it with an AI provider.

### 3. The product writes an invitation you have never seen before. What is it doing?

- **A. Finding a verified invitation that matches your life.**
  A generated invitation is not a verified record of your plans. Check the details against what you actually supplied.

- **B. Retraining its entire model on your short request.**
  This reply uses the model’s learned patterns; your request supplies context for the answer.

- **C. Generating content from learned patterns and available context.** **Correct.**
  The product uses learned patterns and available context to produce the wording. You still check what that wording claims.

- **D. Confirming that every proposed detail is correct.**
  Producing a sentence and checking its truth are different jobs. A draft can propose something nobody arranged.

### 4. Two products give different drafts for the same request. What can you conclude?

- **A. The more polished product must have the better model.**
  Polish tells you about this draft’s presentation. It does not establish which underlying model is better.

- **B. The shorter draft proves the product ignored your request.**
  A short draft may fit the request. Check whether it includes what you needed before judging it by length.

- **C. A model and a product are two names for the same thing.**
  The model is a trained component; the product includes the features and instructions around it.

- **D. Models, context, settings and features can affect the result.** **Correct.**
  Several parts of the product experience can change the answer, even when you type the same request.

### 5. The invitation includes the right days but says a table is booked. Nobody booked it. What matters?

- **A. The whole answer is useless because one detail is wrong.**
  One false detail needs correction, but the surrounding wording may still be useful. Check and edit the draft.

- **B. It contains an unsupported detail inside useful wording.** **Correct.**
  You spotted the false reservation without treating every sentence as worthless. Remove that claim before sending.

- **C. A polished draft means the restaurant was verified.**
  A finished-looking invitation is not evidence of a booking. Check what has actually been arranged.

- **D. The restaurant is safe to keep because it is plausible.**
  A believable restaurant plan is still false if nobody made it. Plausibility cannot supply the missing booking.

### 6. Your draft is far too formal. Which next move could help?

- **A. Explain the audience and tone, then review the revision.** **Correct.**
  Audience and tone give the revision a clearer target. Your next job is to check whether it fits.

- **B. Ask it to sound more confident about the same wording.**
  More confidence would not fix the formal tone. Describe how the invitation should sound to its recipients.

- **C. Assume it knows your friends from your account name.**
  An account name does not tell the product how you and your friends speak to each other. Supply the relevant context.

- **D. Switch products without saying what needs to change.**
  Another product might write differently, but it still needs to know what you want changed.

### 7. Why keep the request the same when comparing products?

- **A. To prove which model will be best for the whole season.**
  This comparison concerns these responses to this task. Different tasks or later product changes can give different results.

- **B. To make every product return identical sentences.**
  The same request can still produce different wording. Those differences are what you are examining.

- **C. To make differences in their responses easier to notice.** **Correct.**
  Keeping the request steady makes the response differences easier to inspect.

- **D. To avoid having to check any of the finished drafts.**
  A comparison is not a fact-check. Each draft still needs checking against your actual task.

### 8. A survey finds that women use AI less often on average. What does that measure?

- **A. How capable women are of learning to use AI.**
  The survey measured use. It did not test women’s ability to learn the tools.

- **B. How accurate every woman’s AI-assisted work is.**
  How often people use AI does not tell you whether their resulting work is accurate.

- **C. How much each individual woman should use AI.**
  A group average is not a prescription for one person. Your useful level of use depends on your tasks and circumstances.

- **D. A difference in reported participation between groups.** **Correct.**
  Reported participation describes use across groups; it does not assign an ability level to individuals.

### 9. The draft follows your instructions but would upset the person receiving it. What now?

- **A. Send it because following the prompt is the standard.**
  Following the words of a request is not enough if the result damages the relationship. Check the likely effect.

- **B. Revise it using what you know about the situation.** **Correct.**
  You know something the draft missed. Use that knowledge to make the message fit its real recipient.

- **C. Treat the tool’s certainty as better social judgment.**
  A certain tone is not evidence that the product understands this relationship.

- **D. Assume another generated draft needs no review.**
  Another draft may help, but you still need to check how it would land with this person.

### 10. You can only access one of the three products today. What should you do?

- **A. Try the task there and save what you learn.** **Correct.**
  One tool is enough for a first attempt you can judge. You can keep that work and compare later.

- **B. Wait until you can pay for all three products.**
  Three subscriptions are not required for a useful first try. Start with what you can access.

- **C. Use confidential work material to make it worthwhile.**
  Confidential material does not make practice more valuable. Use a permitted task or invented details.

- **D. Skip review because there is nothing to compare it with.**
  You can check a draft against your needs and facts without having a second AI answer beside it.

### 11. Does typing your request automatically retrain the model for this answer? — optional bonus

- **A. Yes; each message rebuilds the model before it replies.**
  Supplying a message as context is different from rebuilding the trained model.

- **B. Yes; only paid accounts use previously trained models.**
  A paid plan does not make each request a new training run. Payment and this distinction are separate questions.

- **C. No; the request supplies context for this answer.** **Correct.**
  The request helps shape this answer. Possible later use of conversations for training is a separate question.

- **D. No; the product cannot use the request at all.**
  The request is usable context. Not retraining on it does not mean the product ignores it.

- **B. Saying exactly what result, audience and constraints matter.** **Correct.**
  Result, audience and constraints make “what I want” clear enough to act on.
