A prompt is the material and instruction you give an AI system for a task. A useful one works like a brief: it tells the system what job to do, who the result serves, what it may use, what shape the answer should take and how you will judge it. That can improve the odds of a useful result. It cannot guarantee that the system understands, follows the brief or produces something true.

Brief in five parts

Write these five parts in plain language. They are a durable starting procedure, not a magic formula. Different products, models and tasks can respond differently, so test the result against the job you actually need done.

  1. Job and outcome. Name the deliverable and what it must help the reader do. “Draft a four-sentence client handover email. The client should understand who owns the account and what happens next.”
  2. Audience. Say who will use it and what matters in this moment. “The client is senior, short on time and wary of the handover. I am taking over from James after a three-year relationship.”
  3. Format. Specify the useful length and structure. “Four sentences, under 90 words, with one concrete next step.”
  4. Tone. Describe the writing choices, not only a broad label. “Warm and direct. Sound prepared without over-promising or using a sales pitch.”
  5. Constraints. Name what the answer must use, preserve, exclude or flag. “Use the attached handover note for names, dates and commitments. Do not add a commitment that is not in the note. Flag anything the note does not establish.” Positive descriptions often make a target clearer, but negative constraints remain necessary when they express a real safety, accuracy or product boundary.

Source material is part of the context for the brief, not permission to treat every file as safe or true. Only provide material your account and organization permit you to use. After the draft, check it against the five parts and verify consequential names, dates, numbers and commitments outside the answer; a model’s self-check is not independent verification.

If you cannot fill in one of the five parts, ask the tool to question you before drafting: “Ask me up to three questions needed to complete this brief. Do not draft until I answer.” Its questions may reveal missing context; they do not decide what the goal or evidence should be.

The five-part brief

job + audience + format + tone + constraints. Then check before you use it.

One controlled comparison

Use a harmless task whose answer you can judge. Keep the product, visible model or mode, source material and session the same. Change only the brief.

Vague

“Help with the deck.”

Briefed

“Review the attached Q3 deck for my VP meeting tomorrow. Identify the one slide that most obscures the decision we need. Reply with the slide number, the problem in one direct sentence and one concrete revision. Use only the deck, preserve every sourced number and flag any claim you cannot verify from it.”

Compare the two outputs against the same criteria: Did it perform the intended job? Did it use only the supplied material? Did it follow the requested shape? Which claims still require your check? A more complete brief may improve relevance and control, but the second answer is not guaranteed to be better. Record what changed for this task rather than declaring a universal rule from one trial.

What the analogies are—and are not

The sharp new hire. Anthropic uses a “brilliant but new employee” comparison to explain why its model needs context about your norms and workflow. The useful mapping is missing workplace context. The limit: a model is not a colleague, does not share responsibility or human judgment, and may fail even when the brief would be clear to a person. The analogy tells you nothing about competence, truth, confidentiality, memory, permissions or access.

Romy and Michele. Their polished Post-it-inventor story is a reminder that confident presentation can hide missing substance. The limit: a vague prompt does not always cause fabrication, and a detailed brief does not make an answer factual. Evidence still has to survive checking.

David and Moira. “Fold in the cheese” demonstrates an instruction that makes sense to the speaker but does not tell the doer what action to take. The useful mapping is ambiguity. The limit: an AI system is not David, does not experience confusion as a person does, and can produce a plausible result instead of exposing that the instruction was incomplete.

Use the draft without outsourcing the decision

  • Inspect it against the brief. A fluent answer may still miss the job, source or boundary.
  • Revise with evidence. Say what failed and point back to the relevant acceptance criterion instead of adding random prompt tricks.
  • Verify consequential material outside the answer. Names, dates, numbers, commitments, legal or policy claims and anything sent under your authority need the appropriate source or qualified reviewer.
  • Change tools only when the failure belongs to the tool. Missing context is a briefing problem; unavailable search, files, permissions or output modes may be a product problem.
