Jobs & Work

"Is AI replacing jobs right now?"

Solid but moving

Not wholesale — and "replacing" is the wrong word for what's happening. It's churn, not subtraction: hiring is contracting in some exposed roles while new work opens up in others, and nobody yet knows which way the balance tips. Most of the panic comes from looking at only one half of that.

What's solid — The contraction is real. Stanford's "Canaries in the Coal Mine" study found a 16% relative decline in employment for early-career workers (ages 22–25) in the most AI-exposed occupations since late 2022 — while experienced workers in those same roles held steady or grew. It shows up in employment rather than lower pay, and it held even after the researchers stripped out tech firms, remote-friendly roles, and company-wide shocks. But the creation side is just as real: the World Economic Forum projects 170 million new roles by 2030 against 92 million displaced — a net gain of 78 million, with roughly one in seven jobs being something that doesn't exist yet. And where AI augments people instead of automating their tasks, employment actually grew across every age group.

Where it's contested — The losses are already measured while the gains are still projected — so whether creation outpaces contraction, and how fast, is genuinely unknown. There's also real debate about whether the entry-level dip is even an AI effect, or partly a post-2022 over-hiring correction and higher interest rates working through the system.

What it depends on — For you specifically, it comes down to whether AI can do your core task or just speed you up — and whether you move toward the growing side instead of staying put in the shrinking one.

The myth to drop — Two of them, actually. "AI is causing mass layoffs" overstates it — this is a hiring slowdown in specific roles, not a wave of firings. But "AI creates more than it destroys, so relax" overstates it the other way — that's a projection, not a result yet.

The receipt — Stop asking "will AI take my job" and start asking "where's the new work, and am I moving toward it?" The same tools now let one person do what used to take a whole team — so building your own thing is on the table in a way it wasn't. The risk and the opportunity are the same force, and which one you get depends on where you point yourself. Cher had a closet computer in 1995; being early to the new work has always been the move.

"Which jobs are most affected?"

Solid but moving

If your job is mostly "take information from here and put it there," that's where AI reaches first. But the same shift is growing whole categories of other work — so it's a reshuffle, not just a cull.

What's solid — According to the WEF, the fastest-shrinking roles are clerical: data entry, admin and secretarial work, cashiers, bank tellers. The fastest-growing, proportionally, are AI and machine-learning specialists and data analysts — but it isn't only tech. Care work, teaching, the skilled trades, and green-energy jobs are all expanding at the same time.

Where it's contested — The open question is how fast the growing roles can absorb the people leaving the shrinking ones — retraining doesn't happen overnight, and the two don't always sit in the same place or pay the same.

What it depends on — How routine your core tasks are. The more your day is "move information from A to B," the more exposed you are; the more it leans on judgment, people, or working with your hands, the safer.

The myth to drop — That this is a blue-collar story, or a white-collar one. It cuts across both — sorted by the kind of task, not the colour of the collar.

The receipt — Audit your own week. The "move info from A to B" parts are the exposed parts — and the signal to shift your time toward the judgment and relationship work that's actually growing. That shift is exactly the move the Wednesday Bag has you practice.

"Will AI replace ME specifically?"

It depends

Probably not — and if you're genuinely good at what you do, AI is more likely to extend your reach than erase your job. What it changes is the shape of the work: it takes the rough draft and the grunt-work, you bring the judgment. But how much it helps you depends a lot on whether your work is routine or expert.

What's solid — AI's effect splits by the kind of work — and the picture shifted as better evidence came in. On routine, codifiable tasks (support scripts, basic drafting) it raises the floor: a 2023 study found the least experienced customer-service workers gained about 34% while veterans gained almost nothing. But on expert, high-value work, the newer studies point the other way. A 2026 analysis found experienced developers got far more real value from AI than entry-level ones; a Harvard study found AI helps people attempt new tasks but can't turn novices into experts; and across 83 medical studies, AI now matches non-expert clinicians while still trailing the real experts. At the frontier it's a ceiling-raiser, not a leveler — AlphaFold handed expert biologists protein structures that were out of reach, AI systems now hit gold-medal-level math, and tools like Google's "AI co-scientist" help researchers generate genuinely new hypotheses. None of that helped a beginner; it amplified people who were already excellent.

What's unknown — How it nets out by field over time is genuinely unsettled — and whether the value AI unlocks stays with experts or gets competed away as access spreads. A diagnostician's job and a data-entry job are heading in very different directions.

What it depends on — Whether your work is mostly codifiable (AI levels it, and the floor rises under you) or mostly expert judgment (AI amplifies it, and your ceiling rises) — and how well you direct and check it, which is exactly where real expertise pays off.

The myth to drop — "AI is the great equalizer — now juniors can do what seniors do." The newer evidence says it lets people attempt more, but it can't replace deep expertise; if anything, it rewards it. And stay suspicious of any clean "AI does your job X% as well as you" stat with no real study behind it.

The receipt — The deeper your actual expertise, the more AI amplifies you instead of replacing you — but only if you stay the one who briefs it, checks it, and owns the decision. Don't try to out-type the machine. Be the expert who wields it; that's the role getting more valuable, not less — and the wielding is exactly what the SLAiYER Handbook walks you through.

Environment

"How much water does AI actually use?"

It depends

Less per question than the scary headlines suggest, and a rounding error nationally — but a real problem in the specific towns where the data centres land. There's no single honest number here; there's a range, and a location.

What's solid — All the world's data centres together use something like 0.3 to 0.4% of US water; agriculture uses about 70%, and golf courses use more than data centres do. To make it concrete, all of Google's global water use is roughly equal to irrigating 4,000 acres of Arizona alfalfa — out of 369,000 acres of it grown in that one state.

Where it's contested — The famous "500ml per conversation" figure is shakier than it looks. It was measured per 10 to 50 GPT-3 answers, not per message, and it bundled in the water used to make the electricity; the authors themselves say it swings more than 30-fold by location and model. Modern models are 10 to 33 times leaner, so the "bottle per email" version is roughly 10 to 50 times too high.

What's unknown — Real per-facility numbers are largely missing — disclosure is thin and mostly self-reported.

What it depends on — Where the data centre sits (Nordic hydro versus Arizona desert), how it's cooled (closed-loop barely sips; evaporative drinks), and what you count — cooling only, or the electricity's water too.

The myth to drop — "Every email you send AI drinks a bottle of water." That's a stretched 2023 estimate for an old model, quoted per message when it was really per dozens.

The receipt — Measure AI's water by the watershed, not your inbox — and not by the national average either. About 40% of US data centres sit in high water-stress areas, and in a few towns one campus can draw a third of the municipal supply. Small everywhere, heavy somewhere — and a reminder to check the viral stat before you repeat it, which is rather the whole point of a receipts page.

"What's AI's carbon footprint?"

It depends

Small per question, and honestly fuzzy — nobody publishes the full numbers. And there are two sides to it: the data centres burn real power, but pointed at the right problems, AI also helps cut emissions.

What's solid — The everyday use of AI — inference — now drives most of its emissions, not the one-time training; Google says about 60% of its AI energy goes to inference. A single query is small, with central estimates around 2 to 3 grams of CO₂ (roughly ten times a Google search), though published numbers range widely. For a historical anchor: training GPT-3 emitted about 500 tonnes of CO₂ — the figure behind the old "five cars' lifetime" line, from 2019.

Where it's contested — No major provider publishes complete, verifiable per-query emissions, so every number out there is a reverse-engineered estimate built on different assumptions.

What it depends on — The grid the data centre runs on. The same question on hydro versus coal is a completely different footprint — David Rose would tell you the specifics are the whole answer here.

The myth to drop — Two opposite ones. "One prompt has a huge carbon cost" — it doesn't, per query. And "it's just software, so it's clean" — the data centres are very real. Worth holding the flip side too: aimed at climate work, AI could help cut 3.2 to 5.4 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year by 2035.

The receipt — Per-prompt guilt is a rounding error. What actually matters is what powers the data centre and whether clean energy keeps up with the buildout — and, on the other side, whether we point AI at the climate problems it can genuinely help solve.

"Is AI's energy use getting better or worse?"

Contested

Both at once — and that's a race, not a verdict. Every query keeps getting more efficient, while total energy use keeps climbing because we keep using so much more of it.

What's solid — Efficiency per query is improving fast, as models get leaner and chips get better. But total demand is surging at the same time — the IEA projects data-centre electricity roughly doubling, to around 1,000 TWh, by 2026.

Where it's contested — The real unknown is whether clean-energy buildout keeps pace, or whether the surge leans on natural gas and, in some places, revived coal.

What it depends on — A simple trap economists call the rebound effect — when each use gets cheaper, we do far more of it, and the extra use swamps the savings.

The myth to drop — "Efficiency gains mean the problem is solving itself." It's the opposite: cheaper per query is exactly why total demand explodes.

The receipt — Your individual efficiency isn't the lever. The real question is total demand and what powers it — grid, siting, clean energy. That's a policy fight, not a guilt trip.

"Are data centres raising my electricity bill?"

Contested

The viral "your bill went up 267%" number is junk. But "data centres don't affect my bill" is the other lie. The truth is regional, real, and still being fought over.

What's solid — The watchdog that monitors the biggest US grid named data-centre demand the main reason capacity prices hit records — up 174% for 2025–26 — and residential bills rose hard in the states carrying the most data-centre load, like Virginia, Maryland, and New York.

Where it's contested — Whether that pressure actually lands on ordinary residents is genuinely disputed. Some studies say there's no clear cost-shift — but the loudest "no impact" ones were funded by Amazon and by the utility trade group, so they can't be the page's backbone.

What it depends on — Rate design and who pays for the new power lines — some states are now making data centres their own rate class so the costs don't simply spread to everyone.

The myth to drop — "If you live near a data centre, your bill jumped 267%." Real number, wrong meaning — it's a wholesale price at specific points on the grid, not your household bill, and wholesale is only 30 to 50% of what you pay. Fact-checkers rated the consumer-bill version mostly false.

The receipt — The scary number is wrong; the calm "nothing to see here" is also wrong — and watch who's funding that one. If a data centre is coming to your grid, the real question is "who's on the hook for the upgrades," and that's something you can ask loudly at a utility hearing — Elle Woods walking in with the rate filing tabbed and highlighted.

"Is a data centre good or bad for my town?"

Contested

Not just extraction. A data centre can bring a community real money — sometimes life-changing money — but the size of the win depends almost entirely on the deal that gets struck, and a lot of it is temporary.

What's solid — The windfalls are real. In rural Richland Parish, Louisiana, tax revenue from Meta's data-centre construction pushed teacher bonuses from about $10,000 to roughly $50,000 — near a full year's pay for some — plus about $17,000 for support staff. One Meta tax payment was $22.4 million to a parish that used to collect around $21 million in total.

Where it's contested — Most of that bump is construction-era tax. Once the centre is built it recurs at a fraction, and a finished data centre runs on relatively few permanent staff — Meta committed to about 500. The tax breaks are large, too: a 20-year sales-tax exemption and an 80% property-tax abatement for decades, so the long-run contribution is well below face value. And real costs ride along — home prices spiked, and the region is adding gas plants to feed the power draw.

What it depends on — The negotiated deal — community benefit agreements, the size of the abatement, the job and infrastructure commitments, and whether there are clawbacks if the company misses its targets.

The myth to drop — Both halves of the argument. "Data centres take everything and give nothing" — Richland's teachers are the receipt against that. And "a data centre is a jackpot for any town" — strip out the abatement and the one-time construction bump and the math thins fast.

The receipt — A data centre isn't automatically good or bad for a town — it's a negotiation. The communities that win treat a $20-billion neighbour like the high-stakes counterparty it is: real benefit agreements, real commitments, clawbacks in writing. Miranda Priestly wouldn't accept the first offer, and neither should your town council. The leverage is realest before the deal is signed.

Privacy & Trust

"Is my data being used to train AI?"

Settled

Probably yes — and paying doesn't buy you out the way you'd think. The line that matters isn't free versus paid; it's consumer versus business.

What's solid — On ChatGPT, the Free, Plus, and Pro tiers all train on your chats by default — you opt out under Settings → Data Controls, and only Team, Enterprise, and Edu are out automatically. On Claude, since late 2025, Free, Pro, and Max train by default unless you opt out, and saying yes stretches how long they keep your data to five years; only Team, Enterprise, and the API are exempt. On Gemini, a personal Google account is treated as consumer data — with human reviewers seeing samples unless you turn off Gemini Apps Activity — while a Workspace or business account isn't used for training.

Where it's contested — Opting out isn't a force field. A thumbs-up or thumbs-down can pull a single chat back into review, and Anthropic's policy lets safety-flagged conversations be used even if you opted out — without defining what trips the flag. "Delete" is slow, too: deleted chats can linger about 30 days, and anything already used in a finished training run can't be pulled back.

What it depends on — Which tool, which tier, whether you've actually flipped the setting, and whether you used a temporary chat.

The myth to drop — "I pay for Pro, so my stuff is private." Paying for a consumer plan buys you features, not privacy — and when these settings rolled out, the share-my-data toggle was pre-checked under a big Accept button, so a lot of people opted in just by clicking through.

The receipt — The good news is you have an off switch, and it takes two minutes. Keep genuinely confidential things — client data, your salary, the closed-door conversation — out of consumer chatbots, even paid ones; use a business or enterprise account, a temporary chat, or your own head. This is the Account Rule from Chapter 5 of the Handbook, and it's the one Deb famously didn't follow. Then go flip the toggle: Data Controls on ChatGPT, Privacy Settings on Claude, Gemini Apps Activity on Gemini.

"How often does AI make stuff up?"

It depends

It makes things up — but less, and less randomly, than the panic suggests. How often depends entirely on what you ask: hand it a document to summarize and the top models are very reliable; ask it to recall a fact from memory and it's much shakier.

What's solid — For grounded tasks — summarizing something you've actually given it — the best models stay low, around 1 to 3% on the standard benchmarks, and turning on web search or handing it the source cuts the made-up answers sharply. In one test, a model was wrong about 47% of the time answering from memory alone, but only about 10% with web access on.

Where it's contested — Strangely, the "smarter" reasoning models can make more up, not less, on harder tasks — they over-think and drift away from the source rather than sticking to what's in front of them.

What it depends on — Whether it's working from a source you gave it (reliable) or from memory (riskier), and whether web search is switched on.

The myth to drop — Two of them. "Newer models basically fixed this" — they didn't. And "AI is basically always making things up" — also wrong; it's situational, and very low for grounded work.

The receipt — Treat it like a brilliant, fast, slightly-too-confident intern — fantastic first draft, never the final word. It's Regina George energy: sounds completely sure, occasionally just inventing things. The fix isn't fear, it's habit: web search on, ask for sources, click them, and verify anything that actually matters. "When is AI wrong — and when is it just ahead of us?"

It depends

Sometimes what looks like a mistake is the AI finding something true that we simply hadn't thought of. The catch is that you can't tell genius from garbage by how it feels — only by checking.

What's solid — It has genuinely happened. AlphaGo's famous "Move 37" was called a blunder by every expert watching — until it won the game and rewrote Go strategy. AI systems have since produced novel math proofs and protein structures that looked wrong until someone verified them and found they held. Those weren't hallucinations; they were correct, just unfamiliar.

Where it's contested — This kind of magic mostly happens where there's a hard answer key — a Go board, a math proof, a protein fold — where truth can be checked automatically. Your email draft has no such key. And the very same trait that produced Move 37, going where no human would, is exactly what produces a convincing fake citation.

What it depends on — Whether there's a way to check the answer. In a domain with a clear right answer, a surprising AI move might be brilliant; in an open-ended one, surprising is usually just wrong.

The myth to drop — Both directions. "If the AI did something weird, it must be a mistake" — not always; sometimes it's ahead of you. And "maybe my chatbot's weird answer is secretly genius" — almost never, if there's no way to verify it.

The receipt — Surprising isn't the same as wrong — but you find out which by checking, not by being impressed. This is the real reason to verify everything: not because AI is dumb, but because brilliant and broken look exactly alike until you do the work.

Economy

"Is AI actually making companies more productive?"

Contested

Two true things at once: individual people often get real, large gains from AI — and most companies haven't turned that into results. The gap between "this saved me an hour" and "this moved our numbers" is where the money's getting lost, and it's almost never the technology's fault.

What's solid — At the individual level the gains are real, sometimes huge — "super-users" report up to 5× on their own work, and task-level studies show solid boosts (support reps up about 14%, developers faster on routine code). But at the company level, only about 29% of organizations see meaningful ROI; MIT found roughly 95% of enterprise pilots delivered no measurable P&L impact; and RAND put the share of projects failing to deliver business value near 80%. The reason is remarkably consistent: about 70% of the value comes from people, process, and training — and only around 37% of companies invested in any of it. Most just bolted AI onto old workflows and bought licences, and the result is "shelfware" — boards are now having uncomfortable conversations about what those ten-thousand-seat Copilot deals actually bought.

Where it's contested — A controlled trial found experienced developers were 19% slower with AI on their own codebases while feeling 20% faster. So even the individual gains need a stopwatch, not a vibe.

What it depends on — Almost entirely on implementation — whether the workflow got redesigned, whether people were actually trained, and whether anyone defined what success looks like. As the research puts it, AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there.

The myth to drop — Two opposite ones. "Buy the licences and productivity appears" — that's how you get shelfware. And "AI doesn't actually help anyone" — it helps a trained person enormously; it's the untrained rollout that flops.

The receipt — The technology mostly works; the rollout mostly doesn't. The difference between the company that wasted millions and the one that didn't is whether its people were taught to actually use the thing — which, not for nothing, is the entire premise of being here. Be the trained super-user, not the unused seat.

"Who's actually benefiting financially?"

Solid but moving

Right now the money is piled in two places: the companies selling the shovels, and the people who actually know how to dig. The "rising tide lifts all boats" version hasn't arrived — but the cost of building your own boat just collapsed.

What's solid — The clearest winners are upstream, in the infrastructure. Nvidia made about $58 billion in profit in a single quarter of 2026 — more than Intel's entire year — and captures roughly 90% of AI-chip spending; the chipmakers and clouds behind it (TSMC, Broadcom, AWS, Azure) are printing money selling shovels in a gold rush. Meanwhile broad worker gains haven't shown: median wages are flat, and some of that upstream profit is funded by tech layoffs — over 100,000 in this wave. But here's the genuinely new thing: tiny teams now reach enormous scale. One AI coding company hit about $2 billion in revenue with roughly 50 people; an image studio reached about $500 million with 40 and no outside funding. The floor under "what one person or a small team can build" has dropped through the basement.

Where it's contested — Whether the rest of the economy ever catches up. About 88% of companies have adopted AI but only around 6% see real returns, and AI services earn a small fraction of what's being spent on the infrastructure — which is why serious analysts are openly using the word "bubble." Industrial build-out that pays off, or a capex bonfire? Genuinely unsettled.

What it depends on — Where you sit. Selling shovels: winning. Building a focused thing with a tiny team: newly possible. Waiting for the rising tide to lift your salary: still waiting.

The myth to drop — "AI is already making everyone richer." It's making a concentrated few much richer — and for some workers it funded their own layoff. The honest version: the money is upstream and with the people who can wield the tools.

The receipt — You're probably not going to out-earn Nvidia. But the collapsed cost of building is the part that's actually yours: the thing you couldn't afford to build last year — the team, the budget, the tech — might be a weekend now. That's the founder story this whole site is built on.

Learning & Skills

"Is AI making us dumber?"

It depends

It can — if you let it think for you. Used the other way, it can sharpen you. The early evidence is real, and it's the same story as calculators and GPS, just turned up louder.

What's solid — On the risk side, an MIT brain-scan study in 2025 found that people using ChatGPT to write showed lower brain engagement and weaker memory of their own work; a separate study of 666 people tied heavy AI use to lower critical-thinking scores; and in one clinical study, doctors' unaided skill at spotting tumours dropped 6% after a few months of AI assistance. But the other half is just as real: guided, active use can strengthen thinking — in that same MIT study, people who did the work themselves first and then used AI did better. And offloading isn't new; the same panic greeted the calculator, GPS, and Google.

Where it's contested — The headline study is small (54 people) and still a preprint, so it's a signal, not a verdict.

What it depends on — Whether you use AI to skip the thinking or to push it further.

The myth to drop — Both extremes. "AI rots your brain" is too strong for the evidence we have — and "it's totally fine" is also wrong, because the skill-fade is measurable.

The receipt — Use it to extend your thinking, not replace it. Draft with it, argue with it — then do the actual reasoning yourself on the things you want to stay sharp at. Keep some work unmediated; that's the rep that keeps the muscle. It's why the Wednesday Bag asks you to try the move yourself, not just nod along to it.

"What skills should I actually learn?"

Solid but moving

Four things — and "learn to code" isn't necessarily one of them. The ability to evaluate and direct AI now matters more than the ability to build it.

What's solid — The durable four are prompting (talking to AI well), evaluation (telling good output from garbage), workflow design (knowing where AI actually fits your process), and your own domain expertise (the thing that makes you the expert). The WEF's list of top skills for 2030 backs this up — analytical and creative thinking, AI and data literacy, curiosity and lifelong learning, resilience — and notice that "coding from scratch" isn't on it.

What it depends on — Your field, but the meta-skill is universal: learning to learn quickly, because the tools turn over roughly every six months.

The myth to drop — "You have to learn to code to stay relevant." For most people, directing and checking AI matters more than writing the code yourself.

The receipt — Stop trying to memorize tools that'll change by autumn. Get good at the four above, and at picking things up fast — that's the skill set that survives every model release. It's also, more or less, the entire syllabus of the SLAiYER Handbook.
