Jobs & Work

"Is AI replacing jobs right now?"

Confidence: Solid but moving

Not wholesale — and "replacing" is the wrong word for what's happening. It's churn, not subtraction: hiring is contracting in some exposed roles while new work opens up in others, and nobody yet knows which way the balance tips. Most of the panic comes from looking at only one half of that.

What's solid — The contraction is real but concentrated. Stanford's August 2026 update found a 19% relative employment shortfall, as of June 2026, for workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations compared with less-exposed work. The gap appeared mainly through reduced hiring rather than unusual separations, did not appear comparably among experienced workers, and remained after several robustness checks. The study is descriptive, not proof that AI caused the entire change. Separately, the World Economic Forum projects 170 million roles created and 92 million displaced by 2030 across five major structural trends — including AI, but not AI alone — for a projected net gain of 78 million. The losses are observed; the global gains remain forecasts.

Where it's contested — The losses are already measured while the gains are still projected — so whether creation outpaces contraction, and how fast, is genuinely unknown. There's also real debate about whether the entry-level dip is even an AI effect, or partly a post-2022 over-hiring correction and higher interest rates working through the system.

What it depends on — For you specifically, it comes down to whether AI can do your core task or just speed you up — and whether you move toward the growing side instead of staying put in the shrinking one.

The myth to drop — Two of them, actually. "AI is causing mass layoffs" overstates it — this is a hiring slowdown in specific roles, not a wave of firings. But "AI creates more than it destroys, so relax" overstates it the other way — that's a projection, not a result yet.

The receipt — Stop asking "will AI take my job" and start asking "where's the new work, and am I moving toward it?" The same tools now let one person do what used to take a whole team — so building your own thing is on the table in a way it wasn't. The risk and the opportunity are the same force, and which one you get depends on where you point yourself. Cher had a closet computer in 1995; being early to the new work has always been the move.

"Which jobs are most affected?"

Confidence: Solid but moving

If your job is mostly "take information from here and put it there," that's where AI reaches first. But the same shift is growing whole categories of other work — so it's a reshuffle, not just a cull.

What's solid — According to the WEF, the fastest-shrinking roles are clerical: data entry, admin and secretarial work, cashiers, bank tellers. The fastest-growing, proportionally, are AI and machine-learning specialists and data analysts — but it isn't only tech. Care work, teaching, the skilled trades, and green-energy jobs are all expanding at the same time.

Where it's contested — The open question is how fast the growing roles can absorb the people leaving the shrinking ones — retraining doesn't happen overnight, and the two don't always sit in the same place or pay the same.

What it depends on — How routine your core tasks are. The more your day is "move information from A to B," the more exposed you are; work that leans on judgment, relationships or physical activity is generally less exposed to current generative AI. Less exposed is not the same as safe or guaranteed to grow.

The myth to drop — That this is a blue-collar story, or a white-collar one. It cuts across both — sorted by the kind of task, not the colour of the collar.

The receipt — Audit your own week. The "move info from A to B" parts are the exposed parts — and the signal to shift your time toward the judgment and relationship work that's actually growing. That shift is exactly the move the Wednesday Bag has you practice.

"Will AI replace ME specifically?"

Confidence: It depends

Probably not — and if you're genuinely good at what you do, AI is more likely to extend your reach than erase your job. What it changes is the shape of the work: it takes the rough draft and the grunt-work, you bring the judgment. But how much it helps you depends a lot on whether your work is routine or expert.

What's solid — The effect depends on the task. In customer support, AI raised productivity most for newer and lower-skilled workers — about 34% in one study — while experienced workers gained little. A newer study of public-software developers found larger measurable gains among developers with more GitHub experience, while Harvard research found that AI can help adjacent outsiders attempt unfamiliar work without turning distant novices into experts. Across 83 medical studies, large language models performed similarly to non-expert physicians on average but remained about 15.8 percentage points behind experts, with substantial variation and study-quality caveats. The honest conclusion is not that AI always raises the floor or always raises the ceiling: it can do either, depending on the task, tool and worker.

What's unknown — How it nets out by field over time is genuinely unsettled — and whether the value AI unlocks stays with experts or gets competed away as access spreads. A diagnostician's job and a data-entry job are heading in very different directions.

What it depends on — Whether your work is mostly codifiable (AI levels it, and the floor rises under you) or mostly expert judgment (AI amplifies it, and your ceiling rises) — and how well you direct and check it, which is exactly where real expertise pays off.

The myth to drop — "AI is the great equalizer — now juniors can do what seniors do." The newer evidence says it lets people attempt more, but it can't replace deep expertise; if anything, it rewards it. And stay suspicious of any clean "AI does your job X% as well as you" stat with no real study behind it.

The receipt — Deep expertise gives you more ability to brief, test and judge the system — but do not assume that guarantees a gain. Measure the real task. Don't try to out-type the machine; be the expert who can tell when it helped and when it did not. Working with AI 101 teaches that practical loop.

Environment

"How much water does AI actually use?"

Confidence: It depends

There is no single honest number per question. National shares can look small while the local burden is serious in a water-stressed watershed.

What's solid — One estimate placed US data-centre consumption at roughly 0.3 to 0.4% of US water withdrawals in 2021. That is not a figure for all of the world's data centres, and comparisons with agriculture or golf courses need the same geography and denominator to mean anything.

Where it's contested — The famous "500ml per conversation" figure covered roughly 10 to 50 medium-length GPT-3 responses, not one message, and included water used to generate electricity. The authors found large variation by model, location and time. Google measured a median Gemini text prompt in May 2025 at 0.26ml and reported large year-over-year efficiency gains for that service, but that cannot be generalized to every model or workload.

What's unknown — Real per-facility numbers are largely missing — disclosure is thin and mostly self-reported.

What it depends on — Where the data centre sits (Nordic hydro versus Arizona desert), how it's cooled (closed-loop barely sips; evaporative drinks), and what you count — cooling only, or the electricity's water too.

The myth to drop — "Every email you send AI drinks a bottle of water." That's a stretched 2023 estimate for an old model, quoted per message when it was really per dozens.

The receipt — Measure AI's water by provider, facility and watershed, not by a viral bottle-per-prompt claim or a national average. Small nationally can still mean heavy locally.

"What's AI's carbon footprint?"

Confidence: It depends

There is no universal number per question. The result changes with model size, response length, hardware, data-centre overhead and the electricity grid.

What's solid — Google measured a median Gemini text prompt in May 2025 at 0.24Wh and 0.03g CO₂e under its own system boundaries; that is a useful product-specific measurement, not an industry constant. For historical scale, researchers estimated that one 2020 GPT-3 training run emitted about 552 tonnes of CO₂e including data-centre overhead. The IEA estimates all data centres produced about 180Mt of indirect CO₂ emissions in 2024 and projects about 320Mt in 2030 in its base case.

Where it's contested — No major provider publishes complete, verifiable per-query emissions, so every number out there is a reverse-engineered estimate built on different assumptions.

What it depends on — The grid the data centre runs on. The same question on hydro versus coal is a completely different footprint — David Rose would tell you the specifics are the whole answer here.

The myth to drop — Two opposite ones. One product's prompt measurement is not a universal footprint, and "it's just software, so it's clean" ignores the physical system. The IEA estimates widespread use of existing AI applications could help avoid roughly 1.4Gt in 2035, but says current momentum does not guarantee that outcome and rebound effects could reduce it.

The receipt — Per-prompt guilt is a rounding error. What actually matters is what powers the data centre and whether clean energy keeps up with the buildout — and, on the other side, whether we point AI at the climate problems it can genuinely help solve.

"Is AI's energy use getting better or worse?"

Confidence: Contested

Both at once — and that's a race, not a verdict. Every query keeps getting more efficient, while total energy use keeps climbing because we keep using so much more of it.

What's solid — Efficiency per task is improving, but total demand is growing faster. The IEA estimates that data centres used about 415TWh in 2024 and projects roughly 945TWh by 2030 in its base case — moving from about 1.5% to just under 3% of global electricity use.

Where it's contested — The real unknown is whether clean-energy buildout keeps pace, or whether the surge leans on natural gas and, in some places, revived coal.

What it depends on — Cheaper computation can encourage more use, but rebound is one contributor, not a complete explanation.

The myth to drop — "Efficiency gains mean the problem is solving itself." Per-task efficiency and total demand are different measures; one can improve while the other rises.

The receipt — Your individual efficiency isn't the lever. The real question is total demand and what powers it — grid, siting, clean energy. That's a policy fight, not a guilt trip.

"Are data centres raising my electricity bill?"

Confidence: Contested

The viral numbers often confuse wholesale-market metrics with a household bill. But "data centres do not affect my bill" is too calm. The truth is regional, real and still being fought over.

What's solid — PJM's independent market monitor estimated that data-centre load alone increased 2025/26 capacity-auction revenues by $9.332 billion, or 174.3%. That was not a 174% increase in consumer prices. Residential prices have also risen in several high-load states, but data centres are not the only cause.

Where it's contested — Whether that pressure actually lands on ordinary residents is genuinely disputed. Some studies say there's no clear cost-shift — but the loudest "no impact" ones were funded by Amazon and by the utility trade group, so they can't be the page's backbone.

What it depends on — Rate design, generation and transmission costs, and whether regulators create separate large-load classes or protections against cost shifting.

The myth to drop — "If you live near a data centre, your bill jumped 267%." A market metric does not translate one-for-one into a household bill.

The receipt — The scary number is wrong; the calm "nothing to see here" is also wrong — and watch who's funding that one. If a data centre is coming to your grid, the real question is "who's on the hook for the upgrades," and that's something you can ask loudly at a utility hearing — Elle Woods walking in with the rate filing tabbed and highlighted.

"Is a data centre good or bad for my town?"

Confidence: Contested

Not just extraction. A data centre can bring a community real money — sometimes life-changing money — but the size of the win depends almost entirely on the deal that gets struck, and a lot of it is temporary.

What's solid — The Richland Parish windfall is real: construction-linked tax revenue from Meta's project helped raise certified-teacher bonuses from about $10,200 to $50,935 and classified-staff bonuses from about $3,323 to $17,472.

Where it's contested — Construction revenue is not the same as permanent recurring revenue. The project has also changed materially since this answer was first written: Louisiana Economic Development said in July 2026 that planned investment now exceeds $50 billion, with commitments for 1,000 operational jobs, 7,500 construction jobs, 5GW of power and $1 billion in local infrastructure. The long-run result will depend on the final tax, infrastructure and community-benefit agreements.

What it depends on — The negotiated deal — community benefit agreements, the size of the abatement, the job and infrastructure commitments, and whether there are clawbacks if the company misses its targets.

The myth to drop — Both halves of the argument. "Data centres take everything and give nothing" — Richland's teachers are the receipt against that. And "a data centre is a jackpot for any town" — strip out the abatement and the one-time construction bump and the math thins fast.

The receipt — A data centre isn't automatically good or bad for a town — it's a negotiation. The communities that win treat a $50-billion neighbour like the high-stakes counterparty it is: real benefit agreements, real commitments and clawbacks in writing. Miranda Priestly wouldn't accept the first offer, and neither should your town council. The leverage is realest before the deal is signed.

Privacy & Trust

"Is my data being used to train AI?"

Confidence: Solid but moving

Maybe — and paying for a consumer plan does not by itself answer the question. Consumer and business products use different rules, and the exact product and setting matter.

What's solid — Anthropic says consumer users choose whether new or resumed chats may be used for model improvement; enabling that setting can extend retention to five years, while commercial products are excluded. Google says saved activity from personal Gemini use may be reviewed by humans and used to improve its services; qualifying Workspace editions are not used to train generative AI outside the customer's domain without permission. OpenAI says API inputs and outputs are not used to train its models by default. For ChatGPT personal plans, check the current Data Controls in the exact account rather than inferring the answer from price.

Where it's contested — Opting out is not a force field. Feedback, safety review, retention and deletion exceptions vary by provider, product and setting, so the current policy is the evidence—not a remembered default.

What it depends on — Which tool, which tier, whether you've actually flipped the setting, and whether you used a temporary chat.

The myth to drop — "I pay for Pro, so my stuff is private." Paying for a consumer plan buys features, not one universal privacy contract.

The receipt — Keep genuinely confidential things — client data, your salary, the closed-door conversation — out of consumer chatbots, even paid ones. Check the current controls in the exact product you use, and use an approved business product or a safer workflow when the information is sensitive.

"How often does AI make stuff up?"

Confidence: It depends

It makes things up — but less, and less randomly, than the panic suggests. How often depends entirely on what you ask: hand it a document to summarize and the top models are very reliable; ask it to recall a fact from memory and it's much shakier.

What's solid — There is no honest single hallucination rate. In Vectara's narrow short-document summarization benchmark, current leaders score roughly 2 to 3%, while broader grounded and knowledge-boundary evaluations produce much higher error rates. Search and supplied sources generally improve performance on retrieval tasks, but neither guarantees correctness.

Where it's contested — Benchmarks measure different jobs under different conditions. A low rate on short-document summarization is not the chance that your next answer will be wrong.

What it depends on — The task, model, source quality, retrieval method and how the result is checked. A supplied source makes an answer easier to verify; it does not automatically make it reliable.

The myth to drop — Two of them. "Newer models basically fixed this" — they didn't. And "AI is basically always making things up" — also wrong; it's situational, and very low for grounded work.

The receipt — Treat it like a brilliant, fast, slightly-too-confident intern — fantastic first draft, never the final word. It's Regina George energy: sounds completely sure, occasionally just inventing things. The fix isn't fear, it's habit: web search on, ask for sources, click them, and verify anything that actually matters. "When is AI wrong — and when is it just ahead of us?"

Confidence: It depends

Sometimes what looks like a mistake is the AI finding something true that we simply hadn't thought of. The catch is that you can't tell genius from garbage by how it feels — only by checking.

What's solid — It has genuinely happened in domains with hard checks. Professional commentators initially thought AlphaGo's Move 37 was a mistake; it proved pivotal and influenced later Go strategy. AlphaTensor found novel matrix-multiplication algorithms that could be formally checked, while AlphaFold's protein-structure prediction work was independently recognized by the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. These examples matter because their outputs could be tested.

Where it's contested — This kind of magic mostly happens where there's a hard answer key — a Go board, a math proof, a protein fold — where truth can be checked automatically. Your email draft has no such key. And the very same trait that produced Move 37, going where no human would, is exactly what produces a convincing fake citation.

What it depends on — Whether there's a way to check the answer. In a domain with a clear right answer, a surprising AI move might be brilliant; in an open-ended one, surprising is usually just wrong.

The myth to drop — Both directions. "If the AI did something weird, it must be a mistake" — not always; sometimes it's ahead of you. And "maybe my chatbot's weird answer is secretly genius" — almost never, if there's no way to verify it.

The receipt — Surprising isn't the same as wrong — but you find out which by checking, not by being impressed. This is the real reason to verify everything: not because AI is dumb, but because brilliant and broken look exactly alike until you do the work. (The holy-it's-not-just-search side of this gets its own episode later in the season — "Ask Jeeves Could Never.")

Economy

"Is AI actually making companies more productive?"

Confidence: Contested

Two true things at once: individual people often get real, large gains from AI — and most companies haven't turned that into results. The gap between "this saved me an hour" and "this moved our numbers" is where the money's getting lost, and it's almost never the technology's fault.

What's solid — Controlled studies show gains on some tasks: customer-support agents resolved 14% more issues per hour on average, while effects varied sharply by worker and task. At company level, adoption is much wider than measured bottom-line impact. McKinsey's 2025 survey found 88% reporting regular AI use in at least one function, but only 39% reported any enterprise EBIT impact and most of those put it below 5%; about 6% met its "AI high performer" threshold. Deloitte's 2026 survey found 37% still using AI at a surface level with little or no workflow change.

KPMG's Q2 2026 survey adds a current marker for that gap: only 7% of more than 2,000 business leaders reported established ROI. Organisations reporting clear CEO accountability for AI-informed decisions reported it more often (14% versus 4%). That is a self-reported association, not proof that appointing a CEO owner causes ROI — but it reinforces the point that deployment alone is not implementation.

Where it's contested — A 2025 randomized trial found experienced open-source developers completed selected tasks 19% more slowly with early-2025 AI tools despite expecting to be faster, but METR's 2026 follow-up says newer tools may be changing the result. Even individual gains need a stopwatch, not a vibe.

What it depends on — Almost entirely on implementation — whether the workflow got redesigned, whether people were actually trained, and whether anyone defined what success looks like. As the research puts it, AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there.

The myth to drop — Two opposite ones. "Buy the licences and productivity appears" — that's how you get shelfware. And "AI doesn't actually help anyone" — it can help a trained person, but the gain must be measured.

The receipt — The technology mostly works; the rollout mostly doesn't. The difference between the company that wasted millions and the one that didn't is whether its people were taught to actually use the thing — which, not for nothing, is the entire premise of being here. Be the trained super-user, not the unused seat.

"Who's actually benefiting financially?"

Confidence: Solid but moving

Right now the money is piled in two places: the companies selling the shovels, and the people who actually know how to dig. The "rising tide lifts all boats" version hasn't arrived — but the cost of building your own boat just collapsed.

What's solid — Infrastructure suppliers are the clearest measured winners: Nvidia reported $58.3 billion in GAAP net income on $81.6 billion in revenue in Q1 FY2027. AI software can also scale unusually quickly — Cursor said it crossed $1 billion in annualized revenue with a team of more than 300 in November 2025, and its press page reported $2 billion by March 2026 — but it is not a 50-person company.

Where it's contested — Whether the rest of the economy catches up. McKinsey found 88% of respondents report some regular organizational AI use, while only about 6% met its high-performer definition. Industrial build-out that pays off, or a capex bonfire? Genuinely unsettled.

What it depends on — Where you sit. Selling shovels: winning. Building a focused thing with a tiny team: newly possible. Waiting for the rising tide to lift your salary: still waiting.

The myth to drop — "AI is already making everyone richer." The clearest measured gains are still concentrated upstream and among a small set of high-performing adopters.

The receipt — You're probably not going to out-earn Nvidia. But the collapsed cost of building is the part that's actually yours: the thing you couldn't afford to build last year — the team, the budget, the tech — might be a weekend now. That's the founder story this whole site is built on.

Learning & Skills

"Is AI making us dumber?"

Confidence: It depends

It can — if you let it think for you. Used the other way, it can sharpen you. The early evidence is real, and it's the same story as calculators and GPS, just turned up louder.

What's solid — Early studies raise a real caution, not a verdict. In a 54-person preprint on essay writing, the LLM group showed weaker EEG connectivity and poorer recall; only 18 participants completed the crossover session, where the brain-first group retained stronger recall after gaining AI. A 666-person study found an association between frequent AI use, cognitive offloading and lower critical-thinking scores, but its design cannot establish causation. In an observational colonoscopy study, clinicians' unaided adenoma-detection rate fell from 28.4% to 22.4% after routine AI exposure; the authors explicitly warn that confounding remains possible.

Where it's contested — The headline study is small (54 people) and still a preprint, so it's a signal, not a verdict.

What it depends on — Whether you use AI to skip the thinking or to push it further.

The myth to drop — Both extremes. "AI rots your brain" is too strong for the evidence we have — and "it's totally fine" is also wrong, because the skill-fade is measurable.

The receipt — Use it to extend your thinking, not replace it. Draft with it, argue with it — then do the actual reasoning yourself on the things you want to stay sharp at. Keep some work unmediated; that's the rep that keeps the muscle. It's why the Wednesday Bag asks you to try the move yourself, not just nod along to it.

"What skills should I actually learn?"

Confidence: Solid but moving

Four things — and "learn to code" isn't necessarily one of them. The ability to evaluate and direct AI now matters more than the ability to build it.

What's solid — Our practical four are briefing AI clearly, evaluating its output, redesigning a workflow around it, and deepening the domain expertise needed to judge it. That is LAiDIES' synthesis, not a WEF ranking. WEF's 2025 employer survey separately ranks analytical thinking as the top core skill and AI and big-data literacy, cybersecurity and technological literacy among the fastest-growing, alongside creative thinking, resilience and lifelong learning.

What it depends on — Your field, but the meta-skill is learning to learn quickly as tools and workflows change.

The myth to drop — "You have to learn to code to stay relevant." For most people, directing and checking AI matters more than writing the code yourself.

The receipt — Stop trying to memorize every tool. Get good at the four above, and at picking things up fast — that's the skill set designed to survive model releases. It is also the practical syllabus of Working with AI 101.
