How to Check AI’s Work

You do not need to redo every word. You need to find the parts that could change a decision, then check those properly.

An AI turns meeting notes into a polished client update: “The client approved the revised launch date, expects a 20% lift and wants paid social added to phase one.” The notes actually say the client will review the date on Friday, somebody guessed “maybe 20%,” and paid social was only a question.

Which part deserves attention first?

Chapter 1 of 7

Sort the output

Fluency is presentation. Your first job is to identify what kind of thing each line is and what could change a decision.

Four useful buckets

A draft is proposed wording, structure or creative material. A factual claim says something checkable about the world. An assumption or inference connects facts without being directly stated. A recommendation proposes action and may depend on both facts and values.

A sentence can contain more than one bucket. Split compound statements before you search so one true clause cannot lend credibility to a false neighbour.

  • Draft: “Here is a warmer opening for the email.”
  • Claim: “The contract renews on 1 September.”
  • Inference: “The delay probably means the client is unhappy.”
  • Recommendation: “Move the campaign to September.”

Turn the stakes dial

Ask what happens if the statement is wrong, how reversible the action is, how uncertain the evidence is and how quickly the answer can change. A playlist caption and a medication decision do not deserve the same checking plan.

Check decision-changing claims first: money, deadlines, permissions, safety, public promises, people’s rights or anything hard to undo.

Classify all eight lines before checking your reasoning.

Proposed wording is draft material.

A date in a contract is externally checkable.

The sentence interprets the delay; the client’s state is not directly established.

This proposes action.

A current plan limit is a checkable provider claim.

“Best” is a decision that depends on requirements and values.

Approval is a material, checkable commitment.

This is an interpretation, not a source-established fact.

Chapter 2 of 7

Know what the tool actually did

Search, grounding, citations and provenance can help. None of them is the same thing as verification.

Six different jobs

Generation
Creates the answer. Smooth language is not evidence.
Retrieval or search
Finds material. Finding a page does not make it the right page.
Grounding
Connects or constrains an answer to supplied or retrieved material. The material can still be weak, stale, out of scope or misread.
Citation
Points to a source. The source must exist, open and support the nearby claim.
Provenance
Records an asset’s origin or change history. It can help authenticate a trail without proving that the depicted event or assertion is true.
Verification
Connects a specific claim to adequate evidence under the right scope, date and stakes, then makes a qualified decision.

The chain

A source may be retrieved, summarized by a model and represented by a citation. The human check still asks whether the cited source is the right source and whether its exact content supports the exact claim.

The same model asking itself “are you sure?” may expose inconsistency, but it is not independent evidence. Independence comes from the underlying authoritative record, a qualified owner or a genuinely separate evidence path.

Match five artifacts to what they establish.

Finding material is retrieval; relevance and quality still need inspection.

The model is grounded in the document; the document and interpretation can still be wrong or out of scope.

The link is a pointer, not a support verdict.

The trail can be tamper-evident without proving the depicted event.

That is the claim-to-evidence decision process.

Chapter 3 of 7

Frame the claim before searching

A vague question retrieves vague evidence. Make the statement small enough to test.

Write the claim you actually need

Name the product or person, plan or version, place or jurisdiction, date, conditions, denominator and intended decision. Preserve qualifiers such as “may,” “in this sample” and “for eligible accounts.”

“She doesn’t even go here” is a useful scope reminder: evidence about a different plan, country or version may be real and still not apply. The film reference is only a mnemonic; it does not test the evidence for you.

  • Vague: “It is 50% faster.”
  • Checkable: “In the named study, people completing which task, compared with what baseline, finished 50% faster under which conditions?”
  • Vague: “The plan is free.”
  • Checkable: “Is this feature included in the provider’s free plan for Canadian individual accounts on 25 July 2026?”
Choose the repair that makes each claim checkable.

The repair exposes the denominator, comparison and method.

Plan, account, region and date determine applicability.

Compound claims need separate evidence and verdicts.

Chapter 4 of 7

Choose and inspect evidence

There is no universal source ladder. Match evidence to the claim, then inspect what it actually says.

Claim-matched evidence

Claim typeBest starting evidenceNever sufficient alone
Current feature, price, plan or policyCurrent official documentation, account UI or contract; record region, plan and access dateAI memory, search snippet or undated screenshot
Law, regulation or public ruleCurrent legislation, regulator, court or official authority; qualified professional for consequential applicationGeneric blog or a non-jurisdictional answer
Scientific or empirical resultOriginal paper, dataset and method; correction/retraction status; suitable expert synthesisPress release or percentage without method/denominator
Quote, statement or eventOriginal transcript, recording, filing, document or direct statementQuote graphic, repost or citation without accessible source
Internal commitmentApproved record, contract or named decision ownerModel-written recap or unattributed notes
Media originOriginal asset/context plus provenance where presentMetadata treated as a truth verdict

The seven source questions

  • Identity: who created or published it, and what authority or interest do they have?
  • Relevance: does it address this exact claim rather than a nearby topic?
  • Scope: correct person, product, plan, place, jurisdiction and conditions?
  • Date/version: current enough for this decision?
  • Support: does it actually entail the claim, numbers and qualifiers?
  • Method: how was the evidence produced; what is missing or uncertain?
  • Independence: does corroboration trace to a genuinely separate origin?

Chapter 5 of 7

Run the Receipt Loop

Frame → Split → Find → Inspect → Cross-check → Decide → Record.

A worked decision

Return to the client update. Frame and split three claims: approval of the date, expected 20% lift and paid-social scope. Find the meeting record and named decision owner. Inspect the exact lines. The notes support none of the three as written.

The useful draft survives, but the claims change: “The client will review the proposed launch date Friday. The team discussed a possible lift but has not agreed a forecast. Paid social is an open scope question.”

  • SUPPORTED: adequate evidence supports the framed claim.
  • CONTRADICTED: adequate evidence conflicts with it.
  • UNRESOLVED: evidence is missing, mixed or too weak.
  • NOT APPLICABLE: the evidence addresses a different scope, version or question.

Chapter 6 of 7

Currentness, media and independent checks

A real source can be stale. A real provenance trail can accompany a false claim. A second answer can repeat the first answer’s blind spot.

Stale but real

A page may accurately describe an old model, plan, price or law and still be unusable for today’s decision. Record publication or version date, access date, region and change trigger.

Current provider examples belong in dated, replaceable cards—not inside the durable definition of verification.

Chapter 7 of 7

Use, qualify, escalate or stop

The goal is a better decision—not infinite research or performative certainty.

Proportionate checking

  • Low and reversible: label it draft; one quick source inspection may be enough.
  • Material but reversible: use a claim table, authoritative source, scope/date check and independent corroboration where uncertainty remains.
  • High-stakes or hard to reverse: use authoritative records and the qualified human owner or professional. Stop or escalate when evidence is inadequate.

The unseen transfer challenge

A board memo says: “Tool A is the cheapest option for us, and a new study proves teams finish analysis 50% faster.” The linked price is for a different region and plan. The memo links a news story that mentions 50% but does not identify the task, baseline, sample or original study.

  1. Split the material claims.
  2. Identify the decision-changing claim.
  3. Choose the most diagnostic evidence action.
  4. Name the scope and freshness checks.
  5. Give a qualified verdict and state what would change it.

Comparison rubric

Compare your response yourself. This records reflection; it does not score your reasoning.

Claim: Price, speed and “cheapest/best” are split into separate material claims.

Evidence: Current like-for-like plan terms and the original study/method are selected.

Limitation: Plan/region mismatch and missing task, baseline, sample and method are named.

Action: The learner states what to inspect, what remains unresolved and what would change each verdict.
