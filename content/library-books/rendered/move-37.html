A stone that looked wrong

Imagine watching one of the world’s best Go players and seeing his opponent place a stone where the experts think it does not belong. Not an ordinary opponent, either: a computer system. The commentators hesitate. The move looks awkward. Hours later, it looks inspired.

That happened on 10 March 2016, when AlphaGo played Move 37 against Lee Sedol. The story is often polished into something much grander: the moment a machine became creative, surpassed human thought or woke up in a hotel ballroom in Seoul. The match proved none of that.

It proved something more specific—and more useful. A system had learned how to suggest promising moves, judge positions and search selectively well enough to defeat an extraordinary human player. The surprise only became meaningful because Go supplied shared rules and a result everyone could inspect.

This book follows several moments like that: moments when a new idea removed a genuine obstacle. We will keep asking six ordinary questions. What was hard before? What changed? How did it work? What result could people inspect? Where did the evidence stop? What became possible next?

Use all six questions once

A company shows a dazzling ten-second demo and says its model has “reinvented work.” Let us diagnose that claim. What was hard before? The company has not named the old obstacle. What changed? We saw a new demo, but do not know whether the model, product or presentation changed. How did it work? The clip did not tell us. What result can people inspect? We can inspect ten seconds chosen by the company, not a normal working day. Where does the evidence stop? At that selected example: it does not yet establish reliability, usefulness or reinvention. What became possible next? We do not know until people try the system on real tasks and compare the results.

That diagnosis does not prove the product is poor. It shows why surprise alone has not earned the larger claim. You do not need to memorise the questions. By the end, you should be able to hear “AI breakthrough” and notice which answer is missing. That is the point—not collecting enough technical nouns to ruin an otherwise pleasant dinner.

How a machine learns from being wrong

Suppose you are adjusting a photograph. The final image is too dark, but the result came from several controls: exposure, shadows, contrast and highlights. You could jiggle everything randomly. Or you could work backwards from the bad result and estimate which controls contributed to it.

That is the useful idea behind backpropagation. A neural network contains many adjustable connections, usually called weights. During training, the network produces an answer. The training system measures how far that answer is from the one it was supposed to produce. It then carries that error backwards through the network, estimating how each connection should change so the next attempt is a little better.

The photograph controls are only an analogy. A network is not looking at its mistake, feeling embarrassed and applying judgment. It is following mathematics that changes numbers. But the analogy gives us the important movement: result → measured error → adjustments throughout the system → another attempt.

This mattered because earlier layers could begin learning useful internal patterns instead of waiting for a person to specify every feature. The 1986 paper by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams helped demonstrate how multi-layer networks could learn such representations. Read the paper.

Backpropagation did not make a network wise. It gave training a practical way to distribute correction. Data quality, computing power and whether the lesson transfers beyond training remained separate problems.

Can you say it without the term?

Try explaining backpropagation without using network, weight or algorithm. A sound answer is: “The system compares its attempt with the expected answer, traces the size of the mistake backwards and adjusts the settings that contributed to it.” If your explanation stops at “it learns from data,” the missing part is how the error changes the next attempt.

Long before this training method, Alan Turing made a different foundational move. In 1950 he replaced the slippery question “Can machines think?” with an observable imitation game. That did not explain how to build a learner or prove consciousness. It changed an argument about essence into a question about behaviour. Read Turing’s paper.

When computers stopped waiting for us to describe every picture

If you wanted an old-fashioned vision program to recognise a cat, people first had to decide which visual clues mattered: edges, corners, textures, perhaps whisker-like lines. That works until the cat turns sideways, hides behind a chair or develops the audacity to be orange in poor lighting.

AlexNet changed the practical approach. Instead of receiving a complete human-written list of visual clues, its layers learned patterns from a very large labelled image collection. Early parts of the system became sensitive to simple features such as edges. Later parts combined those signals into more useful shapes and object patterns.

The system was a convolutional neural network. The long name describes a manageable idea: small pattern detectors sweep across an image and look for useful signals wherever they appear. The detectors are learned rather than hand-written. Graphics processors, or GPUs, made it practical to perform huge numbers of the required calculations during training.

In the 2012 ImageNet competition, the AlexNet paper reported a top-five error rate of 15.3 per cent, compared with 26.2 per cent for the second-best entry. That was a large measured gap on that competition. Read the paper.

It was not human sight in a server rack. The system learned patterns useful for a particular dataset. An unfamiliar setting, biased labels or a carefully altered image could still expose serious weaknesses. The breakthrough was not “the computer sees.” It was: learned visual features, enough labelled examples and suitable hardware had become a convincingly better route for this kind of image recognition.

Spot the missing claim

If someone says, “It won the image competition, therefore it understands photographs,” what is missing? The competition tested whether the system could assign expected labels to particular images. Understanding a photograph’s context, humour, harm or meaning is a much larger claim requiring different evidence.

Three ideas hiding inside modern AI

Between 2014 and 2015, three ideas solved three different annoyances. They are often poured into one bucket labelled “deep learning.” Let’s not do that to ourselves.

Attention: look back at the part you need

Imagine translating a long voice note while being allowed to replay only one tiny summary of it. Details vanish. Early translation systems faced a related problem: they tried to compress an entire source sentence into one fixed internal summary.

Attention let the system look back at different source words while producing each new word. When translating a name, it could give more weight to the part containing the name. When translating a verb, it could focus elsewhere. The system was not “paying attention” like a person; it was calculating which positions should matter more at that step. The analogy stops at intention—there is none. Read the 2014 paper.

Explain the fix: Why is one fixed summary risky for a long sentence? Because details needed later may have been squeezed out. If your answer is “attention remembers everything,” correct it: the system calculates which source positions to weight more at each step; it does not guarantee perfect memory or meaning.

GANs: a maker and a detector improve together

Why did generated faces become more convincing when nobody wrote a complete rule for skin, lighting, hair and cheekbones? One useful training design made two systems challenge each other.

A generative adversarial network, or GAN, trains a maker and a detector together. The maker produces candidate examples. The detector tries to distinguish generated examples from training examples. As the detector becomes harder to fool, the maker has to improve.

The familiar analogy is a counterfeiter and an inspector. It helps with the contest, but it has a limit: neither system understands deception, law or artistic purpose. They optimise a mathematical game. GANs created a powerful new route to synthetic images, but supplied no automatic truth, consent, copyright or provenance. Read the paper.

Test the result: A generated portrait looks completely realistic. Which question is still unanswered? Whether the image is truthful, consented to, licensed or properly disclosed. Realism tells you the detector was difficult to fool; it does not settle how the image should be used.

Residual connections: improve the answer instead of rebuilding it

Why could adding more layers make a learning system harder to train rather than automatically better? Each new layer was being asked to reconstruct a useful transformation, even when keeping much of the earlier signal would have been fine.

Residual networks added a shortcut that carries the earlier signal forward. The new layer learns only the useful change—the residual—rather than rebuilding the whole result from scratch.

Think of editing a good paragraph: “keep this, change these two sentences” is easier than rewriting the page blind. Again, the system is not editing prose or judging quality. The mapping is simply existing signal + learned correction. The 2015 paper reported successful training of a 152-layer network and strong ImageNet results. Read the paper.

Say what the layer learns: If the shortcut already carries the old information, the new path learns the adjustment worth adding. If you say “the shortcut skips the work,” you have missed the point: the shortcut preserves a useful baseline so the layer can concentrate on the change.

The earned lesson is that breakthroughs are often components. Attention did not create every modern language system. GANs did not invent art. Residual connections did not make depth magical. Each removed one obstacle that later builders could combine with many other ideas.

Move 37: where should the machine look?

Go presents an absurd number of possible continuations. Trying every future game is not a plan; it is a very long way to discover that the universe has ended first.

AlphaGo’s important move was learning where to spend its effort. One network—the policy network—suggested moves that appeared promising. Another—the value network—estimated which player was likely to win from a position. A tree search then explored useful continuations more deeply instead of treating every legal move equally.

Picture planning a journey through an unfamiliar city. One friend suggests the most promising routes. Another estimates which current location is getting you closer. You investigate the plausible routes rather than walking down every street. AlphaGo’s system did not have friends, a city map or common sense; the analogy only shows the division of labour: propose, estimate, search.

The system first learned patterns from expert games and then improved through reinforcement-learning self-play. It played games, received the unambiguous result—win or lose—and used that signal to improve. Read the AlphaGo paper.

DeepMind describes Move 37 in Game 2 as having roughly a one-in-ten-thousand likelihood under AlphaGo’s move prediction. That means the move was far outside expected play in that position. It does not mean no human had ever imagined placing a stone there. See DeepMind’s match record.

AlphaGo won the match 4–1. That proves strong match performance under Go’s rules. It does not prove Move 37 alone caused the win, that the move was uniquely correct, or that AlphaGo had become generally intelligent. The move’s rarity, the match result and experts’ later interpretation are three separate claims. Read DeepMind’s retrospective.

Lee Sedol’s Game 4 victory matters as well. His Move 78 also surprised the system. The story is not “machines became creative and humans became furniture.” It is a bounded contest in which learned search produced a remarkable strategy and a human still found counterplay.

The idea travelled. AlphaZero later used self-play reinforcement learning with search across chess, shogi and Go. That is evidence of transfer across several games with clear rules—not evidence that the same method can judge a diagnosis, a court case or whether your manager’s email is passive-aggressive. Read the AlphaZero paper.

Teach it to someone who missed the match

Why was selective search more important than “calculating everything”? A good answer mentions three jobs: suggest promising moves, estimate positions and explore the promising routes. If you say only “it learned by playing itself,” you have explained the training source but not how the system chose where to search during a game.

How words and pictures became easier to generate

Modern generative AI did not arrive as one invention. Three developments made different connections possible.

Transformers: every word can check the other words

Earlier language systems often processed a sentence step by step, carrying a changing summary forward. Important information from far back could become difficult to use, and the sequence limited how much training work could happen simultaneously.

A transformer lets each word or token calculate which other tokens matter for its current representation. In “The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too large,” the relationships between it, trophy, suitcase and large matter. Attention helps the system connect those positions directly.

This does not mean the model reads with human understanding. It calculates relationships learned from data. But the architecture made training more parallel and became a foundation for many later language and multimodal models. Read the transformer paper.

Unseen case: A message says, “The meeting was moved because it conflicted with the launch. It now starts Friday.” Explain the useful mechanism without saying transformer or attention. The system needs a direct way to connect “it” with the relevant earlier subject while building its representation. If you answer “it remembers the sentence,” you have named an outcome, not the relationship calculation that helps produce it.

Diffusion: learn to undo noise

Take an image, add a tiny amount of visual noise, then more, until it becomes static. A diffusion model is trained on the reverse job: given a noisy image and the stage of corruption, predict a slightly cleaner version. After enough training, it can begin with noise and repeatedly remove it until an image appears.

It is a little like learning photo restoration by practising on damage you created yourself. The analogy stops at intention and meaning: the model does not know what the “original” imagined picture should be. It learns statistical denoising patterns. The 2020 work supplied an important route to high-quality image generation, but not text guidance, consent or copyright answers. Read the paper.

Unseen case: Someone says the system must be retrieving a stored photograph because a detailed image emerged from static. What would you correct? It learned many small noise-removal steps and used them to generate a new sample. The starting noise does not contain a hidden finished photograph waiting to be uncovered.

CLIP: which caption belongs with which picture?

CLIP learned from image-and-text pairs. It placed matching images and descriptions closer together in a shared numerical space. At use time, it could compare an unfamiliar image with written category descriptions without being trained separately for each exact category.

A shared numerical space is not a secret gallery inside the computer. It means the model represents related text and images with patterns that can be compared mathematically. This connection helped later systems respond to text about images, while retaining biases and weaknesses documented by its authors. Read the CLIP paper.

Unseen case: You type “red shoes at night” into an image search that was never trained on a special folder with that exact label. Why might it still find relevant pictures? Because training brought matching descriptions and images into a comparable pattern space. If you answer only “it recognises red shoes,” the missing part is how new words can be compared with new images without a custom category.

Transformers connect relevant parts of a sequence. Diffusion learns a path from noise toward a sample. CLIP connects descriptions with images. The names matter less than being able to explain the different job each one performs.

AlphaFold: a better place to begin an experiment

A protein begins as a chain of amino acids. The chain folds into a three-dimensional shape, and that shape helps determine what the protein can do. Knowing the sequence is like having the beads on a necklace while needing to know the exact three-dimensional arrangement it takes in use.

The necklace is an imperfect analogy. Proteins are not jewellery, they can move between shapes, and their surroundings matter. But it reveals the problem: the same list of parts does not hand you the finished structure.

AlphaFold2 made a major advance in predicting a protein’s three-dimensional structure from its amino-acid sequence. It looked at evolutionary evidence—patterns across related sequences that hint which parts influence one another—and learned relationships between pairs of amino-acid positions. It then proposed three-dimensional coordinates and supplied confidence information showing where the prediction was more or less trustworthy.

The system did not simply search a database for a finished answer, and it did not watch every atom fold through time. It learned patterns connecting sequence, evolutionary evidence and known structures, then produced a prediction.

CASP14 provided a powerful test. Competitors received protein targets whose experimental structures were withheld. Their predictions were later compared with the experimental results. AlphaFold2 substantially outperformed competing methods and achieved high accuracy for many targets in that blind assessment. Read the Nature paper and CASP14 results.

The breakthrough was not “biology solved.” A predicted structure does not automatically reveal a protein’s function, the effect of a mutation, a disease mechanism or a working drug. Confidence varies. Proteins can have several conformations. Experiments still carry the biological claim across the finish line. The AlphaFold Database documents these limits. Read its guidance.

The useful chain is: sequence → predicted structure and confidence → biological hypothesis → experiment. AlphaFold changed the starting point, not the need for evidence.

Explain the boundary

A friend says, “AlphaFold predicted the shape, so scientists know what the protein does.” What would you correct? A sound answer separates structure from function and prediction from experiment. If your answer is only “AI can be wrong,” make it more precise: which biological claim remains untested?

Your turn: is this a breakthrough yet?

A company announces: “Our new system scored 18 per cent higher than our previous model on an internal benchmark. It is now in our paid product. This changes everything.” No method paper, benchmark details, independent comparison or external evaluation is supplied.

Before reading on, explain to an imaginary colleague why this is—or is not—enough. Avoid the word breakthrough. Say what the company has actually shown.

The defensible answer is: the company reports that its new system performed better than its previous system on a test the company has not described. It has also launched a product. We do not yet know what obstacle was removed, what reusable idea produced the improvement, how the result compares with other systems, whether an outsider can reproduce it, or what changed beyond the company’s own test.

That does not prove the system is unimpressive. It leaves the important boxes blank.

When the next headline arrives, ask: What was hard before? What changed inside the method? What result can someone inspect? What did that test actually measure? Where does the claim stop? What later consequence has been observed?

If you can answer those in ordinary language, you understand the breakthrough. If you can only repeat the launch slogan, the marketing department is still holding the pen.
