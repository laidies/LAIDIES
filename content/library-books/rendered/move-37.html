The Breakthroughs That Changed Modern AI

Modern AI came from faster chips, larger datasets, better training methods, harder tests and interfaces ordinary people could actually use.

How did this become normal?

Your phone recognises your face, filters spam, finds the dog in six years of photographs and reroutes you around traffic. You can ask a computer to explain a contract, make an image or translate a conversation. A decade ago, several of those things were unreliable research projects. Now they are Tuesday.

So what happened?

There was no single invention called modern AI. The version we have now came from several changes landing on top of one another: faster chips, much larger collections of examples, better ways to train models, harder tests, new interfaces and a frankly enormous amount of human labour. A few advances were headline events. Others looked like infrastructure until somebody used them to flatten an old limit.

This book starts in the 2000s, when those ingredients began combining at useful scale. It covers recommendation systems, speech, computer vision, language, image generation, games and science. AlphaGo and AlphaFold are here, properly explained, but they are not being asked to carry the entire history like two women who made eye contact during a chaotic meeting.

Technical terms appear only after the idea they name. You do not need to know what a transformer is before you begin. That is one of the reasons to begin.

One warning. Technology companies use breakthrough the way product launches use revolutionary: frequently, confidently and with a commercial interest in your believing it. The chapters here use a stricter test. What could not be done before? What changed? How was it tested? What happened next? And where did the result stop?

A system can beat a world champion at Go and still know nothing about running a hospital. It can predict a protein's shape and still not tell a scientist whether a drug will work. The achievement does not shrink when we state its limits. It becomes clear enough to understand.

The AI choosing what billions of people see

Most people met AI long before they opened a chatbot. It was already choosing the next video, song, product, post and person on their screen.

The problem was volume. A streaming service might hold millions of options. Early recommendation systems compared patterns across many people's behaviour. If you and another viewer liked several of the same films, the films you disagreed on became useful clues. One influential method reduced a huge table of people and ratings into a smaller set of learned taste patterns. Its name is matrix factorisation. It became prominent during the Netflix Prize, a public competition to improve Netflix recommendations.

Larger services split the work. One model made a shortlist from millions of possible items; another ranked it. First decide what is eligible to appear, then decide its order.

Here is the part personalised for you leaves out. The system needs a target. People at the company choose whether it should predict a click, a purchase, minutes watched, stated satisfaction or some mixture. If it is rewarded for attention, it learns what holds attention. That is not necessarily what informs you, settles you or leaves you pleased with how you spent an hour.

Cher's closet computer in Clueless scored outfits against rules somebody had entered. A recommendation system does something similar at industrial scale, except the platform chooses the scoring rule. If the rule is keep her watching, the system will not quietly substitute make sure she feels good about the last forty minutes. Different brief.

Ranking also affects what people see; what they see affects what they click; those clicks become new training data. The supposedly neutral observer has been rearranging the room.

Research on social effects varies across platforms and populations. A 2026 independent experiment on X found that its algorithmic feed increased engagement and shifted some current political attitudes among the US users studied, without significantly changing their reported partisanship or affective polarisation. That does not settle every argument about algorithms. It establishes that ranking can change exposure and behaviour.

When a feed feels uncannily personal, skip “Does it know me?” Ask what it was paid—in clicks, time or money—to predict.

Sources: Matrix factorisation for recommender systems, Deep neural networks for YouTube recommendations, independent randomised experiment on X's feed.

The hardware that let AI experiments grow

Training a neural network means showing it examples, measuring its errors and adjusting a large number of numerical connections. Then doing it again. And again. The arithmetic is repetitive and there is a lot of it.

A normal computer processor is a generalist. A graphics processor, or GPU, was designed to update many screen pixels at once. It contained many simpler calculating units able to perform similar operations in parallel.

Researchers had used graphics hardware for scientific calculations, but programming it was awkward. In 2006 NVIDIA released CUDA, a system that let programmers use its GPUs for general calculations without disguising the work as graphics.

Neural-network training contained exactly the sort of repeated arithmetic GPUs could accelerate. Experiments that took too long became practical. Researchers could try larger networks, use more examples and discover sooner when an idea was useless—which is an underrated service to everyone involved.

Specialised chips followed. Google's Tensor Processing Unit was designed around calculations neural networks perform repeatedly. Shared software such as TensorFlow and PyTorch handled difficult engineering across data, accelerators and experiments. None could recognise a cat. They made it possible for more researchers to build and test systems that might.

AlexNet's 2012 result depended on a neural-network design, a huge labelled dataset and GPUs. Remove one and the famous demonstration changes.

The shift also changed who could compete. As larger experiments became a reliable route to stronger results, advanced chips, electricity, data centres and specialist engineers became part of the entrance fee. A paper might be public while reproducing it remained financially out of reach.

The chip did not become intelligent. It removed a practical ceiling. From then on, what a model could learn was increasingly tied to what its owner could afford to compute.

Sources: NVIDIA's CUDA history, Google's first TPU production analysis, TensorFlow system paper, PyTorch paper.

The result that redirected computer vision

In 2009 the internet already held an ocean of photographs. “Here is an ocean” is not a useful lesson plan for a computer. The images needed labels, categories and a test.

The ImageNet team built that infrastructure at a scale the field had not had before. They used WordNet, a human-made dictionary of related concepts, and paid online crowdworkers through Amazon Mechanical Turk to check whether images matched labels. The 2009 paper described 3.2 million images across 5,247 categories. An annual competition later supplied a large training collection and a hidden test set.

Earlier vision systems often depended on people deciding in advance which visual clues—edges, corners, textures—might matter. A convolutional neural network learned small detectors from examples, moved them across an image and combined simple patterns into more complex ones. When it answered incorrectly, backpropagation traced the error through its connections and adjusted them.

In 2012 Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton trained a large network on ImageNet using GPUs. Their competition entry produced 15.3 per cent top-five test error; the second-place entry produced 26.2 per cent. Top five meant the correct label counted if it appeared among the model's five highest guesses. The winning number came from several models combined.

That gap redirected the field. Large labelled datasets, learned visual features and parallel hardware had worked together decisively.

The score was closer to Elle Woods acing the LSAT than becoming a fully qualified lawyer. It proved strong performance on a defined test. It did not prove competence at every task anyone might place under the generous heading of “vision.”

No, the machine had not learned to see like a person. It had become much better at sorting selected images into selected categories. Change the photographs, cameras, people or task and performance can fall apart.

The categories were human choices too. The dataset included cultural imbalances and later scrutiny found offensive and invasive labels involving people. ImageNet taught two lessons at once: models could learn useful visual features instead of having every feature specified by hand, and they could become very good at the exact exam people built—including its bad assumptions.

Sources: ImageNet paper, ImageNet history, AlexNet paper, ImageNet competition retrospective.

When speech recognition became useful

Say water in a quiet room. Now say it through a bad phone connection, while laughing, with a cold, in a busy kitchen or in an accent missing from the training data. Same word. Different signal.

Speech recognition must turn moving sound into text while handling speakers, rooms, microphones, speed and noise it may not have encountered. Older systems used several carefully engineered statistical components to estimate which sounds and words most likely produced each recording.

In the early 2010s, research groups replaced an important acoustic component with deep neural networks trained on large collections of recorded speech and transcripts. The networks learned layers of useful sound patterns instead of requiring experts to specify every clue.

A 2012 paper brought together results from four groups. Across several tests, the deep networks beat the older comparison systems, sometimes substantially. This was converging evidence, not one laboratory waking up with hearing.

Accuracy was measured with word error rate: how many words must be inserted, removed or replaced to match the correct transcript. A 2020 independent US study found much higher average error rates for Black speakers than white speakers in its sample of commercial systems. It did not test every product, language or current model. It showed why speech recognition improved and speech recognition works equally well for everyone are separate claims.

The advance moved deep learning into dictation, captions, voice search and assistants. It expanded access for many people and created a fresh barrier for others. Recording, consent, storage and privacy remained product decisions that an accuracy score could not answer.

Speaking to a device began to feel less like enunciating for a deeply unimpressed call-centre menu. That shift came from learned sound patterns, more data and better computing—not a computer acquiring ears.

Sources: four-group speech-recognition overview, large-vocabulary speech study, independent racial-disparity study.

How language models learned useful relationships

Computers once handled words rather like product codes. The code for queen contained no built-in relationship to woman, royal or king.

In 2013 researchers showed an efficient way to learn some of those relationships from surrounding text. A model practised predicting a word from nearby words, or nearby words from a central one. Words used in similar contexts ended up in nearby numerical locations. The method was called word2vec; the locations are word embeddings.

An embedding is not a definition and it does not give a machine human meaning. It makes patterns of word use available for calculation. Because those patterns came from people, the embeddings also reproduced stereotypes in the source text.

Translation exposed another problem. Early neural systems read a source sentence and compressed it into one fixed numerical summary before producing the translation. That summary became a bottleneck, especially for longer sentences.

In 2014 Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio changed the arrangement. As the model produced each translated word, it calculated which parts of the source mattered most at that step. A name might depend on one position; a verb on another. The system no longer had to rely on one fixed summary for the whole sentence.

They called the method attention. It did not mean concentration or consciousness. It was a changing set of numerical weights controlling which earlier pieces influenced the next output.

This solved a specific engineering problem and created a much larger opening. Language models could use context more selectively. Three years later, researchers would reorganise an entire architecture around that idea.

Sources: word2vec paper, sequence-to-sequence learning, neural attention.

A new way to generate images

Recognising an image means choosing what it resembles. Generating one means producing a new example that fits patterns learned from many others.

In 2014 Ian Goodfellow and seven colleagues proposed training two neural networks against each other. A generator made candidate images. A discriminator tried to distinguish those images from the training examples. When the discriminator spotted a weakness, the generator received a better signal about what to change. As the generator improved, the discriminator had to improve too.

The pair became a generative adversarial network, or GAN. The first paper showed handwritten digits, faces and small photographs. Nobody was mistaking them for a fashion campaign. The important change was the training arrangement: the model could learn to generate without researchers writing a complete formula for every possible image.

The arrangement was clever and temperamental. If the discriminator became too strong, the generator received little useful guidance. A generator could also discover one reliable type of output and keep making variations of it, a failure called mode collapse.

Later GANs produced convincing synthetic faces and scenes. They changed image editing and design research, and separated visual realism from documentary truth at scale. A photograph-like image no longer needed a camera, a person or an event on the other side of it.

GANs did not remain the only route to image generation. Their lasting contribution was proving that one learning system could improve through pressure from another system that was also changing. The answer key did not have to stay still.

Source: GAN paper.

Why AlphaGo changed more than a scoreboard

Go uses black and white stones on a grid. The rules are manageable. Good luck with the next twenty moves.

A strong player has to consider how one quiet stone might change a fight across the board much later. Each move opens hundreds of replies, then hundreds more. “We'll just check every possibility” is the sort of plan that sounds excellent until the spreadsheet opens.

In March 2016, AlphaGo placed a white stone in a spot many expert players thought was wrong. It was Move 37 in the second game of a five-game match against Lee Sedol, one of the world's leading Go players. The stone landed farther from the edge than convention usually favoured. Experienced players looked at it and thought: excuse me?

This is where the story can get silly quickly. “The machine was creative.” “The machine had intuition.” Slow down. An unexpected move is not evidence of insight. Surprise is cheap. The game still has to be played.

AlphaGo divided the problem into three jobs. It proposed moves worth considering, estimated which player was more likely to win from a position, then spent extra calculation on the continuations that looked promising. Researchers call those parts a policy network, a value network and Monte Carlo tree search. Together, they let the program propose, estimate and investigate without searching everything.

AlphaGo first studied recorded games from strong human players. Then versions of the program faced one another. A final win or loss supplied feedback, and the system adjusted so choices associated with winning became more likely. That is reinforcement learning: try an action, receive a result later, change the odds of making a similar choice next time.

DeepMind described Move 37 as having roughly a one-in-ten-thousand probability under the model of human Go it had learned. Do not turn that into a grand legend. It does not measure creativity. It says the move sat far outside the patterns that particular model expected from its human-game data.

Lee Sedol tested the move under actual match conditions. AlphaGo won Game Two and eventually the match four games to one. Game Four matters too: Lee made his own unexpected move, AlphaGo responded badly and Lee won. A system can be stronger overall and still have a bad day at the office. Both facts can sit at the same table.

Go gave AlphaGo fixed rules, legal moves, endless safe practice and an opponent determined to beat it. Hiring, medicine and news are not nearly so polite. People disagree about the goal and the easiest score may reward the wrong thing.

Move 37 did not make AlphaGo a person. It showed that learned judgement could direct limited search into parts of an enormous possibility space that human convention rarely visited. That is the part worth keeping.

Sources: AlphaGo paper, AlphaGo research record, AlphaZero, MuZero.

The architecture behind today's language models

The translation systems in the previous chapter still processed language one step after another. That matched the order of a sentence but made training slow: each step waited for the previous one, leaving much of a GPU's parallel capacity unused.

In 2017 eight researchers published Attention Is All You Need. Their architecture let every position in a passage calculate direct relationships with other positions. It removed the recurrent relay that had carried information forward one step at a time, so many relationships could be calculated in parallel.

They called it a transformer.

Take the sentence: “The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too large.” To handle it, the model can calculate relationships with both trophy and suitcase and use the surrounding text to decide which connection is useful. This is not human comprehension. It is a way for context to change the representation of each word.

The original paper tested the design on translation. The larger consequence appeared later. Transformers worked well with the GPUs already reshaping the field. Researchers could train much larger models on much more data. Variations moved into images, audio and science.

The transformer did not provide truth. It became very good at relationships between pieces of data. A model can use those relationships to produce a sentence that fits perfectly and describes an event that never happened. Fluency remains presentation, not verification.

This architecture sits at the centre of modern AI because it combined a strong way to handle context with efficient large-scale training. It did not invent language or understanding. It made far larger training runs useful.

Source: transformer paper.

Pretraining without labelling every answer

ImageNet required people to label millions of pictures. The same approach could not cover every language task. Most writing does not arrive marked good legal summary, polite refusal or email that will not start a six-person reply-all crisis.

Language models found a way to generate practice questions from existing text. Hide a word and ask the model to recover it. Show the start of a passage and ask what comes next. The original text supplies the temporary answer.

This is self-supervised learning. The system has not taken charge of its own education. People still choose the data, objective, exclusions and model. The examples simply provide their own training targets.

Google researchers introduced BERT in 2018. It hid some words and used context on both sides to predict them. After broad pretraining, the model could be adjusted with a smaller labelled collection for a specific job—a stage called fine-tuning.

Another family predicted the next piece of text. OpenAI described GPT-3 in 2020. At that scale, users could sometimes specify a task with an instruction or a few examples in the prompt without changing the model's learned settings. One model could attempt translation, rewriting and question answering depending on the text placed in front of it.

Researchers also measured relationships between performance, training data, model size and computing. These scaling laws made larger training runs look like a more predictable investment. They did not prove that size creates truth or wisdom. Later work showed the balance between model size, data and computing mattered.

Pretraining changed the industry. Instead of building one model per task, organisations could train an expensive general model and adapt it many times. It also concentrated power and absorbed the problems in the data: private information, stereotypes, copyrighted work and repeated falsehoods.

The machine did not read the internet and emerge educated. It completed an immense number of prediction exercises made from human material. That narrower account explains why the field changed.

Sources: BERT, scaling laws, GPT-3, compute-optimal training.

What AlphaFold2 actually changed

A protein is a chain of amino acids folded into a three-dimensional shape. That shape affects which surfaces meet, which pocket can hold another molecule and whether the protein can do its job. Knowing the amino-acid sequence is useful. It is not the same as having the working structure.

Scientists determine structures with methods including X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance and cryo-electron microscopy. These require specialist equipment, careful samples and serious time. For decades, predicting an accurate structure from the sequence remained one of biology's hard problems.

Every two years, CASP—the Critical Assessment of protein Structure Prediction—made it difficult to mark your own homework. Organisers obtained structures established experimentally but not yet public. Teams received the sequences and submitted predictions. Only then were the hidden structures revealed.

At CASP14 in 2020, DeepMind's AlphaFold2 produced a major jump in accuracy. For many targets, its error approached the scale of experimental uncertainty. Independent assessors compared the predictions with answers the team had not seen.

Evolution supplied part of the evidence. Related organisms often have related protein sequences. When two positions change together over time, that can suggest the corresponding parts of the folded protein interact.

AlphaFold2 worked back and forth between two developing maps. One held clues about relationships across a family of sequences. The other held possible distances and positions in three-dimensional space. A new sequence clue could change the proposed shape; that shape could change which sequence relationships still made sense. After several rounds, the system produced predicted atomic coordinates and confidence estimates.

It did not simulate a tiny protein physically folding. It predicted a likely final structure from learned patterns and constraints.

That changed the first day of an investigation. A scientist could often begin with a useful structural hypothesis instead of no shape. The AlphaFold database later made hundreds of millions of predictions available.

It did not finish biology. Proteins move, interact and sometimes take several forms. A predicted structure does not prove what a protein does, whether a drug works or how to treat a disease. AlphaFold2 moved a difficult starting line. The real world still gets a vote.

Sources: AlphaFold2 paper, CASP14 official results, AlphaFold database paper.

How diffusion models made images from words

Take an image and add a little noise. Keep going until the picture disappears. Because the clean original is known, every damaged version creates a training problem: what change would make this slightly less noisy?

A diffusion model learns those small reversals. To generate an image, it starts with noise and repeatedly applies the learned denoising step. Broad structure appears, then shapes and details. There is no hidden photograph inside the static. The model has learned directions that tend to move noisy patterns towards the kinds of images in its training data.

Doing that work across every pixel was expensive. Latent diffusion compressed an image into a smaller internal representation, did much of the denoising there and converted the result back into pixels. Latent means compressed internal space. It is an engineering term, not evidence of an imagination.

Text made the process controllable. OpenAI's CLIP learned relationships between hundreds of millions of image-and-caption pairs. Later generators used related connections so a phrase could influence the denoising process. A sentence became a flexible visual instruction.

That changed who could explore an image idea and how quickly. It also made imitation, deceptive images and non-consensual material easier to produce.

The data is part of the mechanism. LAION-5B described billions of image–text pairs filtered from the web. Those pairs existed because people made, photographed, captioned and uploaded things. Saying a model learned patterns does not answer questions about permission, attribution, bias or removal. Saying every output is a stored copy is also inaccurate. The useful question is provenance: what entered the training collection, under what terms, and what rights remain with the people whose work was used?

Sources: 2015 diffusion formulation, denoising diffusion, CLIP, latent diffusion, LAION-5B.

Human feedback changed the audience

GPT-3 could continue text. That is not the same job as helping a person. Ask a raw language model a question and it might answer, continue the question, imitate a web page or wander off with the confidence of someone who has not read the brief.

Researchers used human feedback to make the behaviour follow instructions more reliably. People wrote examples of useful responses and ranked alternatives. A separate model learned to predict those preferences. The language model was adjusted to favour higher-scoring answers.

This family of methods is called reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. The word human is doing serious work. People decided what counted as clear, safe or useful. Their assumptions, disagreements and working conditions entered the system.

It did not install Miranda Priestly inside the model. It supplied many smaller judgements: this answer is clearer than that one; this response follows the instruction; this one should never have left the building. The labelling rules determined whose standards the model was being trained to follow.

The 2022 InstructGPT paper documented labelers providing demonstrations and rankings. Separate reporting described Kenyan workers employed through Sama to classify disturbing text for an OpenAI safety tool. These were related parts of a larger labour chain, not one interchangeable job. It would be inaccurate to say those workers performed all of ChatGPT's preference training.

OpenAI released ChatGPT as a research preview on 30 November 2022. The ingredients—transformers, next-word pretraining, instruction tuning and human feedback—already existed. The interface changed access.

A blank chat box let people ask in ordinary language, read the answer, correct it and continue. “Shorter.” “Use this example.” “Explain that without the jargon.” The public could discover the model's range and failures without writing code.

Feedback training did not install judgement or a fact-checker. The assistant could invent sources, agree with a false premise and deliver nonsense in immaculate formatting. Retrieval-augmented generation offered a partial repair by finding external documents and placing passages in the current context. It can still find the wrong source or use it badly.

ChatGPT turned a research lineage into a product non-specialists could test. The world did not suddenly receive machine intelligence. It received a very accessible interface to a powerful and unreliable language model.

Sources: InstructGPT, ChatGPT research preview, TIME investigation of Kenyan safety labour, retrieval-augmented generation.

AI moves into weather forecasting

Weather forecasting starts with measurements of the atmosphere and uses physical equations to project that state forward. The calculations run across a three-dimensional grid on powerful computers. It is difficult, expensive and extremely well developed.

Google DeepMind trained GraphCast on decades of reconstructed atmospheric states from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. Instead of recalculating every physical interaction for each forecast, the model learned how one weather state tended to develop into the next.

GraphCast represented the globe as connected points at several scales. Local connections carried nearby weather; broader ones moved information over longer distances. A structure of points and connections is a graph, hence the name.

Its 2023 paper reported better accuracy than the European centre's leading single-outcome physics system on 89.3 per cent of 2,760 tested combinations of weather variable and forecast time. On the stated Google hardware, it produced a ten-day forecast in under a minute.

The number needs its boundaries. The comparison used a particular historical dataset, group of variables and accuracy tests. Weather agencies also need uncertainty estimates, rare-event monitoring, new observations and expert judgement. GraphCast depended on a physics-based system to create the reconstructed history used for training.

The result still changed the field. Several groups developed strong learned forecasting models. In 2025 the European centre put its own AI Forecasting System into operations alongside its physics-based systems, first as a single forecast and later as an ensemble.

This is a useful contrast with Go. A forecast does not meet an opponent inside fixed rules. The atmosphere supplies the result. Tomorrow arrives, and everyone gets to see how the model did.

Sources: GraphCast paper, European centre's operational AI forecasting record, operational ensemble and limitations.

Who can build and challenge modern AI?

A chat box feels weightless. Behind it are chips, data centres, electricity, cooling, software, capital and people.

As larger training runs became a dependable route to stronger benchmark results, frontier model-building moved towards companies able to afford them. Stanford's 2026 AI Index reported that industry produced more than 90 per cent of the notable models it counted in 2025. That is a dated measurement using the report's definitions, not a permanent law. It shows where expensive development had concentrated.

The International Energy Agency estimated that data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity worldwide in 2024 and projected roughly 945 by 2030 in its base case, with AI an important driver alongside other digital services. Those figures cover data centres, not AI alone. Local pressure can be much larger where facilities cluster around particular power grids and communities.

Human labour disappears just as easily. People make the text, images and recordings; collect and clean data; label objects; compare answers; classify harmful material; write software; maintain hardware and investigate failures. Automated often describes the final ten seconds the user sees.

Access is not one switch either. Model weights are the numerical settings produced during training. Releasing them may let others run or adapt a trained model. It does not necessarily reveal the training data, software, safety testing or legal permissions. Open source should not be used as a warm general compliment for every weight release.

The systems in this book were never models alone. ImageNet required crowdworkers. AlphaGo began with human games. Chat assistants used human comparisons. AlphaFold depended on decades of laboratory structures. The supply chain belongs inside the explanation because it determines who can build, inspect, challenge and benefit from the result.

Sources: Stanford AI Index 2026, International Energy Agency on data centres, BLOOM, OECD analysis of AI markets.

What we know, what we do not, and what could come next

The next stage is discussed with two terms that often arrive carrying more certainty than evidence: artificial general intelligence and superintelligence.

There is no universally accepted test for AGI. Definitions usually point towards a system that can perform across a broad range of cognitive tasks, learn new tasks and operate with less task-specific engineering than today's models. Companies and researchers disagree about the threshold. No press release can settle a definition its author helped choose.

Buffy had a prophecy. AGI has competing definitions and benchmark suites. Do not confuse the genres.

Superintelligence is a further claim: a system that greatly exceeds the best human performance across very broad domains. No system meeting that description has been established by the evidence covered here. Rapid self-improvement, sudden takeoff and permanent loss of human control are serious research and policy questions. They are not events that have already occurred.

Current systems can nevertheless become more capable and more able to act through tools. That could accelerate parts of science, widen access to expertise and help people complete work previously out of reach. It could also scale cyber misuse, surveillance, concentrated power and errors that travel farther before a person notices.

History gives us a useful discipline. ImageNet improved learned visual classification; it did not create human sight. AlphaGo changed learned search; it did not create general judgement. AlphaFold2 changed the start of structural investigations; it did not complete biology. ChatGPT changed access; the chat box did not make its answers true.

When the next system is announced, look past the launch video. Which old limit moved? What independent test pushed back? Did the result survive outside the laboratory? What new work became possible? Where does the test stop resembling real life? Who controls the machinery, and who pays when it fails?

A future system may justify a new chapter. It earns one through evidence and consequence, not volume.

Sources: GNoME materials paper, Google DeepMind's Levels of AGI, OpenAI Charter, scholarly definition and analysis of superintelligence, governance of agentic systems.

How updates work

This is a living history. A new edition is triggered when strong evidence changes the importance, interpretation or consequences of a chapter—or when a newer result survives long enough to deserve one.

Sources at the end of each chapter lead mainly to original papers, official test records and independent research used to check broader claims. Company accounts are useful for release dates and descriptions of a company's own work. They are not independent proof that the work changed society.

Corrections should identify the chapter, exact sentence, supporting source and proposed change. An accepted correction creates a new version and requires fresh review.

Recent agent systems, AI-generated scientific candidates and claims about progress towards AGI remain provisional. Strong performance now does not yet prove lasting consequence. The book will keep promising separate from changed the field.

Historical credit also needs continued work. Papers name authors more readily than data workers, translators, test organisers, public funders, infrastructure engineers and the people whose work became training material. Future editions should improve that record rather than hide the gap in a gracious paragraph near the back.

The rule for a new chapter is demanding on purpose: a real limit moved; outsiders could inspect the result; something consequential followed; and a nontechnical reader can explain what changed without repeating unexplained terms.

If a launch cannot yet clear that standard, it belongs on the watchlist. It can wait there with the other urgent announcements.
