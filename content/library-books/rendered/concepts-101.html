THE 101s · CONCEPTS 101

Follow one request through the system.

AI is not one mysterious thing that either “knows” or “doesn’t know.” A product gathers your request and the material available for this task, sends that context to a trained model, may let the model use tools, and returns an output that still needs judgment. This book gives you one connected map of that trip so the vocabulary stops floating around as seventeen unrelated definitions.

The whole map in one line

Your goal + prompt + available context → product → model → optional tools → output → your check. Training shaped the model before today’s task. Retrieval, memory and tools can add material now. None of them makes the result automatically true.

What the analogy gets right—and where it stops

Think of the system as a reference-desk workflow. Your question and the material you bring are the request; the product is the desk that decides what can be used; the model is one worker producing a candidate answer; tools are the catalogue, calculator or scanner; and your check determines whether the result is fit to leave the desk.

The limit: a real librarian can understand responsibility, notice social context and refuse for human reasons. An AI model does not become a person, a professional or an authority because the workflow resembles a desk. The analogy maps the hand-offs. It does not prove judgment, truth, confidentiality or permission.

The recurring case: a client handover email

We will follow one harmless, ordinary job through every section: you want an AI assistant to draft a client handover email from your notes and the current service policy.

The useful result must name the new contact, preserve the date and commitments in your notes, use the current policy, and flag anything the sources do not establish. That gives us something concrete to inspect. If the answer invents a promise, uses an old policy or drops the date, the failure is visible.

The system, not one magic box

Provider, product, model and tools do different jobs

  • The provider develops and operates models and products.
  • The product is the app or service you open. It controls the interface, account, instructions, permissions, available models and tools.
  • The model is a trained component that transforms input into output. A large language model, or LLM, is designed to work with language.
  • The tools are extra capabilities the product may connect: search, file retrieval, calculators, code execution or actions in another service.

The model is not the whole product. The same model can behave differently when the product changes its instructions, available context, tools or safety controls. One product may expose several models, choose one automatically or hide the model name.

What training did before you arrived

Training data is the collection of examples used to adjust a model while it is being built so its parameters can learn patterns. The mix and training stages vary by model and provider. The important boundary is this: training happened before your current request.

Your handover notes are not automatically “training data” simply because you attach them. They can become current context for this task. What the provider may retain or use later depends on the exact product, account, settings and governing terms; Accounts 101 owns that decision.

What “generative” describes

Generative AI produces content from learned patterns plus the instructions and material available now. The output might be text, an image, audio, video or code. “Generative” describes the kind of output. It does not mean the system searched the web, checked a source, understood your organization or earned permission to use private material.

In our case, the language model generates the handover draft. It is not retrieving a finished email from a filing cabinet. It produces a new sequence based on patterns, your prompt and the context the product supplied.

Where “algorithm” fits

An algorithm is a defined procedure for transforming input into output. AI systems contain many algorithms: training procedures, tokenization, retrieval ranking and generation among them. “The algorithm” is therefore usually too vague to explain a result. Ask which procedure or system layer matters.

Retention map

Before today: training shaped the model. During this task: the product assembles context and may use tools. After the answer: you decide whether the output is supported and usable.

What reaches the model for this task

Prompt and context are related, not identical

A prompt is what you give the system to work from: a question, instruction, example, image, file or combination. Context is the wider material available when the model produces this response. It may include the prompt, product instructions, earlier turns, retrieved passages, attached files or remembered details.

For the handover email, the prompt states the job and constraints. The notes and policy supply evidence. Product instructions may add a default style. The model can only use material that actually reaches its working context; a file sitting elsewhere on your computer is not silently available.

Tokens and the context window

A token is a small unit a model processes—often a word, part of a word or punctuation. Tokenization varies by model and language. The context window is the bounded working input available while the model produces a response.

That working space is not the model’s training data. Products may select, truncate or summarize earlier material before it reaches the model. A large advertised limit means more can fit; it does not guarantee that every detail will be used equally well.

Research using multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval found position-sensitive performance in selected models and controlled tasks. That is a reason to make evidence easy to locate and to check the result—not a universal law or a current-product benchmark, and not a promise that “the middle always fails.”

Memory is a product feature

A memory feature may carry selected information between conversations and place relevant parts into later context. It is not the context window, a complete transcript or a guaranteed project record. What can be retained, how it is selected and which controls exist depend on the product, account and settings.

If the correct client name or house style matters, include the authoritative version in the task. Do not assume an old remembered detail is current.

A knowledge cutoff is a warning, not a warranty

A knowledge cutoff describes a model-specific boundary in the information represented through training. It is not one date shared by every product, and the model’s own answer about its cutoff is not authoritative. A cutoff also cannot prove that an older fact remains true.

For the current service policy, use the current official policy itself. A model may know an older version perfectly and still give the wrong answer for today.

Failure

“Use what you remember about our policy.” The product may supply an old memory, the model’s trained information may be stale, or no policy may reach the context at all.

Recoverable request

“Use the attached policy dated 6 August 2026. For every commitment in the draft, point to the supporting line. If the policy does not establish it, flag it instead of guessing.”

What tools add

Retrieval fetches; grounding connects

Retrieval is the step where a system searches for or fetches material relevant to the task. It might search the web, a company knowledge base or uploaded files. Retrieval can fail by finding the wrong source, missing the best source or returning stale material.

Grounding means making the answer traceable to supplied or retrieved evidence. A grounded answer should let you see which claim came from which passage. Grounding can still fail if the source is weak, the passage does not support the claim or the answer stretches beyond its scope.

For the handover email, retrieval may locate the policy. Grounding should connect each promise in the draft to the exact policy line. A citation-shaped link is not enough; open it and check that it supports the sentence.

Multimodal means more than one kind of material

A multimodal system can work with more than one type of input or output, such as text and images. That can let it read a photographed note or inspect a chart. It does not guarantee accurate reading, complete extraction or factual interpretation. Important names and numbers still need comparison with the original.

A sandbox limits consequences

A sandbox is an isolated environment intended to limit what code or an automated system can access or affect. It is one control, not a synonym for safety. Permissions, containment, monitoring and an independent stop mechanism do different jobs.

In our email case, a draft-only workspace limits consequences better than direct send access. But the draft can still contain a false promise. Containment reduces what the system can do; it does not make what the system says correct.

Where the answer can fail

Hallucination can be one wrong detail

A hallucination is false or unsupported content inside an AI-generated answer. It may be one invented date, quote, person, source or commitment in otherwise useful work. The system is not necessarily lying; lying requires intent.

Generative models are optimized to produce plausible output from patterns and current context. Plausibility and support are not the same target. Missing evidence, ambiguous instructions, weak retrieval and product design can all create room for confident guessing.

In the handover draft, “Your renewal price is locked for twelve months” sounds helpful. If neither the notes nor policy say it, the sentence is unsupported regardless of how polished it sounds.

Use a check that can fail

  1. Mark the material claims. Names, dates, numbers, commitments, policies and causal explanations carry the risk.
  2. Match each claim to evidence. Find the exact passage, calculation or qualified reviewer.
  3. Check scope and date. A real source can still concern another account, region, version or time.
  4. Keep the unsupported part out. Revise, qualify or remove it. Asking the same model “Are you sure?” is not independent verification.
The model supplies a candidate, not the final authority.

The right question is not “Did AI write this?” It is “Which parts matter, what supports them, and who is authorized to decide?”

Capability labels are not one ladder

Reasoning model

A reasoning model or reasoning mode spends additional computation on some multi-step tasks before producing an answer. It can improve performance on particular problems. It does not certify the assumptions, sources or conclusion. A slower answer can still reason carefully from the wrong policy.

Agentic AI

Agentic AI can pursue a goal through multiple steps and use tools with some autonomy. The risk changes because the system can do more than produce a draft: it may search, edit files, send messages or trigger another service. Evaluate the goal, permissions, environment, checkpoints, logs and independent stop—not only the model’s answer quality.

Drafting the email is generative. Finding the policy is retrieval. Comparing claims may use reasoning. Sending the email is an action. One product can combine all four; the labels describe dimensions of the system, not four rival species.

AGI

Artificial general intelligence, or AGI, is a disputed idea about systems with broad capability and transfer across many unfamiliar intellectual tasks. There is no single agreed test or finish line. Definitions may emphasize breadth, performance, autonomy or economic value differently.

LAiDIES’ present position is that AGI does not exist today. That statement must still be presented with the boundary: credible experts disagree about definitions, thresholds and timelines. When someone claims AGI has arrived, ask which definition, which tasks, which comparison and which independent evidence.

AI winter

An AI winter is a period when enthusiasm, funding or institutional support for AI falls after expectations outrun results. It is a historical pattern, not a forecast that every boom must collapse on schedule. The useful lesson is to separate demonstrated capability from promises and market heat.

Put the map to work

When an AI result disappoints you, locate the layer before changing everything:

  • Wrong job or shape? Fix the prompt or brief.
  • Missing or stale information? Supply or retrieve the right source.
  • Dropped detail? Reduce or restructure the context and make evidence findable.
  • Missing capability? Check the product, model, plan, permissions or tools.
  • Unsupported claim? Verify outside the answer and remove what does not survive.
  • Risky action? Reduce permissions, use a sandbox, require checkpoints or keep the task draft-only.

You now have one model for the whole system: training shaped the model; the product assembles context; tools may extend what the system can see or do; the model produces a candidate; evidence and human authority decide what happens next.

Concepts quick reference

Use this section when you need one definition. Each term points back to the connected system above.

Agentic AI

AI that can pursue a goal through multiple steps and tools with some autonomy. Judge its permissions, checkpoints, logs, environment and stop controls—not only its answer.

AGI

Artificial general intelligence: the disputed idea of broad capability and transfer across many unfamiliar intellectual tasks. There is no single agreed test or finish line.

AI winter

A period when AI enthusiasm, funding or institutional support falls after expectations outrun results. It is a historical pattern, not an automatic forecast.

Context

The material available for this response: prompts, instructions, conversation, files, retrieved passages or remembered details actually supplied by the product.

Context window

The bounded working input a model can use for one response. Products may select, truncate or summarize what enters it; it is working space, not training data.

Generative AI

AI that produces new content from learned patterns plus current instructions and material. Generating content does not prove that it is true or authorized.

Grounding

Connecting an answer to supplied or retrieved evidence so its claims can be traced and checked. A weak source can still produce a weak grounded answer.

Hallucination

False or unsupported content inside an AI-generated answer. It can be one wrong detail in otherwise useful work.

Knowledge cutoff

A model-specific boundary in information represented through training. For anything current, use a live official or primary source.

Model / large language model (LLM)

A model is a trained component that transforms input into output. An LLM is designed for language. Neither is the whole product.

Multimodal

A system that can work with more than one type of material, such as text and images. More input types do not guarantee accurate interpretation.

Prompt

What you give the AI to work from: a question, instruction, example, image, file or combination.

Reasoning model

A model or mode that uses additional computation for some multi-step tasks. More computation can help without certifying the sources or answer.

Sandbox

An isolated environment intended to limit what code or an automated system can access or affect. It limits consequences; it does not make output correct.

Retrieval

The step where a system searches for or fetches relevant material. Retrieval finds material; grounding connects claims to it.

Token

A small unit a model processes, often a word, part of a word or punctuation. Tokenization varies by model and language.

Training data

The examples used to adjust a model so its parameters can learn patterns. Material supplied today is current context, not automatically new training data.
