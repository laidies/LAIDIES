The useful AI words from across SUNNYVAiLE, translated in one place.

Use this book when somebody drops an AI term into a meeting and you need the LAiDIES translation quickly. Terms are arranged alphabetically. Every entry uses the analogy from the episode, class or reference lesson that taught it, explains why the word matters, and takes you back to the full lesson.

A

Agentic AI

In plain English. An AI system that can pursue a goal by choosing steps and using permitted tools—not only return one answer. Depending on its access, it might search, open files, update a spreadsheet, run code or complete a sequence of tasks.

The distinction to remember. Generative describes the content an AI produces. Agentic describes a system choosing steps and using tools. One system can be both.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Agentic AI — acts” maps goal, tools, permissions and actions through Cher’s closet computer, then shows the practical risk and approval pattern.

AGI

In plain English. Artificial general intelligence: the name for AI capability broad enough to perform across many kinds of intellectual work, rather than being limited to one narrow task or type of task.

The distinction to remember. AGI is a real research goal, but there is no single agreed finish line. Researchers and companies use different definitions, tests and thresholds—and disagree about whether any current system qualifies.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “AGI — the disputed threshold” shows the now-to-AGI ladder, explains why different definitions produce different claims and gives you the questions that make an “AGI” claim testable.

AI winter

In plain English. A long stretch when AI’s promises outran what the technology could actually do, enthusiasm and funding fell, and the field became much less fashionable to talk about.

Picture it. The money froze and the public story went quiet. The work did not disappear; researchers kept building through the winter until capability and interest thawed again.

Why you care. AI has had hype cycles before. A loud promise is not proof, and a setback is not proof the whole field has vanished.

Where to learn more. Episode 4 → “Why AI froze—and thawed” follows the promises, funding freeze and work that continued through the winter.

C

Context

In plain English. The background that makes the request about your actual situation instead of a generic one: who it is for, what happened, what matters and what the AI needs to see.

Picture it. Andy sees a blue sweater. Miranda Priestly sees cerulean, the designers who chose it and the route it took to a clearance bin. Context is the part that turns “blue” into this exact cerulean sweater.

Why you care. More instructions cannot replace missing information. If the answer feels generic, the AI may need the real email, policy, audience or decision—not a cleverer command.

Where to learn more. Episode 2 → “The brand-new café across town” teaches context through “the usual” at a place that has never met you.

Context window

In plain English. The amount of material an AI can work with at one time: your conversation, attached files, instructions and anything a tool has retrieved for the current task.

Picture it. The desk, not the whole library. You can pile a surprising amount onto it, but as the stacks grow, the page buried in the middle becomes harder to notice.

Why you care. A long chat can remain open while quietly losing the plot. When instructions drift or source details disappear, start a fresh chat with only the material and rules that still matter.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Token & context window” shows what fills the desk. Concepts 101 → “The context window degrades under load” shows why official capacity is not the same as reliable attention.

G

Generative AI

In plain English. AI that produces content from your request and the material you give it—an email, image, summary, slide, song, video or piece of code.

Picture it. Carrie Bradshaw with a brief. Give her the subject, audience, receipts and format, and she can write the column. She cannot make the receipts true.

Why you care. Generation and verification are different jobs. A polished answer can still be wrong, incomplete or missing the context that matters.

Keep this. It can make the draft. It cannot make it true.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Generative AI — makes” places it beside reasoning models, agentic AI and AGI so the difference is visible.

Grounding

In plain English. Giving the AI specific material to answer from—a policy, document, transcript, database result or live search—instead of asking it to rely only on patterns learned during training.

Picture it. An open-book test. You put the correct binder on the desk and say, “Answer from this, and show me the page.”

Why you care. Grounding makes an answer easier to check, but it does not make it automatically true. The source can still be stale, irrelevant or misread, so the answer needs to show which material supports which claim.

Where to learn more. Episode 3 → “David, Meet Elle” verification method turns grounding into a repeatable checking move. Concepts 101 → “How to reduce hallucinations” explains why provided evidence lowers risk without eliminating it.

H

Hallucination

In plain English. False or unsupported content delivered as part of an AI answer. It can be one invented citation or wrong date inside otherwise useful work.

Picture it. The Burn Book: every entry was invented, and every one arrived with the same completely unbothered confidence. The tone did not come with a warning label.

Why you care. You cannot hear the difference between a supported sentence and an invented one. Anything important needs receipts.

Keep this. Polished is a style. Evidence is a standard.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Why hallucinations happen” explains the mechanism. Episode 3 → “The Burn Book Problem” shows why polished nonsense is so easy to trust.

K

Knowledge cutoff

In plain English. The date after which a model’s training no longer contains new events or information. Anything newer must be supplied through current context, retrieval or a live tool.

Picture it. A friend who watched every episode of Sex and the City through the 2004 finale and then stopped. She can know that era perfectly and still know nothing about And Just Like That.

Why you care. A model can answer a current question in the present tense without having current information. Prices, laws, product features, people and news need a dated source—not confidence.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “The cutoff, explained” shows the frozen view of the world and the three ways to bring newer information in.

M

Model / large language model (LLM)

In plain English. The trained component that turns an input into an output. The output might be words or images, a prediction or a choice about what to do next. A large language model, or LLM, is designed to work with language.

Picture it. Episode 05’s fashion house: the model is the house’s supermodel. You might meet it through the house’s own boutique, a department store, or a stylist using several houses. The picture helps separate the model from the place you use it; it is not a technical blueprint.

Why you care. The product around a model can add instructions, memory, search, files, tools and other models. Changing either the model or the system around it can change the result.

Keep this. A model is part of the experience—not the whole thing.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Model / LLM” explains what changes when you switch the model, interface or system around it.

Multimodal

In plain English. Able to work across more than one kind of material—such as text, images, audio or video—inside the same model or task.

Picture it. One colleague who can read the memo, inspect the screenshot, listen to the call and then draft the follow-up. The modes are the different ways information arrives.

Why you care. A multimodal tool can use the actual screenshot or recording instead of depending on your description of it. That can remove ambiguity, but you still need to check what it noticed, missed or inferred.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Training data” shows how multimodal models learn across text, images, audio and code—and why seeing more formats is not the same as understanding them like a person.

P

Prompt

In plain English. What you give the AI to work from: your question, instruction, request, pasted material or attached file.

Picture it. Moira says, “fold in the cheese.” David Rose says, “What does that mean?” A vague prompt leaves AI in David’s kitchen, holding the bowl and guessing.

Why you care. The AI cannot recover details you never gave it. Tell it the job, the audience, the constraints and what a useful result should look like.

Where to learn more. Episode 2 → “The David Rose School of Prompting” shows the vague and useful versions side by side. Briefing 101 → “The Anatomy of a Brief” turns that lesson into a reusable structure.

R

Reasoning model

In plain English. A model designed to spend more computation working through a problem before it gives the final answer.

Picture it. The Watcher in the library. She does not shout the first fluent thought from the doorway; she works the whole problem through before telling the Slayer where to go.

Why you care. Reasoning models are useful for logic, maths, planning and multi-step decisions, but slower does not mean infallible. Use the extra work where the difficulty or stakes justify it, then check the result.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Reasoning models — think first” contrasts them with generative and agentic AI. Concepts 101 → “Which one when” turns the difference into a practical choice.

Retrieval

In plain English. The step where an AI system searches outside its trained model and brings relevant material into the current task before generating an answer.

Picture it. Sending the librarian to fetch the likely binder. Retrieval is the trip to the shelves; grounding is making the answer use what came back.

Why you care. Retrieval can bring in current or private information, but it can also fetch the wrong page, a stale result or a source that never proves the claim. “It searched” is not the same as “it found the right evidence.”

Where to learn more. Episode 3 → “The Burn Book Problem” shows why source-connected systems can still mix the wrong information into one polished answer. Episode 3 → “David, Meet Elle” verification method shows how to check what was retrieved before trusting the result.

T

Token

In plain English. A small chunk of text the model processes. A token may be a whole word, part of a word or punctuation.

LAiDIES translation. People count pages and words; AI systems count smaller pieces. Those pieces are what fill the space available in a chat.

Why you care. Long conversations and large files use more of that space. If a huge chat becomes forgetful or vague, it may be carrying too much at once. You do not need to count tokens by hand.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Token & context window” explains how the pieces fill a chat’s working memory and why long conversations drift.

Training data

In plain English. The examples used to train a model so it can learn patterns: text, images, audio, code or other material, depending on the job.

Picture it. Fei-Fei Li helped build ImageNet because a machine could not learn to recognize the visual world without being shown an enormous, carefully-labelled collection of it.

Why you care. What a model learned from shapes what it can recognize, produce and miss. Training data is not the same as the file you attach to a chat today; that file is current context.

Where to learn more. Concepts 101 → “Training data” explains what the model learned from and why that differs from a file attached today. Episode 4 → “Fei-Fei Li and ImageNet” shows the idea in the history that changed machine vision.
