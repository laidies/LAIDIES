# Vocab 101

The useful AI words from across SUNNYVAiLE, translated in one place.

Use this book when somebody drops an AI term into a meeting and you need the
LAiDIES translation quickly. Terms are arranged alphabetically. Every entry
uses the analogy from the episode, class or reference lesson that taught it,
explains why the word matters, and takes you back to the full lesson.

## A

### Agentic AI

**In plain English.** AI that can take a goal, choose steps and use tools to
act—not only write an answer. Depending on its permissions, it might search,
open files, update a spreadsheet, run code or complete a sequence of tasks.

**The distinction to remember.** *Generative* describes the content an AI
produces. *Agentic* describes a system that can choose steps and use permitted
tools in pursuit of a goal. A system can be both.

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Agentic AI —
acts”](/library.html#concepts-101::3.%20Agentic%20AI%20%E2%80%94%20acts)
maps goal, tools, permissions and actions through Cher’s closet computer, then
shows the practical risk and approval pattern.

### AGI

**In plain English.** Artificial general intelligence: the name for AI
capability broad enough to perform across many kinds of intellectual work,
rather than being limited to one narrow task or type of task.

**The distinction to remember.** AGI is a real research goal, but there is no
single agreed finish line. Researchers and companies use different definitions,
tests and thresholds—and disagree about whether any current system qualifies.

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “AGI —
the disputed threshold”](/library.html#concepts-101::4.%20AGI%20%E2%80%94%20the%20disputed%20threshold)
shows the now-to-AGI ladder, explains why different definitions produce
different claims and gives you the questions that make an “AGI” claim testable.

### AI winter

**In plain English.** A long stretch when AI’s promises outran what the
technology could actually do, enthusiasm and funding fell, and the field became
much less fashionable to talk about.

**Picture it.** The money froze and the public story went quiet. The work did
not disappear; researchers kept building through the winter until capability
and interest thawed again.

**Why you care.** AI has had hype cycles before. A loud promise is not proof,
and a setback is not proof the whole field has vanished.

**Where to learn more.** [Episode 4 → “Why AI froze—and
thawed”](/issues/issue-04.html#ai-winter-story) follows the promises, funding
freeze and work that continued through the winter.

## C

### Context

**In plain English.** The background that makes the request about your actual
situation instead of a generic one: who it is for, what happened, what matters
and what the AI needs to see.

**Picture it.** Andy sees a blue sweater. Miranda Priestly sees cerulean, the
designers who chose it and the route it took to a clearance bin. Context is the
part that turns “blue” into this exact cerulean sweater.

**Why you care.** More instructions cannot replace missing information. If the
answer feels generic, the AI may need the real email, policy, audience or
decision—not a cleverer command.

**Where to learn more.** [Episode 2 → “The brand-new café across
town”](/issues/issue-02.html#context-explained) teaches context through “the
usual” at a place that has never met you.

### Context window

**In plain English.** The amount of material an AI can work with at one time:
your conversation, attached files, instructions and anything a tool has
retrieved for the current task.

**Picture it.** The desk, not the whole library. You can pile a surprising
amount onto it, but as the stacks grow, the page buried in the middle becomes
harder to notice.

**Why you care.** A long chat can remain open while quietly losing the plot.
When instructions drift or source details disappear, start a fresh chat with
only the material and rules that still matter.

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Token & context
window”](/library.html#concepts-101::Token%20%26%20context%20window) shows what
fills the desk. [Concepts 101 → “The context window degrades under
load”](/library.html#concepts-101::The%20context%20window%20degrades%20under%20load)
shows why official capacity is not the same as reliable attention.

## G

### Generative AI

**In plain English.** AI that produces content from your request and the
material you give it—an email, image, summary, slide, song, video or piece of
code. It works from patterns learned during training plus the context
available now.

**Picture it.** Carrie Bradshaw with a brief. Give her the subject, audience,
receipts and format; she can write the column. She cannot make the receipts
true.

**Why you care.** Generation and verification are different jobs. The same
product may create an answer and search for sources, but a link beside a claim
is still an invitation to check—not a truth stamp. **It can make the draft. It
cannot make it true.**

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Generative AI —
makes”](/library.html#concepts-101::1.%20Generative%20AI%20%E2%80%94%20makes)
places it beside reasoning models, agentic AI and AGI so the difference is
visible.

### Grounding

**In plain English.** Giving the AI specific material to answer from—a policy,
document, transcript, database result or live search—instead of asking it to
rely only on patterns learned during training.

**Picture it.** An open-book test. You put the correct binder on the desk and
say, “Answer from this, and show me the page.”

**Why you care.** Grounding makes an answer easier to check, but it does not
make it automatically true. The source can still be stale, irrelevant or
misread, so the answer needs to show which material supports which claim.

**Where to learn more.** [Episode 3 → “David, Meet Elle” verification
method](/issues/issue-03.html#verification-method) turns grounding into a
repeatable checking move. [Concepts 101 → “How to reduce
hallucinations”](/library.html#concepts-101::How%20to%20reduce%20hallucinations)
explains why provided evidence lowers risk without eliminating it.

## H

### Hallucination

**In plain English.** False or unsupported content delivered as part of an
AI-generated answer. It might be one invented citation, wrong date or made-up
detail inside otherwise useful work—not only a completely fictional response.

**Picture it.** The Burn Book: every entry was invented, every entry arrived
with Regina George confidence, and the tone supplied no warning label.

**Why you care.** Fluent writing makes supported and unsupported sentences
sound equally finished. Anything important needs receipts. **Polished is a
style. Evidence is a standard.**

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Why hallucinations
happen”](/library.html#concepts-101::Why%20hallucinations%20happen) explains
the mechanism. [Episode 3 → “The Burn Book
Problem”](/issues/issue-03.html#burn-book-problem) shows why polished nonsense
is so easy to trust.

## K

### Knowledge cutoff

**In plain English.** The date after which a model’s training no longer
contains new events or information. Anything newer must be supplied through
current context, retrieval or a live tool.

**Picture it.** A friend who watched every episode of *Sex and the City*
through the 2004 finale and then stopped. She can know that era perfectly and
still know nothing about *And Just Like That*.

**Why you care.** A model can answer a current question in the present tense
without having current information. Prices, laws, product features, people and
news need a dated source—not confidence.

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “The cutoff,
explained”](/library.html#concepts-101::The%20cutoff%2C%20explained) shows the
frozen view of the world and the three ways to bring newer information in.

## M

### Model / large language model (LLM)

**In plain English.** The trained component of an AI system that turns an
input into an output. It may produce words or images, interpret material, make
a prediction or choose a next action. A large language model, or LLM, is a
model designed to work with language.

**Picture it.** Episode 05's fashion house: the provider is the house, the
model is one of its supermodels, and an app is a boutique or department store
where you may encounter one or more of them. The picture helps separate the
parts; the software is not literally a fashion business.

**Why you care.** You may reach a model through an app, API or coding tool.
The product around it can add instructions, memory, search, files and other
models. So “Which AI is best?” has no permanent one-word answer. **A model is
part of the experience—not the whole thing.**

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Model /
LLM”](/library.html#concepts-101::Model%20%2F%20LLM) explains what changes when
you switch the engine, the interface or the system around it.

### Multimodal

**In plain English.** Able to work across more than one kind of material—such
as text, images, audio or video—inside the same model or task.

**Picture it.** One colleague who can read the memo, inspect the screenshot,
listen to the call and then draft the follow-up. The modes are the different
ways information arrives.

**Why you care.** A multimodal tool can use the actual screenshot or recording
instead of depending on your description of it. That can remove ambiguity, but
you still need to check what it noticed, missed or inferred.

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Training
data”](/library.html#concepts-101::Training%20data) shows how multimodal models
learn across text, images, audio and code—and why seeing more formats is not
the same as understanding them like a person.

## P

### Prompt

**In plain English.** What you give the AI to work from: your question,
instruction, request, pasted material or attached file.

**Picture it.** Moira says, “fold in the cheese.” David Rose says, “What does
that mean?” A vague prompt leaves AI in David’s kitchen, holding the bowl and
guessing.

**Why you care.** The AI cannot recover details you never gave it. Tell it the
job, the audience, the constraints and what a useful result should look like.

**Where to learn more.** [Episode 2 → “The David Rose School of
Prompting”](/issues/issue-02.html#david-rose-school-of-prompting) shows the
vague and useful versions side by side. [Briefing 101 → “The Anatomy of a
Brief”](/library.html#briefing-101::The%20Anatomy%20of%20a%20Brief) turns that
lesson into a reusable structure.

## R

### Reasoning model

**In plain English.** A model designed to spend more computation working
through a problem before it gives the final answer.

**Picture it.** The Watcher in the library. She does not shout the first fluent
thought from the doorway; she works the whole problem through before telling
the Slayer where to go.

**Why you care.** Reasoning models are useful for logic, maths, planning and
multi-step decisions, but slower does not mean infallible. Use the extra work
where the difficulty or stakes justify it, then check the result.

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Reasoning models — think
first”](/library.html#concepts-101::2.%20Reasoning%20models%20%E2%80%94%20think%20first)
contrasts them with generative and agentic AI. [Concepts 101 → “Which one
when”](/library.html#concepts-101::Which%20one%20when) turns the difference
into a practical choice.

### Retrieval

**In plain English.** The step where an AI system searches outside its trained
model and brings relevant material into the current task before generating an
answer.

**Picture it.** Sending the librarian to fetch the likely binder. Retrieval is
the trip to the shelves; grounding is making the answer use what came back.

**Why you care.** Retrieval can bring in current or private information, but it
can also fetch the wrong page, a stale result or a source that never proves the
claim. “It searched” is not the same as “it found the right evidence.”

**Where to learn more.** [Episode 3 → “The Burn Book
Problem”](/issues/issue-03.html#burn-book-problem) shows why source-connected
systems can still mix the wrong information into one polished answer. [Episode
3 → “David, Meet Elle” verification
method](/issues/issue-03.html#verification-method) shows how to check what was
retrieved before trusting the result.

## T

### Token

**In plain English.** A small chunk of text the model processes. A token may be
a whole word, part of a word or punctuation.

**LAiDIES translation.** People count pages and words; AI systems count smaller
pieces. Those pieces are what fill the space available in a chat.

**Why you care.** Long conversations and large files use more of that space. If
a huge chat becomes forgetful or vague, it may be carrying too much at once.
You do not need to count tokens by hand.

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Token & context
window”](/library.html#concepts-101::Token%20%26%20context%20window) explains
how the pieces fill a chat’s working memory and why long conversations drift.

### Training data

**In plain English.** The examples used to train a model so it can learn
patterns: text, images, audio, code or other material, depending on the job.

**Picture it.** Fei-Fei Li helped build ImageNet because a machine could not
learn to recognize the visual world without being shown an enormous,
carefully-labelled collection of it.

**Why you care.** What a model learned from shapes what it can recognize,
produce and miss. Training data is not the same as the file you attach to a
chat today; that file is current context.

**Where to learn more.** [Concepts 101 → “Training
data”](/library.html#concepts-101::Training%20data) explains what the model
learned from and why that is different from a file you attach today. [Episode
4 → “Fei-Fei Li and ImageNet”](/issues/issue-04.html#training-data-story)
shows the idea in the history that changed machine vision.

## How new words enter Vocab 101

Episodes, classes and reference lessons can all introduce a term. Recurring
NewsStand language begins as dated **Current Vocab** beside the story; a term
moves into this book only when it is durable, useful beyond one headline and
has a proper LAiDIES explanation.

General computer literacy belongs in its own book if readers need it. It does
not get mixed into the AI terms here.
