### 1. From “ARGHH, WTF?” to “Ahh. That’s why.”

AI is unusual because you do not simply switch it on and receive one stable, pre-determined result.

Think about electric light. You flick a switch and the circuit does its job. You do not need to brief the bulb on the mood, provide three examples of successful illumination or ask it to make the room “brighter, but less overhead-office-break-room.” Electricity transformed society, but most of us can benefit from a light without understanding power generation, grid balancing or what Nikola Tesla and Thomas Edison were arguing about.

AI asks more of you at the point of use.

The application can generate, analyse, compare, plan, classify, search or take actions through connected tools. That range is exactly what makes it exciting. It is also why “I pressed the AI button” is not a complete method.

The system needs a job. It needs the relevant context. It may need examples, constraints, source material, access to tools and a clear idea of what a useful result looks like. Then **you** need enough understanding to notice whether the result fits the job, whether the information is current and whether the system has quietly made something up in a tone usually reserved for a person holding a clipboard.

That is the difference between plug-and-play and **brief, inspect and steer**.

LAiDIES itself is a useful example. AI can help us research, write code, test an idea, examine a claim or create an early visual direction. It cannot be told “make a brilliant learning town for women” and left alone for the afternoon. Believe us. We have the rejected beige rectangles to prove it.

To get a useful result, we have to tell the system what we are building, who it is for, what is already true, which decisions are locked, what good looks like and what absolutely must not happen. We inspect what it produces. We correct the method when it fails. We keep the judgment.

Once you understand the parts of an AI system, a bad result becomes less mysterious. You can ask:

- Did I give it the information it needed?
- Is the model capable of this job?
- Did the product retrieve the right material?
- Did a tool fail?
- Was my instruction vague?
- Did the system produce a plausible answer where it needed actual evidence?

Instead of “this thing is useless,” you get to “ohhh, *that* is what happened, and I know what to change.”

That is a very different relationship with the technology. You are no longer standing outside the machine, pressing the same button with increasing hostility. You can open the hood, locate the problem and decide whether to repair the approach, use another tool or decline to use AI for that job at all.

No, dear reader, thou shalt not forsake the AI after one terrible answer. Thou shalt first check whether the poor thing was given a six-word instruction, no context and a task requiring information it could not possibly have.

And if the system really is wrong for the job? Excellent. Knowing when **not** to use AI is part of understanding it too.

### 2. From “OMG, the end is nigh!” to “Ugh, as if.”

The second reason is the noise.

AI is developing quickly. Companies want investment, customers, influence and market share. Researchers publish new findings. Governments propose rules. Workers report real benefits and real harms. Every day, all of that gets compressed into headlines, posts, videos and a man on LinkedIn announcing that three prompts have “changed work forever.”

Some of it is excellent. Some of it is advertising with a graph. Some of it belongs in the tabloids between a royal feud and a photograph of a celebrity apparently buying oat milk while “looking tense.”

The result is emotional whiplash.

One story says AI is about to solve disease, climate change and your calendar. The next says your profession will be gone by Thursday. A product demonstration looks astonishing, but nobody mentions that it was selected from fifty attempts. A model fails one test, and suddenly the entire field is declared dead. A model passes one test, and Skynet is apparently circling for parking.

You cannot make sense of those claims by memorising which company released what this week. The names will change. The prices will change. The rankings will change before somebody has finished making the comparison chart.

The fundamentals travel.

If you understand the difference between a model and a product, you can see why two applications using related technology behave differently. If you understand training and inference, you can spot when somebody claims a chatbot “learned” something it merely held in the current conversation. If you understand agents, tools and permissions, you can ask whether the system actually took an action or merely suggested one. If you understand evaluation, you can look at a dazzling benchmark and ask the deeply unfashionable but essential question: **what, exactly, did this test prove?**

That is what we do at the LAiDIES NewsStand. We take a live story, separate the confirmed facts from the company framing and the dramatic interpretation, then connect it back to the durable concepts in this book. Eventually, you should be able to do the same thing without us.

Not because we want to put the NewsStand out of business. Please continue visiting. The staff have outfits planned.

Because AI literacy should give you independent judgment. When a creator says “DM me for the secret prompt,” you should be able to tell whether it is a genuinely useful technique, a familiar idea in a new tube of lip gloss or complete nonsense with excellent lighting.

### 3. From “Whatever!” to “RSVP: Yes. I have notes.”

Once you understand how AI works, you can see that the important questions are much bigger than which chatbot writes the nicest email.

The invitations to those questions are already arriving. They just do not look like invitations.

They look like a meeting about introducing AI into your team. An employee consultation about automated monitoring. A city-council notice for a proposed data centre. A school-board discussion about AI in classrooms. A new public rule covering systems that rank, recommend or make decisions about people.

There is no cream card in the post. Nobody has written **AI POLICY DISCUSSION** in gold calligraphy. But that is what these moments are: invitations to help decide where AI belongs, what evidence should be required, which risks need guardrails and who remains accountable.

**RSVP: Yes** does not mean saying yes to every AI system. Absolutely not. It means saying yes to the discussion. Yes, I would like to see the evidence. Yes, I have questions about who benefits and who carries the risk. Yes, I noticed that the word *innovation* has been doing suspiciously heavy lifting in this proposal.

AI systems already influence what people see, which applications receive attention, how work is assigned, where fraud is investigated and which decisions get made faster. Building and running AI also requires physical infrastructure: chips, servers, networks, cooling equipment, data centres and electricity. The “cloud” is not a celestial mist. It is somebody else’s computer, in a real place, attached to a power grid.

That means AI decisions show up everywhere.

At work: Which tasks should be automated? Which should remain human? What happens to the person whose performance is being ranked by a system she cannot question? Who is accountable when the output is wrong?

In your community: Should a new data centre be approved? What would it require from the local grid and water system? Which jobs, tax revenue or infrastructure might it bring? Which claims are supported by the actual proposal, and which arrived via your Aunt Linda, whose friend’s twenty-year-old cat died shortly after construction began?

Linda means well. Linda has not established causation.

The joke does not make the real questions frivolous. Data centres use real electricity and can create local costs and benefits. That is precisely why the discussion deserves better than “technology always wins” on one side and “the data centre killed Mr Whiskers” on the other.

At the level of governments and society, the questions become larger still. Who controls the most capable models? Who gets access to the computing power needed to build them? Which uses require rules, audits or the right to appeal? How should education, employment and public services change when machines can perform more cognitive work? Which benefits are broadly shared, and which costs are quietly handed to somebody else?

You do not need to build a model to have a serious view on any of this. But you do need enough understanding to distinguish the model from the product, the capability from the marketing, the demonstrated harm from the imagined one and the technical constraint from the political choice.

Karen Spärck Jones, one of the foundational figures in information retrieval, had a line we keep close in SUNNYVAiLE: **“Computing is too important to be left to men.”**

Ditto for AI.

And, frankly, it is too important to be left entirely to the loudest people in the room, regardless of gender. The systems will be shaped by the people who understand enough to ask good questions, challenge bad assumptions and insist that the consequences count.

That includes you.

Not as an inspirational slogan. As a practical requirement.
