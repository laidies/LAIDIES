Chapter 1: The different types of AI and how they fit together

The words have escaped the lab and are now breeding in PowerPoint. And we bet you two bucks that the person giving the presentation likely doesn’t know what many of these terms actually mean.

Predictive AI. Generative AI. Multimodal AI. Agentic AI. Embodied AI. Narrow AI. General-purpose AI. AGI. ASI. You hear one term in a product launch, another in the news and four more in a meeting where everyone is nodding with the serene confidence of people who absolutely plan to Google them later.

If the list feels confusing, you have not missed one basic lesson that everyone else understood. The language really is tangled. Engineers may use a label to describe how a system was built. A product team may use one to describe what the system does. A researcher may be talking about the forms of information it can process. A keynote speaker may be making a claim about how intelligent future systems could become. Then all four call their answer a “type of AI” and place it in the same slide deck. Excellent. Very helpful.

Start with one AI system

Before we sort out the words, let’s start with the thing they are trying to describe.

An AI system turns input into an inferred output

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the broad field of building machine-based systems that infer outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions from inputs.

An AI system is the working arrangement that receives information—an input—uses represented patterns, rules or relationships to infer a result—an output—and makes that result available for use. Your spam filter receives an email and estimates whether it belongs in spam. A chatbot receives your words and produces a response. A warehouse robot receives sensor information and may use an AI result to help control a physical movement. All count as AI systems; clearly, they are not doing the same job or carrying the same stakes.

One piece of vocabulary will save us trouble immediately. A model is the component that performs an inference. An AI system makes that calculation usable with inputs, software and a process for the output. The finished product is what someone actually uses; it may add an interface, stored information, tools, permissions, ordinary software and people. The distinction matters because “the model can classify an image” does not prove “the product handles a customer claim safely.”

Now imagine a customer-service assistant. A customer sends an email and a photograph of a damaged chair. One part estimates the problem and urgency. Another writes an explanation. The system works with both image and text. If a person approves and the assistant continues into the service system to prepare a ticket, it may work through several steps instead of stopping after one answer.

That one product could therefore be described as predictive, generative, multimodal and agentic. Those words are not four rival species. Each reveals something different:

  • Predictive tells you that part of the system estimates what is likely.
  • Generative tells you that part of it produces content.
  • Multimodal tells you that it works with more than one form of information.
  • Agentic tells you that it may continue through steps and actions.

The distinctions matter in real life. If a system is generative, a polished answer may have been produced rather than retrieved from a checked source. If it is agentic, permissions and stopping points suddenly matter because the system may do something after answering. If someone calls it AGI, she is making a much larger and far more disputed claim about the breadth and adaptability of its intelligence.

So we are not going to memorise one giant list. We are going to untangle six different questions people have bundled inside the phrase type of AI:

  1. What job does it perform?
  2. What forms of information can it handle?
  3. Does it stop after one result or continue through steps and actions?
  4. Does it interact with the physical world?
  5. How broad and adaptable are its abilities?
  6. How was the capability built?

Some answers genuinely nest inside others. Machine learning sits inside the broader AI field; neural networks sit inside machine learning; deep learning sits inside the neural-network family. Other answers can overlap in one product. A generative system can be multimodal. An agentic system may use generative and predictive models. An embodied robot may be highly specialised. None of those combinations automatically makes it AGI.

The diagram below uses a customer-service assistant to give you the whole relationship once. Then we will take it apart slowly, with actual examples, and put it back together at the end. No mystery labels. No taxonomy Jenga.

Tell Me More about an AI system: what “infer” means

To infer is to work out a result from the information, patterns or rules available rather than retrieve one answer that a person wrote for this exact case.

Take the spam filter. Ordinary software delivers the email and displays the subject line. The AI part estimates whether this particular message belongs in spam. The result is not floating in the email waiting to be discovered with tweezers. The system applies represented rules or learned patterns to the current input and produces a classification.

Inference does not mean the system reasoned like a person, understood the email or reached the truth. It names the move from input to calculated output.

Full Nerd Alert on an AI system: where the system boundary sits

Where we draw the system boundary depends on the question. An engineer evaluating model accuracy may isolate the model and its test inputs. Someone evaluating the complete service must include which photograph reaches the model, how its score is converted into a decision, whether a person can correct it and which action follows.

That is why two accurate reports can appear to discuss “the AI” while measuring different things. Chapter 2 will unpack every component. For now, keep the boxes separate: a model performs a calculation; a system makes that calculation operational; a product gives someone a way to use it.

What job does the AI perform?

The first question is about the result the system produces. Two of the labels you will hear most often are predictive and generative.

Predictive AI estimates what is likely

Predictive AI is a broad public label for systems that estimate a likely category, value, outcome or order from available information.

You were using predictive AI long before everyone started asking chatbots to write poems. Spam filters, fraud alerts, arrival-time estimates, search rankings and streaming recommendations all involve some form of estimation.

Four common predictive jobs are related, but they are not interchangeable:

Predictive jobWhat it estimatesEveryday example
Classification or recognitionWhich category an input most likely belongs to“Probably spam”; “probably a dog”; “probably the same face”
Value or outcome predictionAn unknown or future value or outcome“Likely arrival time”; “chance this transaction is fraudulent”
RankingThe relative order of several optionsWhich search result, song or product appears first
RecommendationWhich option or options may be most relevant“You may want to watch this next”

Here, value or outcome prediction is one job inside the larger family called Predictive AI. The family label covers systems that classify, estimate, rank or recommend by calculating likely outcomes; it does not mean every predictive system only forecasts what happens next.

Think of a Friday night at Blockbuster. The clerk classifies Scream as horror, predicts that your mother will absolutely refuse to watch it, ranks the remaining choices and recommends Clueless. Four related moves; four different jobs inside the larger predictive family.

An AI product can connect them in the same order. It may classify hundreds of films, predict how relevant each one is to you, rank the candidates and recommend the top few. The recommendation is what you see. Several predictive steps may sit behind it.

This is useful because each step can fail differently. The system can assign the wrong category, make a weak prediction, rank for a goal you dislike or recommend something using incomplete information. “The recommendation was bad” describes the visible result. It does not tell you where the problem began.

Predictive does not mean psychic, certain or capable of predicting everything. The system is estimating from the information and patterns available to it. A probability wearing lip gloss is still a probability.

Tell Me More about Predictive AI: how clues become a score

Let’s take the fraud alert that appears when your bank thinks a purchase looks suspicious. The system has not developed a tiny personal grudge against your new boots. It has been given clues: perhaps the amount, time, location, shop and recent activity on the account.

In machine learning, those clues are often called features. The model has learned relationships from earlier examples and uses them to produce a score for this new purchase. A higher score might mean “looks more like the suspicious examples”; a lower one might mean “looks more like the ordinary examples.”

The score still does nothing by itself. The bank chooses a cutoff. Above that cutoff, the purchase might be blocked or sent for review. Below it, the purchase may proceed. That cutoff is called a threshold.

So there are two different decisions hiding inside one fraud alert:

  1. the model estimates how suspicious the purchase looks; and
  2. people and product software decide what to do with that estimate.

That is why two banks can use similar models and still interrupt different purchases. The maths produces an estimate. People choose the policy around it.

Full Nerd Alert on Predictive AI: what makes a score trustworthy?

Suppose a fraud model gives genuine frauds higher scores than ordinary purchases most of the time. That is useful: it means the model is generally putting the riskier cases nearer the top of the review pile. In model evaluation, the technical word for that sorting ability is discrimination. Here it means distinguishing and ranking cases; it is not the everyday or legal meaning of unfair treatment, which is a different and equally important question.

Now suppose the model labels a group of purchases as having a 70 per cent chance of fraud. If its numbers are dependable, about seven out of ten comparable purchases should actually turn out to be fraudulent. That match between the number and what happens is called calibration.

A model can be good at one and bad at the other. It might put the riskiest purchases first while wildly exaggerating their percentages. Think: correct order, untrustworthy numbers.

There is another catch. During training, builders decide what “better” means. A fraud system rewarded for catching nearly every fraud may also block more legitimate shopping. A recommendation system rewarded for keeping you watching may learn what prevents you from going to bed, not what you will be glad you watched.

The technical name for the target being improved is the objective. It is not a law discovered inside the data. It is a choice made by people. So when a score looks impressively precise, ask: precise at predicting what, under which conditions, and in service of whose goal?

Generative AI produces new content

Generative AI produces content: text, images, audio, video, code or other digital material. When you ask for a draft, illustration or summary, the system creates an output using patterns learned during training plus the information and instructions available for your request.

It is not usually searching a filing cabinet for one completed answer that was stored there waiting for you. It produces the result as it goes. That is why the same request can yield different wording and why small changes in context can matter.

Generated does not mean true, original or good. It means the system produced this output instead of only retrieving one finished item. The output may still repeat common patterns, clichés, biases or errors. If you request a campaign slogan and receive “Unlock Your Potential,” the system has technically generated content. Creativity has filed a grievance.

Ask an AI assistant to turn your meeting notes into an email and you can watch the job happen: your notes and instruction become the input; the system produces a new draft for this request. That is useful because you can begin with a tailored result instead of a blank page. It also means the polished paragraph is generated output, not proof that every name, date or promise inside it is correct.

Predictive and generative describe different visible jobs:

  • Predictive AI estimates a category, value, outcome or order.
  • Generative AI produces content.

A product may do both. It might predict which support issue a customer has and then generate a reply.

Tell Me More about Generative AI: how prediction produces a paragraph

Generative AI is not being placed inside the public product category Predictive AI. The confusion comes from the ordinary verb predict, which also names one mathematical operation inside a language model.

You may have seen your phone suggest the next word while you type. A language model does something related on a vastly more capable scale. It looks at the text available so far and asks, “Given all the patterns I learned, what small piece of text could sensibly come next?”

That small piece is called a token. A token may be a whole word, part of a word or a punctuation mark. The model chooses a token, adds it to the text and asks the next-word question again. Then again. And again. Those repeated choices build the response one piece at a time.

So there are two statements at different levels:

  • Generative AI describes the product job you see: the system produces a new draft.
  • Next-token prediction describes one internal calculation used by a generative language model: it repeatedly estimates what token could come next.

That internal calculation does not make Generative AI a subtype of Predictive AI in the classification this chapter is teaching. One is the visible content-producing job; the other is a mechanism inside a particular kind of generative model.

The distinction matters because a well-predicted next word is not the same thing as a well-researched fact. The model is building a plausible continuation. It is not automatically checking whether Janet really approved the budget.

Full Nerd Alert on Generative AI: how the model chooses the next token

At each step, the model does not see one inevitable next token. It scores the full set of tokens it could produce, giving each a different estimated likelihood. That complete set of possibilities and scores is called a probability distribution.

Imagine the sentence: “She opened the fridge and discovered...” Milk might be a very ordinary continuation. A penguin is less likely. An unpaid electricity bill would be a bold narrative choice. The model has many possible next pieces, not one sentence hidden in a vault.

The product then uses a decoding method to narrow and choose from that distribution. Some methods favour the most likely options very strongly. Others allow more variety. A setting often called temperature can influence how adventurous that choice becomes: lower settings usually concentrate attention on the likelier options; higher settings can make less likely options easier to select. The exact effect depends on the product and its other settings.

Once a token is chosen, it becomes part of the text the model sees for its next prediction. One slightly odd choice can therefore steer the later sentence in a new direction. Hundreds of choices can become a polished answer that no person wrote in advance.

This explains both the magic and the menace. The response can be fresh, fluent and useful because it is assembled for you. It can also be fresh, fluent and completely wrong because next-token selection is not a built-in truth detector.

What forms of information can it handle?

This question is not about the job. It is about the forms of information the system can receive or produce.

Multimodal AI works across more than one modality

A modality is a form of information, such as text, image, audio, video or sensor data.

A text-only system works with one modality. A multimodal AI system can work with more than one. It might receive a photograph and a spoken question, then produce text or speech.

Multimodal does not compete with predictive or generative because it answers a different question:

  • Predictive or generative describes the job.
  • Multimodal describes the forms of information involved.

Imagine uploading a photograph of a damaged chair and asking an assistant to draft a repair request. Examining the image and reading your instructions makes the interaction multimodal. Writing the request makes it generative. If the system also estimates the severity of the damage, that part is predictive.

One interaction; three truthful labels; three different properties.

Multimodal does not mean the system understands the scene as a person does. It tells you which forms of information it can process, not whether its interpretation is correct.

Tell Me More about Multimodal AI: how an image and words meet

Return to the photograph of the damaged chair. You can look at the picture, read “Is this safe to sit on?” and connect the word this to the chair without consciously translating either one into computer-friendly form.

The model cannot begin there. The image and sentence arrive in different formats, so each must be turned into patterns of numbers the model can work with. The image may be broken into small regions. The sentence is broken into tokens. Learned parts of the system turn those pieces into numerical representations—computer-usable descriptions of patterns in the image and text.

The useful part is not that the product accepted two uploads. It is that the model can connect them: chair with the object in the photograph, crack with the damaged joint and safe to sit on with the question being asked.

That is the deeper meaning of multimodal: not several files sitting politely beside one another, but information from different forms being related well enough to help perform one task.

Full Nerd Alert on Multimodal AI: why the connection can go wrong

Words, images and sound organise information differently. In a sentence, order matters. In an image, position matters. In speech, timing matters. A multimodal system has to preserve those differences and work out which pieces belong together.

The component that first turns one kind of input into a useful representation is often called an encoder. You can think of its job as preparing that information for the model—not translating it into English, but converting it into numerical patterns the system has learned to use.

The system also needs to decide what deserves attention. If you ask about the crack in a chair, the damaged joint matters more than the floral wallpaper behind it. An attention mechanism helps the model give different weight to different pieces and relationships. It is a mathematical focusing process, not the model consciously looking harder.

And it can focus on the wrong thing. The model may connect crack to a line in the wallpaper, miss faint text on a label or treat two nearby objects as one. That is why a clearer crop, a direct question and the original document can matter. Multimodal gives the system more kinds of information; it also gives the system more ways to connect them incorrectly.

Does it stop after one answer or keep working?

Now we are asking how the complete system operates after it produces one result.

Agentic AI continues through steps and actions

Ask a typical chatbot to draft an itinerary and it produces an answer. You decide what to do next.

An agentic AI system can continue through a task. It selects a next step, may use a tool, observes what happened, updates what it knows about the task and continues until it reaches a stopping point or needs help.

Compare these two results:

  1. “Here is a suggested itinerary for Montreal.”
  2. “I checked the dates you approved, searched the permitted booking service, compared the available flights with your budget, asked you to choose between the two reasonable options and—after you confirmed—submitted the booking.”

The first system generated an answer. The second continued through a sequence of steps, observations and permission-bound actions. That continued loop is the important agentic idea.

Agentic does not mean alive, conscious, correct or free to do whatever it likes. Its available tools, permissions, instructions, checkpoints and stopping conditions are designed by people. The autonomy is delegated and bounded, even when someone has designed the boundaries badly.

Generative and agentic can overlap, but neither contains the other:

  • A generative chatbot can write one answer and stop. It is generative but not meaningfully agentic.
  • An agentic system may use a generative model to write messages while it works through a task.
  • An agentic system can also rely on predictive components, fixed rules and tools.

We will build the complete agent loop in Chapter 6. For now: generative produces content; agentic continues through a task.

Tell Me More about Agentic AI: the loop that keeps the task moving

Imagine giving an assistant one job: find three flights that match your dates and budget, then stop and ask which one you prefer.

To keep that task moving, the system needs an agent loop:

  1. Remember the goal: three suitable flights, not a surprise holiday package.
  2. Choose the next step: search the approved booking service.
  3. Use a tool: send the search with the correct dates and destination.
  4. Check what happened: did the tool return flights, an error or nothing useful?
  5. Update its notes: keep the useful results and record what still needs doing.
  6. Decide whether to continue: compare the flights, ask you a question, finish or stop because something went wrong.

The technical name for the information carried from one step to the next is state. The rule that says “finish now,” “ask a person” or “do not continue” is a stopping condition.

This is why agentic AI is usually best understood as a system pattern, not a magical new species of model. A model may draft the search or choose a step, but software around it supplies the tools, permissions, notes and stopping rules that let the whole process continue.

Full Nerd Alert on Agentic AI: how one small error snowballs

Suppose the flight search quietly returns results for the wrong Tuesday. The system fails to notice, records those flights in its notes, compares their prices and asks you to approve the cheapest one. Every later step may look tidy while resting on one bad observation.

That is the danger of a loop: errors can travel. The model can choose the wrong tool. A tool can return incomplete information. The system can misunderstand the result. A mistaken note can become the starting point for the next decision.

Engineers call the work of coordinating all those parts orchestration. Good orchestration does not merely keep the system moving. It gives it ways to stop safely:

  • require confirmation before spending money;
  • refuse tools outside the approved permissions;
  • check important results before carrying them forward;
  • stop after repeated failure; and
  • return the task to a person when the evidence is ambiguous.

More steps can make a system more useful, but they also create more places for a plausible mistake to become an actual consequence. Agentic still does not mean AGI. A returns assistant can complete ten carefully controlled steps without becoming broadly intelligent—or developing an opinion about your shoes.

Does it act in the physical world?

A system can continue through software actions without ever moving a physical object. Embodied AI answers the separate physical-world question.

Embodied AI can sense or act through physical equipment

Embodied AI interacts with the physical world through equipment such as a robot body, camera, microphone, vehicle, sensor or mechanical control.

A warehouse robot may sense its surroundings, choose a route and move an object. A self-driving vehicle may use cameras and other sensors to estimate what is around it and control its movement. The physical connection is what makes these systems embodied.

Embodied and agentic are not synonyms:

  • A physical robot may follow a tightly fixed process. It is embodied but not necessarily agentic.
  • An agentic assistant may work entirely in software. It is agentic but not embodied.
  • An embodied robot may also be predictive, multimodal and agentic.

Embodied does not mean the system understands the physical world as a person does, moves safely or knows when its action is sensible. It tells you that physical sensing or action is part of the system. The quality and safety of that interaction are separate questions.

This distinction becomes rather important when “the system made a poor recommendation” turns into “the system moved a 200-kilogram object toward Janet.”

Janet would like the risk assessment updated.

Tell Me More about Embodied AI: sense, move, check, repeat

Stay with the warehouse robot as it approaches a trolley that someone has left partly in its route. It needs to do more than decide, once, that the floor is clear.

It repeatedly cycles through three jobs:

  1. Sense: use cameras or other sensors to gather new information.
  2. Estimate and plan: work out where it is, what is nearby and which movement might help.
  3. Act and check: move, then sense again to discover what actually happened.

That final check is crucial. A wheel can slip. The trolley can move. Janet can step into the route with no respect for the plan. The machine’s last estimate begins getting old the moment the physical world changes.

The new information received after an action is called feedback. An embodied system uses feedback to keep correcting the difference between what it intended to happen and what really happened.

Full Nerd Alert on Embodied AI: why timing and control matter

Imagine trying to carry a very full cup of coffee while seeing the spill two seconds after it happens. Even a brilliant plan would struggle with that delay.

Physical systems face the same problem. The delay between sensing something, deciding what to do and completing the movement is called latency. If the delay is too long, the world may have changed before the machine reacts.

The system therefore needs control: a way to make small adjustments as new feedback arrives so that the machine approaches its target without swerving, shaking or overshooting it. A warehouse robot can plan the correct route and still fail if its position estimate drifts or its controller reacts too slowly.

Builders often practise in a simulation because it is cheaper and safer to test thousands of situations there. But a simulation cannot perfectly reproduce glare, friction, worn equipment, strange objects or a person doing something gloriously unpredictable. The difference between performance in simulation and performance in reality is called the sim-to-real gap.

That is why a capable software model cannot simply be placed inside a machine and sent outside with a jaunty “Good luck!” Physical intelligence has to survive timing, movement and the deeply inconvenient fact that the real world refuses to hold still.

How broad and adaptable are its abilities?

This question asks how far a capability travels: one bounded job, many different jobs or a much larger disputed claim about adapting across unfamiliar intellectual work. A broad label is useful only when we also know where the evidence stops.

Specialised or narrow AI has a bounded job

Specialised AI, also called narrow AI or sometimes artificial narrow intelligence (ANI), is designed for a bounded function or domain: detecting spam, recognising a particular pattern, recommending products or optimising a defined process.

Narrow does not mean weak. A specialised chess system can outperform every person at chess while being unable to help with anything outside it. Being spectacular at one job does not secretly make it your new life coach.

Imagine a system built to spot damaged products in photographs from one factory line. It may be extraordinarily accurate inside that job and still fail on a new camera angle or unfamiliar kind of damage. That boundary matters because “specialised in damage detection” does not mean “understands every damaged object everywhere.” The useful follow-up is: specialised for which job, using which inputs, under which conditions? A smaller scope can be a strength when the system is carefully tested for that exact setting.

Tell Me More about Specialised AI: where the boundary sits

The boundary is not one neat task name. It has at least three parts: which inputs the system receives, the conditions in which it operates and which output it is expected to produce.

Our factory inspector might accept close photographs from two approved cameras, operate under controlled lighting and output one of five known damage categories. Change the image source, lighting or list of possible faults and you have changed the problem even if the slide still says “damage detection.” This is why a useful specification describes the operating boundary, not merely the job title.

Full Nerd Alert on Specialised AI: why excellent can still be narrow

Technical performance is always measured against a defined test. A specialised system might be excellent at separating two known categories, ranking familiar options or controlling one repeated process. That evidence can be strong without transferring outside the tested boundary.

The name for that boundary problem is distribution shift. The word distribution describes the mixture of cases the system encounters: which cameras took the photographs, what the lighting looked like, which kinds of damage appeared and how often. If the real mixture differs from the training and test mixture, the model may be operating out of distribution.

Picture our factory inspector. During testing, ninety out of every hundred photographs came from Camera A in bright light. At the new factory, most arrive from Camera B beside a window at dusk. The job title—damage detection—has not changed. The evidence reaching the model has. Engineers therefore test generalisation: whether performance carries beyond the examples used to fit the model into relevant new cases.

So narrow capability is not second-rate intelligence waiting to graduate. It is capability with a bounded scope and evidence boundary. The honest description names both.

General-purpose AI can help with many different tasks

General-purpose AI can perform a wide variety of tasks rather than one bounded function. Current general-purpose systems may work with language, images or code; answer questions; summarise; translate; analyse and assist with many well-scoped activities.

That breadth is real. It is also jagged. A system can solve a difficult problem and then fail at something that appears much simpler. It may do well on a familiar task and poorly when the situation becomes unusual, long, culturally specific or dependent on the physical world.

General-purpose does not mean universally capable, consistently reliable or generally intelligent in the human sense. It describes breadth across tasks, not perfection across them.

Suppose one assistant can summarise a policy, draft an invitation, explain a chart and help repair a formula in a spreadsheet. That is real breadth: the same underlying model or product can attempt several materially different jobs. The value is flexibility; the catch is that a longer menu does not promise equal skill or reliability across every item.

Tell Me More about General-purpose AI: a longer menu is not the same as mastery

Now change the spreadsheet headings, make the policy depend on local law or ask the assistant to notice that the chart uses the wrong denominator. The system must do more than select another item from its menu; it must carry something useful into a changed situation. That is transfer.

The breadth can also sit at different levels. A general-purpose model may support writing, images and code while the product around it exposes only one carefully controlled customer-service job. Or one broad-looking product may quietly route different requests to several specialised components. What the interface offers and what one underlying model can do are related claims, not identical ones.

So “general-purpose” earns two follow-up questions: How different are the tasks? and Which part is general-purpose—the model, the complete product or the product catalogue? Those answers tell you far more than the size of a feature list.

Full Nerd Alert on General-purpose AI: breadth, transfer and reliability

Three properties help describe general-purpose capability more carefully. Breadth is the range of tasks the system can attempt. Transfer is whether something useful carries into a materially different situation. Reliability is whether the ability keeps working under the conditions where it matters.

Those properties do not rise together. A system may attempt thousands of tasks but require carefully familiar formats. Another may transfer one learned pattern into a new setting but still fail too often for a high-stakes use. A feature list measures neither transfer nor dependability by itself.

Evaluation therefore needs a capability profile, not one average score. Picture a grid: different tasks run down one side; familiar, changed and genuinely unfamiliar conditions run across the top. Record both performance and how often it holds. A high average can hide a dangerous pattern—excellent writing and coding results beside unreliable legal interpretation, or strong familiar tasks beside collapsing transfer.

That profile makes the three properties visible together. Breadth changes which rows the system can attempt. Transfer changes whether performance survives the move across columns. Reliability changes how consistently the result holds when the task matters. “General-purpose” becomes an evidence claim you can inspect, not a compliment floating above a feature list.

AGI is a disputed proposed threshold

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) is the proposed idea of an AI that can understand, learn and apply knowledge across a very wide range of intellectual tasks—including genuinely unfamiliar ones—with something closer to the adaptability we expect from a capable person.

The crucial word is general. A specialised system may be exceptional at one bounded job. A current general-purpose system may attempt writing, coding, image analysis, planning and many other tasks. A proposed AGI would need to do more than attempt a long menu of familiar tasks. It would need to carry useful understanding from one kind of problem into another, work out what it needs to learn and adapt when the situation does not match the examples it has already seen.

There is no universally accepted definition or test. Researchers and companies disagree about how broad the task range must be, what level of performance counts, how much new learning or transfer is required and how reliable the system must remain when conditions change.

LAiDIES does not classify today’s products as AGI. Current general-purpose systems can do astonishingly different things, but they remain jagged: brilliant on one problem, baffling on another, heavily dependent on human-supplied context and unreliable in genuinely unfamiliar situations.

AGI is also not another word for generative, agentic, conscious or embodied. Generative describes producing content. Agentic describes continuing through steps and actions. Embodied describes physical interaction. AGI is a disputed claim about the breadth, transfer and reliability of capability. A system could be agentic without being AGI; current systems already are.

Imagine your smartest friend agrees to help with a charity auction she has never organised before. She learns the unfamiliar rules and software, connects what she knows about budgets and events, asks for missing information and changes course when the venue plan fails. She is not replaying one memorised auction script. She is carrying useful knowledge into a new situation and adapting as it changes—the kind of transfer and general adaptability an AGI claim would need to demonstrate across many different domains, not merely one heroic evening with a raffle basket.

If you need one confident sentence, use this: AGI is the disputed idea of AI that can adapt and apply what it knows across many unfamiliar intellectual tasks, rather than being capable only within bounded or previously learned patterns. No current system has universally accepted evidence that it meets that threshold.

Tell Me More about AGI: can it handle the unfamiliar?

Imagine giving a system a problem from a field it has never worked in before. You explain the goal, provide the available evidence and introduce an unfamiliar rule. The system may use that rule during the current conversation without permanently changing the model. This is called in-context learning: useful adaptation from instructions or examples available in the current context.

That can be impressive, but it does not by itself prove the broader learning ability an AGI claim may imply. Remove the examples, start a new context or change the domain again: what carries forward? Continual learning asks whether a system can acquire useful ability over time without simply losing earlier abilities. Transfer asks whether what was useful in one situation helps in a materially different one.

So the deeper test is not merely “Did it follow one new rule once?” It is: can it identify what it does not know, learn what the situation requires, retain or reconstruct the useful lesson, connect it to prior knowledge and adapt across many materially different fields—reliably? AGI definitions disagree about how much of that must occur, which is one reason the label remains disputed.

Full Nerd Alert on AGI: what would serious evidence need to show?

Imagine someone announces, “This system is AGI.” Before everyone releases the balloons, we need to know what was actually demonstrated.

  • How broad was the test? Did it cover genuinely different kinds of intellectual work or many versions of one task?
  • How good was the performance? Better than a beginner, a capable professional, the best specialist tool or the strongest human team?
  • What happened when the situation changed? Could the system cope with unfamiliar rules, missing information and misleading clues?
  • Did knowledge transfer? Could it use something learned in one field to make progress in another?
  • Could it adapt? Did it notice failure, learn what was different and change its approach?
  • How much help did people provide? Was the system working independently or being rescued through carefully chosen prompts and hidden interventions?
  • Was it reliable and safe? How often did it fail, and what would those failures cost outside the demonstration?

Researchers disagree about which combination deserves the AGI label. Even benchmark scores need context. A test may resemble material seen during training, reward a narrow test-taking strategy or measure only one carefully arranged setting. Passing the exam is evidence about the exam. It is not automatically evidence that the system can run the charity auction, diagnose a new scientific problem and navigate every unfamiliar intellectual situation that follows. A benchmark with a publicist is still a benchmark.

ASI is hypothetical

Artificial superintelligence (ASI) is a hypothetical category for intelligence far beyond human capability across a broad range of cognitive work.

This is not the same as a specialised system beating every person at one task. A chess system can defeat the world champion and remain unable to explain a parking ticket. An ASI claim would be much larger: a system exceeding the strongest human experts or teams across most intellectual fields, potentially connecting ideas and solving problems that people could not solve—or independently verify—on their own.

Picture the earlier charity-auction comparison. A proposed AGI would resemble the remarkably adaptable friend who can enter many unfamiliar intellectual situations, learn and perform capably. ASI would not mean that friend became slightly faster. It would mean a hypothetical intelligence operating beyond the best available human capability across the planning, science, mathematics, persuasion, design and every other intellectual problem in the room.

There is no current ASI system and no agreed test for one. ASI belongs in serious discussion about possible futures, governance and risk, not in a product feature list beside “accepts PDFs.”

If you need one confident sentence, use this: ASI is the hypothetical idea of AI that greatly exceeds human capability across most intellectual work. It does not exist today, and AGI does not automatically or inevitably lead to it.

Tell Me More about ASI: how would people check a beyond-human answer?

A normal test works because someone already knows, or can independently establish, what a good answer looks like. We can check a calculation, repeat an experiment or wait to see whether a prediction comes true.

Now imagine a hypothetical system proposes a scientific explanation that the best human teams cannot yet derive or verify. A confident paragraph and an impressive internal score would not settle the question. People would need external checks: break the claim into testable predictions, reproduce parts through independent methods, compare results from teams that did not share the same assumptions and observe what happens when the claim meets reality.

This creates a verification problem unique to the size of the ASI claim. If people cannot understand or reproduce the entire reasoning, evaluation has to rely more heavily on checkable consequences, independent replication, disagreement between methods and carefully bounded trials. None of those proves that a system is ASI. They show why “far beyond human capability” would change not only what the system could do, but how difficult it would be for people to know when to trust the result.

Full Nerd Alert on ASI: what could count as evidence?

Because ASI is hypothetical, there is no operating mechanism to take apart. The deeper technical job is to define what evidence could distinguish this enormous claim from exceptional performance on one task.

Serious evidence would have to establish at least three things. First, the advantage would need to appear across a broad range of intellectual work. Second, it would need to persist in unfamiliar cases rather than disappear when the test changes. Third, independent evaluation would need to distinguish genuine capability from hidden human support, favourable test design or material the system had already encountered.

No current system has demonstrated that combination. Researchers do not agree on a test, and we do not know that reaching any proposed AGI threshold would produce ASI. The accurate relationship is: AGI is a disputed proposed threshold; ASI is a further hypothetical claim. Neither is a guaranteed next step.

If a product launch places “ASI” beside “accepts PDFs,” someone has brought the wrong slide deck.

How specialised, general-purpose, AGI and ASI relate

These four labels do not form one proven ladder. They make progressively larger claims, but they do not describe guaranteed upgrades:

  • Specialised AI has a bounded job. It may be superhuman inside that boundary.
  • General-purpose AI can attempt many different jobs, but its performance can remain jagged and unreliable.
  • AGI is a disputed proposed threshold: broad, adaptable intellectual capability that transfers into genuinely unfamiliar situations.
  • ASI is a further hypothetical claim: capability far beyond the strongest humans across most intellectual work.

The relationship is therefore about the size and strength of the capability claim, not a development timetable. A current general-purpose system does not become AGI because it gains another feature. An AGI threshold, if one were ever demonstrated, would not prove that ASI must follow. A neat arrow on a slide is a theory about the future, not a law of nature.

How was it built?

This question is different from what the system does. It asks where the capability came from. Four labels belong here, and this time some of them really do sit inside others.

Rule-based AI follows logic written by people

In rule-based AI, people explicitly encode the relevant logic. A spam filter might say: when an email contains this phrase, comes from an unknown sender and includes a suspicious link, send it for review.

Rule-based does not mean primitive or perfectly transparent. Thousands of interacting rules can become difficult to maintain. The key distinction is that people supplied the decision logic rather than asking a model to learn the pattern from examples.

That matters when you need a decision to follow an explicit policy and be traceable to the rule that fired. It also tells you where change must happen: new examples will not quietly teach the system a better rule; someone must update and test the logic.

Tell Me More about Rule-based AI: following the decision path

Imagine a workplace expense system. If the amount is above a limit, require a manager. If the category is travel, require a receipt. If both are true, require both checks. The system follows the branches people wrote.

That makes one kind of explanation possible: we can trace which rules fired. But the trace is useful only if the rules themselves are current, complete and free of contradiction. A perfectly followed rule can still be a terrible rule. Brenda has several thoughts about the expense policy.

Full Nerd Alert on Rule-based AI: when the rulebook becomes the problem

Rules work well when the relevant conditions can be stated clearly and the exceptions remain manageable. The software that applies those rules is often called an inference engine. It takes known facts—“the expense is travel” and “the amount is over £500”—then follows the applicable rules to reach a conclusion.

The trouble is rule interaction. Add “international travel needs finance approval,” “directors may self-approve” and “no one may approve their own international travel,” and the system now needs a deliberate way to resolve a conflict. As the rulebook grows, the number of possible combinations can grow much faster than the number of rules. One innocent patch may alter decisions somewhere Brenda did not expect.

Keeping the system useful also creates a knowledge-maintenance problem: someone must notice when the world changes, translate that change into logic, test the new rule against the old ones and remove rules that no longer belong. New examples do not automatically rewrite the logic. That can be a strength in legal, safety or approval settings where a deliberate rule should not quietly reinvent itself overnight.

Machine learning learns patterns from examples

Machine learning develops a model from data or examples instead of requiring someone to write every input-to-output rule. A machine-learning spam filter studies messages already labelled spam or legitimate and learns patterns that help it estimate where a new message belongs.

Machine learning does not mean the system learned like a person or discovered the correct answer by itself. People choose the examples, the target, the test and what happens after the model produces a score.

That matters when the system gets something wrong. You usually cannot repair a learned pattern by finding one sentence of logic and rewriting it. Builders may need to change the training examples, objective, evaluation or product checks around the model. The construction method tells you where a useful correction can—and cannot—happen.

Tell Me More about Machine Learning: what training changes

During training, the model makes estimates on examples, compares them with the desired results and adjusts its internal numerical settings so later estimates improve. It is not storing one human-written rule for every email. It is changing a mathematical pattern-finder.

Stay with the spam filter. Messages labelled spam and legitimate provide examples. The model adjusts until useful combinations of words, senders, links and other features help separate the groups. Then builders test it on messages that were not used for those adjustments.

Full Nerd Alert on Machine Learning: objectives, overfitting and new examples

Training needs the same objective we met in Predictive AI: a measurable target used to judge whether an adjustment improved the model. Catching more spam might improve one score while also trapping more legitimate mail. The target determines which trade-off the training process rewards.

Builders therefore separate training examples from evaluation examples and inspect more than one kind of error. A model that looks excellent on familiar data may fail when messages, users or conditions change. The score is evidence about a defined test, not a permission slip from the mathematics department.

One specific failure is overfitting: the model becomes very good at the peculiarities of its training examples without learning a pattern that carries into relevant new cases. Builders use separate validation data while developing the model and test data for a later assessment so they can see whether improvement travels beyond the examples used to adjust it. A fabulous training score and a collapsing test score are the mathematical equivalent of knowing every answer on last year’s exam and none of the subject.

Neural networks learn through connected layers

A neural network is one family of machine-learning models. It passes numerical information through connected layers of adjustable operations to produce a result.

The name was inspired by a very loose comparison with networks of biological neurons. A neural network is not a digital brain, and the name does not prove consciousness or human-like understanding.

Imagine a fraud system considering the purchase amount, location and recent account activity. A neural network can pass those numerical clues through connected layers, letting later operations work with combinations created by earlier ones before producing a risk score. The layered structure matters because useful patterns may depend on clues working together, not on one clue announcing “Fraud!” while wearing a tiny burglar mask.

Tell Me More about Neural Networks: how clues influence the result

Return to the fraud example. The network receives numerical versions of clues such as amount, location and recent activity. Connections inside the network give different influence to different patterns. One layer transforms the incoming numbers; later layers transform the earlier results; the final operations produce a score.

During training, the adjustable connection strengths change. If purchases with a particular combination of clues repeatedly turn out to be fraudulent, the network can learn to make that pattern influence its score more strongly. No tiny banker lives inside assigning opinions to shoes.

Full Nerd Alert on Neural Networks: backpropagation and gradient descent

The gap between the network’s answer and the chosen target is represented by a loss function. Think of loss as the training process’s very specific complaint: not “do better,” but a number expressing how wrong this answer was according to the objective.

Next comes backpropagation. It works backward through the network to calculate how much each adjustable connection contributed to that loss. It does not tell the network the correct rule. It assigns numerical responsibility for the error.

An optimisation method called gradient descent then nudges the adjustable values in directions expected to reduce loss. One nudge teaches very little. Repeated across many examples, the adjustments can build a useful mapping from inputs to outputs—rather like improving a recipe through thousands of precisely measured taste tests, except nobody gets cake and the measurements happen in very large arrays of numbers.

That mechanism can learn relationships too complicated to write as a practical rule list. It can also learn shortcuts, biases or accidental patterns in its data. “Learned from examples” describes the construction method; it does not certify the lesson learned.

Deep learning uses neural networks with many learned layers

Deep learning is the part of the neural-network family that uses multiple learned layers. The word deep refers to the number and arrangement of layers, not to the model having deep thoughts about the human condition.

Deep learning can support predictive systems, generative models, multimodal processing and embodied machines. It describes a construction family, not the finished product’s job.

Your phone may use deep learning to recognise objects in a photograph; a language model may use it to work with relationships across text. In both cases, multiple learned layers help transform raw input into representations useful for the task. The practical point is not that “deep” means smarter. It explains why the same construction family can support very different visible AI jobs.

Tell Me More about Deep Learning: from raw input to useful representations

Suppose a model is learning from images. Early layers may become useful for simple local patterns such as edges or colour changes. Later layers can combine earlier patterns into more useful representations of textures, shapes or objects for the task.

The builders do not normally handwrite every layer’s complete visual rulebook. Training adjusts the connections so the layers together become useful for the chosen objective.

Full Nerd Alert on Deep Learning: representation learning and architecture

The deeper technical idea is representation learning. Instead of people deciding every useful feature in advance—“measure this edge, count that shape”—training lets the model develop intermediate numerical descriptions that help the chosen objective. Those descriptions are not labels a person wrote for each layer; they emerge as the complete network is adjusted.

The arrangement of layers and connections is the model’s architecture. Architecture builds in assumptions about how information should move. A design that compares nearby parts of an image encourages different relationships from one built to connect words across a long passage. Builders choose the architecture; training adjusts the values inside it.

Some deep systems are trained end to end: the signal from the final objective travels back through the whole chain, so multiple layers learn together rather than each stage being separately hand-programmed. That can discover powerful internal representations. It can also make failures harder to trace, because there may be no single human-readable rule explaining why thousands of learned values combined into one result.

More layers therefore do not guarantee a better, broader or safer system. Results still depend on the architecture, data, objective, evaluation and product around the model. “Deep” is an engineering description, not a certificate from the philosophy department.

How the construction families fit together

These labels form the clearest genuine family tree in this chapter:

AI → machine learning → neural networks → deep learning

Read the arrow as “contains this smaller family,” not “levels up into.” Every deep-learning system uses a neural network, machine learning and AI. The reverse is not true. Not all AI uses machine learning. Not all machine learning uses neural networks. Rule-based AI sits elsewhere inside the broad AI field.

Real products often combine methods. A learned model may classify a request, explicit rules may block prohibited actions, a generative model may draft text and ordinary software may require human approval. Construction can power many visible jobs; it does not tell you, by itself, what the finished product does.

Put all six questions together

Describe one system with all six questions

Let’s carry one system through all six questions.

Return to the customer-service assistant from the diagram. It reads a customer’s email and photograph, estimates how urgent the problem is, drafts a response and—after a person approves—opens a service ticket and checks whether it succeeded.

Its job

It is predictive when it estimates urgency. It is generative when it drafts the response.

Its information

It is multimodal because it works with text and an image.

How it operates

It is agentic only if it continues through the ticket process, uses the permitted tool, observes the result and decides whether the task is complete. Drafting one response and stopping would not be enough.

Where it acts

It is not embodied if it operates only through software. If it controlled a repair robot in the physical world, embodiment would become relevant.

How broad it is

The customer-service product has a specialised job. Nothing in this example demonstrates AGI.

How it was built

We cannot tell from the visible behaviour alone. It may use machine-learning models, explicit rules and ordinary software together. “Unknown” is the technically tidy answer until we have evidence.

The labels do not compete. Together, they give you a more complete description: a specialised, multimodal product that uses predictive and generative AI and may operate agentically.

Tell Me More about the six questions: how one change can change the labels

Return to the customer-service assistant and change one thing at a time.

  • Remove the photograph and let it read only the email. It may still be predictive and generative, but this interaction is no longer multimodal.
  • Let it draft a reply and stop. The product may still be generative, but it is not agentic because it does not continue through the ticket process.
  • Connect it to a repair robot that can inspect and move a physical object. Embodied now becomes relevant; the software-only version was not embodied.
  • Put a general-purpose model inside it. The model may support many tasks while the customer-service product still performs a specialised job.

The labels change when the relevant property changes. They do not change because the marketing slide found a more exciting adjective.

The chapter in one minute

AI is the broad field. A single AI system can receive several type labels because the labels describe different properties:

  • Predictive and generative describe jobs.
  • Multimodal describes information forms.
  • Agentic describes continued operation through steps and actions.
  • Embodied describes physical-world interaction.
  • Specialised, general-purpose, AGI and ASI describe different breadth claims or categories, with AGI disputed and ASI hypothetical.
  • Rule-based AI follows explicit logic; machine learning, neural networks and deep learning form a nested construction family.

The question is not “Which single type wins?” The useful question is “Which properties describe this system, and what evidence do we have for each one?”

Key Definitions

Standard definitions

  • Agentic AI — AI that continues through steps, tools and observations toward a goal; introduced here and explained fully in Chapter 6.
  • AGI — the disputed idea of AI that can adapt and apply what it knows across many unfamiliar intellectual tasks, rather than remaining capable only within bounded or previously learned patterns; no current system has universally accepted evidence that it meets the threshold.
  • AI — the broad field of building machine-based systems that infer outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions from inputs.
  • AI system — a system that receives inputs and infers outputs using represented patterns, rules or relationships.
  • ANI — artificial narrow intelligence; another name used for specialised or narrow AI.
  • ASI — the hypothetical idea of AI that greatly exceeds human capability across most intellectual work; it does not exist today, and AGI would not automatically or inevitably lead to it.
  • Breadth — how many materially different kinds of task a system can attempt.
  • Classification or recognition — estimating which category an input most likely belongs to.
  • Deep learning — machine learning using neural networks with multiple learned layers; the family relationship, everyday examples and construction job are taught here, and Chapter 3 explains how those layers learn and operate.
  • Embodied AI — AI that senses or acts through physical equipment.
  • General-purpose AI — current AI that can perform a variety of tasks rather than one specialised function, though capability and reliability may vary sharply across them.
  • Generative AI — AI whose product job includes producing new content from learned patterns and current input.
  • Inference — working out a result from available inputs, represented patterns or rules rather than retrieving one answer written for the exact case.
  • Input — information supplied to an AI system for its current operation.
  • Machine learning — building a model from data or examples rather than writing every input-to-output mapping directly; the construction distinction is defined here, and Chapter 3 explains training and inference in full.
  • Modality — a form of information, such as text, image, audio, video or sensor data.
  • Model — the mathematical or rule-based component that performs an inference inside a wider AI system.
  • Multimodal AI — AI that handles more than one modality.
  • Neural network — a family of machine-learning models that passes numerical information through connected layers of adjustable operations; Full Nerd Alert introduces loss functions, backpropagation and gradient descent, while Chapter 3 explains the wider learning process.
  • Output — the result an AI system infers or produces from an input.
  • Predictive AI — the broader public label for systems whose jobs include classification, prediction, ranking or recommendation.
  • Product — the visitor- or worker-facing experience that may combine an AI system with an interface, stored information, tools, permissions, ordinary software and people.
  • Ranking — ordering options using predicted scores or relevance.
  • Recommendation — selecting or presenting options judged relevant to a person or situation.
  • Reliability — how consistently a capability works under the conditions where it matters.
  • Representation — a numerical form in which a model can work with patterns from text, images, sound or other information.
  • Rule-based AI — AI whose relevant logic is explicitly represented in designed rules.
  • Specialised or narrow AI — AI designed for a bounded task or domain.
  • Training — the process in which a machine-learning model’s adjustable settings change so its results improve against chosen examples and objectives.
  • Transfer — using something learned in one situation to make progress in a materially different one.
  • Value or outcome prediction — one predictive job: estimating an unknown or future value or outcome.

Tell Me More definitions

  • Agent loop — the repeating goal, next-step, tool, observation, updated-state and stopping sequence used by an agentic system.
  • Continual learning — acquiring useful ability over time while retaining earlier abilities rather than overwriting them catastrophically.
  • Feature — a piece of information made available to a model for a task, such as a transaction amount or location.
  • Feedback — new information about what happened after an action, used to adjust what happens next.
  • In-context learning — using instructions or examples in the current context to perform or adapt to a task without necessarily changing the model’s trained parameters.
  • Next-token prediction — the repeated internal calculation in which a generative language model estimates which token could come next; it does not make Generative AI a subtype of the public category Predictive AI.
  • State — information an agentic system carries from one step to the next about its goal, progress and observations.
  • Stopping condition — a rule that tells a multi-step system when to finish, ask for help or stop safely.
  • System pattern — a way of arranging models, software, tools, state and rules so the complete system behaves in a particular way, such as continuing through an agent loop.
  • Threshold — a chosen cutoff that turns a model score into a product action, such as sending a purchase for review.
  • Token — one text unit processed or produced by a language model; it may be a whole word, part of a word or punctuation.

Full Nerd Alert definitions

  • Architecture — the arrangement of a model’s layers and connections, which shapes how information can move and which relationships the model is equipped to learn.
  • Attention mechanism — a mathematical process that gives different weight to pieces of information and their relationships for the current calculation.
  • Backpropagation — the training calculation that works backward through a neural network to determine how its adjustable values contributed to the loss.
  • Calibration — how well a model’s probability numbers match what happens across comparable cases.
  • Capability profile — a view of performance across materially different tasks and conditions rather than one average score.
  • Control — the process of turning a desired physical result into commands and correcting movement using feedback.
  • Decoding method — the rule a generative model or product uses to select an output token from the model’s scored possibilities.
  • Discrimination — in model evaluation, the ability to rank more likely cases above less likely ones; this is different from the everyday and legal meaning of unfair treatment.
  • Distribution shift — a change in the mixture or conditions of cases a model encounters compared with the data used to build or evaluate it.
  • Encoder — a model component that turns one form of input into numerical representations the wider system can use.
  • End-to-end training — training several connected stages together using a signal from the final objective rather than programming or optimising every stage separately.
  • Generalisation — performance that carries from training examples into relevant new cases.
  • Gradient descent — an optimisation method that adjusts model values in directions expected to reduce the loss.
  • Inference engine — software that applies explicit rules to known facts to reach conclusions or actions.
  • Knowledge-maintenance problem — the continuing work of translating changes in the world into explicit rules, testing their interactions and removing logic that no longer belongs.
  • Latency — the delay between an event, the system receiving information about it and the resulting response.
  • Loss function — the numerical measure used during training to express how far a model’s result is from the chosen target.
  • Objective — the target used to judge and improve a model during training; people choose it, and it may not represent everything they actually value.
  • Orchestration — the software coordination of models, tools, permissions, observations, state and stopping conditions in a multi-step system.
  • Out of distribution — describing cases or conditions that differ materially from the mixture represented in the data used to build or evaluate a model.
  • Overfitting — learning the peculiarities of training examples so closely that performance does not carry well into relevant new cases.
  • Probability distribution — the model’s set of possible next outcomes or tokens together with their estimated likelihoods.
  • Representation learning — learning useful numerical features or internal representations from data rather than having people specify every feature in advance.
  • Rule interaction — the way explicit rules combine, conflict or produce unexpected results when several apply to the same case.
  • Sim-to-real gap — the difference between performance in a simulation and performance in the messier physical world.
  • System boundary — the chosen line around the model, software, inputs, processes and people included in a particular system claim or evaluation.
  • Temperature — a product setting that can change how concentrated or varied token selection is; it does not add facts or judgment.
  • Test data — examples kept separate until a later assessment of how well a developed model performs beyond its training and development data.
  • Validation data — examples kept separate from training adjustments and used while developing a model to check whether improvements generalise.
