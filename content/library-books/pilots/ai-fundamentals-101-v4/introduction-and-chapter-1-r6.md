AI is a truly transformative technology that, in some form, is accessible to almost everyone. It is changing how we work, live and operate as a society. But the way we interact with it is different from the way most of us interact with other transformative technologies.

Take electricity. It changed work, home and society, too. But most of us flick a switch or plug something in and—bam!—we have light at night. We may understand a little of the science and history behind it (thank you, Nikola Tesla, Thomas Edison and the many other people who built modern electrical systems), but our daily interaction is essentially plug and play. Pun very much intended.

AI is different because what we give the system helps shape what it gives back. Our request, context, examples, files, settings and choice of tool can all change the result. We can use it to help us build and create a startling range of things—like this website, for example—but to get something useful, we need to understand more about how the system works and how to interact with it.

If we do not, we are more likely to get something completely different from what we intended, become frustrated and walk away. We do not want that. This technology is too useful to dismiss with, *I don’t get the hype. This thing sucks!* But a polished answer is not automatically a good one either, so understanding AI also helps us know when **not** to trust it.

### One tiny map before we start

Every AI interaction in this book will fit somewhere on this map:

> **What you provide (input) → the AI system → what it produces (output) → what happens next (consequence)**

Suppose you paste meeting notes into an AI assistant and ask for an email. The notes and your instructions are the **input**. The **AI system** works with them. The draft email is the **output**. Then comes the part people often forget: the **consequence**. You may edit the draft, send it to forty people, base a decision on it or discover that the system confidently gave Janet credit for Brenda’s idea. Poor Brenda.

That is why producing an output is not the end of the story. What people or connected systems do with it matters, too.

Whenever an AI claim becomes confusing, ask three questions:

1. **What does the system do?** Describe the actual task, not the marketing label.
2. **What does it use?** Look for the inputs, information, model, tools and permissions involved.
3. **What happens after the output?** Does a person check it, does it influence a decision or does the system take another action?

We will keep adding detail to this map as we go. By the end, chips, models, data centres, agents and all the other vocabulary will have somewhere sensible to live. No buzzword left wandering the streets unsupervised.

# Chapter 1: So… what kind of AI are we talking about?

The words have escaped the lab and are now breeding in PowerPoint.

Predictive AI. Generative AI. Multimodal AI. Agentic AI. Narrow AI. General-purpose AI. AGI. ASI. Every new presentation seems to arrive with another label, and everyone says theirs as though it explains everything.

It does not.

Your email spam filter, phone photo search, streaming recommendations and an assistant that drafts a proposal may all use AI. They are not the same kind of system, they do not produce the same kind of result, and they should not be trusted in the same way. But they share one broad idea: each receives some form of input and uses an internal method to infer an output rather than simply replaying one fixed response.

That word **infer** matters. It means the system works out an output from the input using patterns, rules or relationships represented inside it. We will start opening that box in Chapter 2. For now, the useful point is this:

> **AI is a broad family of systems—not one machine, one model or one behaviour.**

That is why asking “What type of AI is it?” can produce several correct answers. The labels may be describing different things: what the system does, what kinds of information it handles, how it operates or how broad someone claims its abilities are.

We are going to take those one at a time, because several true labels are much easier to understand once you know which question each one answers.

## The AI you were using before it started writing poems

Long before everyone was asking chatbots to draft emails, AI was quietly sorting, predicting and recommending things all around you.

An email system examines a new message and classifies it as likely spam or likely legitimate. A photo app examines visual features and groups images that probably contain the same person or object. A bank system may estimate whether a transaction looks unusual. A streaming service ranks possible films or songs and recommends what to show you first.

These words are related, but they are not interchangeable:

| What the system does | Plain meaning | An everyday encounter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Classifies or recognises** | Decides which category an input most likely belongs to | “Probably spam”; “probably a dog”; “probably the same face” |
| **Predicts** | Estimates an unknown or future value from available information | “Likely arrival time”; “chance this transaction is fraudulent” |
| **Ranks** | Puts options into an order using some scoring method | Which search result, song or product appears first |
| **Recommends** | Selects or presents options judged relevant to a person or situation | “You may want to watch this next” |

Think of a Friday night at Blockbuster. The clerk recognises that *Scream* is horror, predicts that your mother will absolutely refuse to watch it, ranks the remaining choices and recommends *Clueless*. Four related moves; four different jobs.

An AI product can connect those moves, too. It might classify hundreds of items, predict which ones are relevant, rank the candidates and then display a recommendation. What you see is the final suggestion; behind it may be several different steps.

That matters because each step can fail differently. The system could put an item in the wrong category, make a weak prediction, rank using a goal you dislike or recommend something based on incomplete information. “The recommendation was bad” describes the visible result. It does not yet tell you where the problem began.

### Pin this

**Predictive AI** is a loose public label for systems that estimate a likely category, value or outcome. It does not mean the system can predict anything, see the future or produce certainty. A probability wearing lip gloss is still a probability.

## Generative AI: the one making new stuff

Generative AI produces content: text, images, audio, video, code or other digital material. When you ask for a draft, illustration or summary, the system generates an output based on patterns learned during training plus the information and instructions available for your current request.

It is not usually searching a filing cabinet for one completed answer that was stored there waiting for you. It produces the result as it goes. That is why the same request can yield different wording, why small changes in context can matter and why an answer can sound original, polished and completely wrong.

“New content” also does not mean “a completely new idea no human has ever had.” It means the system generated this output rather than only retrieving one finished item. The output may still echo common patterns, clichés, biases or material in the system’s training and context. If you ask for a campaign slogan and receive “Unlock Your Potential,” the machine has technically generated content. Creativity has filed a grievance.

Generative describes **what kind of output the system produces**. It does not tell you whether the system is accurate, current, original, safe, connected to tools or able to continue across a longer task.

## Multimodal AI: what kinds of information can it handle?

A **modality** is a kind of information, such as text, image, audio or video.

A system that only receives and produces text is single-modality. A system that can examine a photo and answer a spoken question about it is working across several modalities. It may receive an image and audio, then produce text or speech.

This means one product can be both **generative** and **multimodal** without the labels competing:

- *Generative* tells you that it produces content.
- *Multimodal* tells you that it can work with more than one kind of input or output.

For example, imagine uploading a photograph of a damaged chair and asking an assistant to draft a repair request. Looking at the image is multimodal input handling. Writing the request is generation. Same interaction; two useful descriptions.

Multimodal does not mean the system understands the scene as a person does. It tells you which forms of information the system can process, not whether its interpretation is correct.

## Agentic AI: when the system keeps going

Ask a typical chatbot to draft an itinerary and it produces an answer. You decide what to do next.

An **agentic** system can continue through a task: decide on a next step, use a tool, observe what happened, adjust and keep going toward a goal until it reaches a stopping point or needs help.

Imagine the difference between:

1. “Here is a suggested itinerary for Montreal.”
2. “I checked the dates you approved, searched the permitted booking service, compared options against your budget, asked you to choose between the two reasonable flights and—only after you confirmed—submitted the booking.”

The second system is not merely producing a longer answer. It is operating through a sequence of actions and observations. That continued loop is the important agentic idea.

Agentic does **not** mean alive, conscious, correct or free to do whatever it likes. The system’s available tools, permissions, instructions, checkpoints and stopping conditions are designed by people. Its autonomy is delegated and bounded—even when the boundary is poorly designed.

And agentic systems can make very ordinary mistakes at impressive speed. If an early step is wrong, later steps may build on it. This is why a useful agent needs more than enthusiasm and access to your calendar. It needs permissions that match the task, ways to check what actually happened, sensible stopping conditions and human confirmation where the consequence matters.

We will take that mechanism apart properly in Chapter 6, after we have learned what models, product software, current information and tools are. For now, remember:

> **Generative means it produces content. Agentic means it continues through steps and actions. A system may be one, both or neither.**

## Embodied AI: when the system has a physical way to act

An embodied AI system interacts with the physical world through something like a robot body, sensors or mechanical controls. A warehouse robot, for example, may sense its surroundings, choose a route and move an object.

Embodied and agentic are related but not identical. A physical robot may follow a tightly fixed process. An agentic system may operate entirely in software. One label tells you there is physical-world interaction; the other tells you about continued operation toward a goal.

This distinction becomes rather important when “the system made a poor recommendation” turns into “the system moved a 200-kilogram object toward Janet.”

Janet would like the risk assessment updated.

## General-purpose AI is not the same as AGI

Current general-purpose AI systems can perform a wide variety of tasks: work with language, generate images or code, answer questions and assist with many well-scoped activities. That breadth is real and impressive.

It is also jagged. A system can solve a difficult problem and then fail at something that looks much simpler. It may perform well in a familiar setting and poorly when the task becomes unusual, long, culturally specific or dependent on the physical world. Broad usefulness is not the same as uniformly dependable intelligence.

There are two different questions hiding here. The first is about the breadth of systems that exist now: **specialised** or **general-purpose**. The second is about disputed or hypothetical future capability: **AGI** and **ASI**. They belong in the same conversation, but they are not one tidy three-step ladder.

### Narrow or specialised AI

A system designed for a bounded function or domain: detecting spam, recognising a particular pattern, recommending products or optimising a defined process. “Narrow” does not mean weak. A specialised system can outperform people at its specific task while being unable to help with anything outside it.

### AGI: artificial general intelligence

AGI is a proposed and disputed category for intelligence that is broadly capable and can transfer what it knows across unfamiliar intellectual tasks. There is no universally accepted definition or test. Researchers and companies disagree about how much breadth, performance, learning ability, reliability and autonomy should count.

LAiDIES does **not** classify today’s products as AGI. Current general-purpose systems can do many different things, but breadth across tasks is not the same as robust general intelligence. A system that writes code, analyses an image and plans a trip may still fail unpredictably, depend heavily on human-supplied context and struggle to transfer its abilities into a genuinely unfamiliar situation.

### ASI: artificial superintelligence

ASI is a hypothetical category for intelligence far beyond human capability across a broad range of cognitive work. There is no current ASI system. It belongs in serious discussion about possible futures, governance and risk—not in a product feature list beside “accepts PDFs.”

AGI and ASI are not guaranteed upgrades waiting in the next software release. If a slide shows **AI → AGI → ASI** like a Pokémon evolution chart, put the laser pointer down. That is one possible story about the future, not a law of nature.

### Pin this

- **Specialised AI exists now:** bounded to a task or domain, but potentially very capable within it.
- **General-purpose AI exists now:** broad across many tasks, but uneven and fallible.
- **AGI is disputed:** no universal definition, test or agreed threshold; LAiDIES does not say it exists today.
- **ASI is hypothetical:** a proposed future category beyond broad human cognitive capability.

## Tell Me More! Why one product can have several labels

Let’s take one hypothetical workplace assistant. It can read a customer email and photograph, estimate which issue is most urgent, draft a response and—after explicit approval—open a service ticket through a connected tool.

Several labels apply:

- It **classifies or predicts** urgency.
- It is **multimodal** because it handles text and an image.
- It is **generative** because it drafts a response.
- It may be **agentic** if it continues through the ticket process, observes the result and chooses what to do next.
- It is **not therefore AGI**. Those useful abilities do not settle the disputed requirements for general intelligence.

The labels are not rival answers. They answer different questions about the same system.

The useful route is to inspect the actual system and ask concrete questions about its behaviour—not force every label into one list of competing “types.”

## Full Nerd Alert! The four questions hiding inside “What type is it?”

When someone asks what type of AI a product uses, find out which question they really mean:

1. **What result does it produce?** A classification, prediction, ranking, recommendation, generated item or something else?
2. **What kinds of information can it receive and produce?** Text, images, audio, video, sensor data—or several at once?
3. **How does it operate after the first output?** Does it stop, follow a fixed workflow or continue choosing steps and using tools toward a goal?
4. **How broad is the capability claim?** A specialised task, a current general-purpose system, disputed AGI or hypothetical ASI?

These are not the only useful ways to classify AI. Later we will also care about how a system was built, what model it uses, where its information comes from, how much autonomy people delegated, what infrastructure runs it and who bears the consequences.

But these four questions will already rescue you from most “What type of AI is this?” conversations.

## Try it on something you use

Choose one AI feature you encountered this week. Do not begin with its marketing label. Ask:

- What input did it receive?
- What result did it produce?
- Was that result a classification, prediction, ranking, recommendation or generated item?
- Which modalities were involved?
- Did the system stop after the output, or continue through tools and actions?
- Is anyone making an AGI-sized claim about evidence that only shows one bounded task?

Now explain the system to someone else without using the phrase “AI-powered.” If you can describe what it actually did, congratulations: you have already improved the meeting.

## The map so far

We began with one broad family: AI systems receive inputs and infer outputs that can affect what happens next.

Inside that family, systems can perform different tasks. They can recognise, predict, rank, recommend or generate. They can handle one modality or several. They can stop after one result or continue through an agentic loop. They can operate only in software or interact through a physical body. And claims about breadth range from specialised systems to current general-purpose AI, disputed AGI and hypothetical ASI.
