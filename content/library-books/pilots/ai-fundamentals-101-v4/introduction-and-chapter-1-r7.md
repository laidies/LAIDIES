# Chapter 1: The different AI labels and how they fit together

The words have escaped the lab and are now breeding in PowerPoint. And we bet you two bucks that the person giving the presentation likely doesn’t know what many of these terms actually mean.

Predictive AI. Generative AI. Multimodal AI. Agentic AI. Embodied AI. Machine learning. General-purpose AI. AGI. ASI. One appears in a product launch, another turns up in the news, and six more arrive in a meeting where everyone is nodding with the serene confidence of people who absolutely plan to Google them later.

Here is the first useful thing to know: **those labels are not one list of competing options.** They describe different things about an AI system.

Take a travel assistant. It can produce a new itinerary, work with your written request and a screenshot, and search permitted services through several steps before asking you to choose. *Generative* describes the new itinerary. *Multimodal* describes the text and image. *Agentic* describes the goal, steps and results. Three labels are true because each tells you something different about the same product.

One AI label may tell you **how the system was built**. Another tells you **the job it performs**. Another tells you **which forms of information it can use**. Another tells you **whether the system can work through several steps or act in the physical world**. AGI and ASI make much bigger claims about how broad or powerful an intelligence could be.

That is the idea this chapter will secure. We are not going to turn you into a walking glossary. We are going to make the labels sit still long enough for you to see which question each one answers.

## AI is the field, not one machine

Let’s start with the largest label of all.

**Artificial intelligence**, or **AI**, is the broad field of making computer systems do work that requires them to interpret information and produce a useful result. That result might be recognising a face in a photograph, estimating whether a bank transaction looks suspicious, choosing which song to recommend, creating an image or planning the next move in a game.

That is still a broad description, because AI is a broad field. It does not tell you that a machine thinks like a person. It does not tell you whether the result is correct. It does not even tell you how the system did the work. It simply tells you that the product uses one or more AI methods to turn information into a result.

Here is a concrete example. You open the photographs on your phone and search for “dog.” The phone finds the pictures containing dogs even though you never typed the word *dog* underneath each photograph. Somewhere inside the product, a computer system has learned or been given a way to recognise visual patterns that tend to belong to dogs. It uses those patterns on the photographs in front of it and returns a result you can see.

That is an AI job. No robot voice. No dramatic red eye. No declaration of world domination. Just: “Here are the 426 photographs of your dog, including 38 in which she appears to be judging you.”

### Two broad ways people have built AI systems

AI did not begin when chatbots became popular. For decades, people have built AI systems in several ways. Two broad approaches will help you understand the landscape now.

In a **rule-based system**, people write the instructions the computer should follow. A simple rule might say: *if a transaction is above a certain amount and occurs in a country the cardholder has never used before, send it for review.* The people building the system decide the conditions and the response.

In **machine learning**, people do not write every individual rule for every possible case. They give the system examples and a way to improve at finding useful patterns in those examples. After that learning process, the system can use the patterns it found on a new case, such as a new transaction or a new photograph.

Imagine teaching someone to identify spam. The rule-based route says: “If the subject contains WIN A FREE CRUISE in capitals and twelve exclamation marks, send it to junk.” The machine-learning route shows the system a large number of messages marked *spam* or *not spam* so it can learn a much richer set of clues. The cruise rule may still help, but the learned system is not limited to the exact phrases a person remembered to write down.

Neither approach is automatically better. A clear rule can be easier to inspect and exactly right for a stable task. Machine learning is useful when the patterns are too numerous or messy to describe rule by rule. Real products often combine both.

The relationship to pin down is simple:

> **AI is the broad field. Machine learning is one important way of building AI inside that field. It is not the whole field.**

Later chapters will show exactly how machine learning learns and what a model is. For now, you only need the family relationship: all machine learning used for intelligent tasks belongs inside AI, but not every AI system is built only with machine learning.

## Some labels describe the job

Now we can ask a much more useful question: **what job is this part of the system doing?**

There are more than two answers. Predictive and generative are common labels, but they are not the only two boxes available, and “what result does it produce?” is not a multiple-choice question with only those answers.

An AI system may perform one or several of these jobs:

- **Recognize or classify:** decide which category something most likely belongs to. Is this email spam? Is that object a dog? Does this scan show the pattern a clinician is looking for?
- **Estimate or forecast:** calculate a likely value or outcome. How long will delivery take? How likely is this transaction to be fraudulent? How much stock may be needed next week?
- **Rank:** put options in an order. Which search result should appear first? Which job applications should a person review first? Which song is most likely to suit this moment?
- **Recommend:** suggest an option based on the information and goal available. What might you watch, read, buy or do next?
- **Generate:** produce new content for the request, such as text, an image, audio, video or code.
- **Plan or control:** choose a sequence or next action, such as finding a route, scheduling work or moving a machine.

These jobs can be connected. A streaming service may recognise facts about a film, estimate how relevant it is to you, rank thousands of choices and recommend the first few. One visible row saying *Because you watched...* may rest on several AI jobs.

### Predictive AI estimates, sorts or recommends

**Predictive AI** is a loose public label for systems that use available information to estimate a category, value, outcome or order. Spam filters, fraud alerts, arrival-time estimates, search rankings and recommendations have used predictive methods for years.

Predictive does not mean psychic. It means the system calculates what appears likely from the information and patterns available to it. A fraud score may be useful without being certain. A recommendation can be reasonable and still make you wonder whether the streaming service has ever met you.

Remember Friday night at the video shop? A clerk could classify *Scream* as horror, estimate that your mother would refuse to watch it, rank the remaining choices and recommend *Clueless*. Four related decisions; one service. A modern recommendation product can connect similar jobs at much larger scale.

The practical point is that each job can fail differently. The product may put an item in the wrong category, estimate badly, rank for a goal you dislike or recommend using incomplete information. “The recommendation was terrible” describes what you saw. It does not yet tell you where the trouble began.

### Generative AI produces new content

**Generative AI** produces new content for the request: a draft email, an image, a piece of music, a video clip, code or another created output.

Suppose you paste rough meeting notes into an AI assistant and ask it to write a follow-up email. The assistant is not usually retrieving one completed email that was filed away under *Brenda’s chaotic Tuesday meeting*. It produces a new draft using patterns it learned earlier and the notes and instructions you supplied now.

That is what *generative* tells you. The output was produced for this request.

It does **not** tell you that the content is true, original, tasteful or good. If the assistant writes, “Let’s circle back and unlock synergies,” it has successfully generated new content. Whether it should be allowed near your colleagues is a separate decision.

Predictive and generative jobs can appear in the same product. A customer-service system might estimate which issue a message is about, retrieve the relevant account details, rank possible solutions and generate a reply. The labels tell you which jobs occurred; they do not tell you that the product belongs to one pure species.

### Go deeper: why generative AI still makes predictions inside

This is a famous source of confusion, so let’s tidy it now without dragging you into the engine room before Chapter 7.

A generative language system creates text one small piece at a time. To choose each piece, it estimates which possibilities would make sense after the text it has so far. In ordinary language, it is making a prediction about what could come next.

That does not mean **Generative AI** must sit inside the public product category **Predictive AI**. The same word is being used at two levels:

- *Generative* describes the job you see: producing new content.
- *Predicting the next piece* describes one calculation used inside a generative language model.

Think of baking a cake. The finished job is *baking*. Checking whether the sponge is likely to be done after 25 minutes is a prediction inside that job. We do not therefore classify all baking as a branch of weather forecasting because both contain an estimate.

We will build the real generative mechanism later, after you know what models, training and tokens are. For now, keep the levels separate: **a system can generate content by making repeated internal estimates. The visible job and the internal calculation are related, not identical labels.**

## Other labels describe what information and actions are involved

We now know that recognition, ranking, recommendation and generation describe jobs. The next labels answer different questions. They can sit across several jobs like a transparent sheet placed over the same picture.

### Multimodal AI works with more than one form of information

A **modality** is simply a form of information: text, images, audio, video or readings from a sensor.

A **multimodal** AI system can work across more than one of those forms. You might upload a photograph of a broken office chair and type, “Please describe the damage and draft a repair request.” The product uses an image and text as inputs, then produces text as its output.

The important word is *across*. Uploading two files does not automatically prove the AI connected them. The useful capability is that the system can relate the words *broken chair* to the right object and visible damage in the photograph.

Multimodal is not a job competing with generative. The same interaction can be:

- **multimodal** because it works with image and text; and
- **generative** because it produces a new written request.

If it also estimates how serious the damage appears, that part may be predictive. One product, several compatible labels, each adding a different fact.

Multimodal can describe a model, a complete system or a product, depending on which parts actually handle the different forms. You do not need to settle that engineering boundary in Chapter 1. You do need to know that the label tells you about **forms of information**, not the product’s only job or its intelligence level.

### Agentic AI works through a goal, steps and results

**Agentic AI** describes a way of arranging a complete system so it can work through a task rather than only produce one response.

Imagine you ask a travel assistant to find three flights that match your dates and budget, then stop and ask which one you prefer. To complete that task, the system needs to keep hold of the goal, choose a next step, use an approved search tool, inspect what came back and decide whether to continue, ask you a question or stop.

The useful pattern looks like this:

1. **Goal:** find three suitable flights.
2. **Next step:** search the approved service using the dates and destination.
3. **Action:** use the tool it has permission to use.
4. **Result:** inspect the flights returned—or notice that the search failed.
5. **Update:** keep what matters and decide what remains to be done.
6. **Stop, ask or continue:** present the options, request your choice or stop safely because something is wrong.

An agent can stop. In fact, a well-designed one should stop when the task is finished, permission is missing, evidence is unclear or a person needs to decide. “It kept going” is not the definition. Your agents stopping when they should not may be annoying, but it does not make them non-agentic. It means the stopping, instructions, tools or surrounding system may need attention.

Agentic also does not mean generative. Many current agentic products use a generative model to interpret instructions, write messages or choose a next step. But the **agentic** label describes the whole goal-and-action loop. That loop may also use predictions, rules, search tools and ordinary software.

And agentic absolutely does not mean AGI. A tightly controlled returns assistant may work through twelve steps—look up an order, check a policy, offer an exchange and create a label—while remaining specialised to returns. Competence across steps is not the same claim as broad, general intelligence.

### Embodied AI senses or acts in the physical world

**Embodied AI** connects an AI system to physical equipment. A robot may use cameras or other sensors to gather information, then control wheels, arms or another mechanical part. A vehicle may interpret what its cameras and sensors detect and adjust steering or braking.

That physical connection is the key. A software assistant that sends an email is taking an action, but it is not embodied. A warehouse robot following a fixed route is embodied, but it may not be agentic. A robot that adjusts its plan after sensing a blocked aisle could be both.

Again, the labels answer different questions:

- **agentic:** does the system work through a goal, results and next steps?
- **embodied:** does it sense or act through physical equipment?

Neither label tells you whether the system is wise, reliable or safe. In the physical world, a bad decision can become a moving object, which is why testing, limits and stopping matter so much.

## Specialised, general-purpose, AGI and ASI make claims about breadth

So far, our labels have described approaches, jobs, information and action. The next group asks: **how broad are the system’s abilities?**

This is where the evidence status matters. Specialised and general-purpose systems exist now. AGI is a proposed and disputed category. ASI is hypothetical.

### Specialised AI is built for a bounded area of work

A **specialised AI** system is designed for a particular task or limited area: spotting suspicious transactions, recommending songs, detecting a manufacturing defect or optimising delivery routes.

Specialised does not mean simple. A system can be astonishingly capable within its area and useless outside it. The world’s best fraud detector cannot necessarily plan a wedding, explain a blood test or tell Aunt Linda what happened to that cat.

### General-purpose AI can be used across many kinds of work

A **general-purpose AI** system can support many different tasks rather than one narrow job. Current large AI models can write and revise text, analyse images, work with code, summarize documents and help plan across many subjects. Products can adapt those broad capabilities to many uses.

Broad is not the same as dependable everywhere. A system may perform impressively across twenty familiar tasks and then fail strangely on the twenty-first. It may need careful instructions, current information, tools, verification or a person who understands the situation. Range tells you that it can work across many areas. It does not guarantee judgment, reliability or effortless transfer to anything new.

### AGI is a disputed proposal about genuinely general intelligence

**Artificial general intelligence**, or **AGI**, is a proposed category for an AI system with intelligence broad and adaptable enough to learn and perform across unfamiliar intellectual tasks—not just switch among many tasks it was already prepared to handle.

Here is the practical distinction people are trying to capture.

A current general-purpose system may write code, analyse a photograph and plan a trip. That is real breadth. But it may still fail unpredictably, depend heavily on the information and instructions a person supplies, and struggle when the situation changes in a way it has not encountered.

An AGI-sized claim would be larger. It would suggest a system can take what it knows, understand a genuinely new intellectual situation, learn what the new situation requires and use its abilities reliably across it. Not merely: “It can do lots of impressive things.” More like: “It can adapt its intelligence across the kinds of unfamiliar problems for which people currently have to learn, reason and transfer understanding.”

Imagine someone who memorized the instructions for 200 board games. She has impressive range. Now hand her a new game whose rules are incomplete, notice that one rule conflicts with another and ask her to work out a sensible way to play. The interesting question is no longer how many games she has seen. It is whether she can understand the unfamiliar problem, transfer useful ideas, learn what is missing and adapt.

That comparison does not prove a test for AGI. It makes the disputed issue visible: **breadth across known tasks is not automatically the same as robust transfer into unfamiliar ones.**

There is no universally accepted definition or test for AGI. Researchers and companies disagree about how much breadth, learning ability, reliability, autonomy and real-world performance should count. Some definitions compare a system with human abilities; others try to avoid one human benchmark. The thresholds and timelines are live arguments, not settled facts.

LAiDIES does not classify today’s products as AGI. When a company, researcher or headline uses the term, ask what definition and evidence they are using. “It passed an impressive benchmark” and “it can reliably adapt across unfamiliar intellectual work” are not automatically the same claim.

### ASI is a hypothetical intelligence far beyond ours

**Artificial superintelligence**, or **ASI**, is the hypothetical idea of an AI system that greatly exceeds the strongest human capabilities across most or all important intellectual areas.

ASI is not “a chatbot, but with better answers.” It is a much larger future claim about intelligence beyond human ability across science, strategy, invention and other domains. No system has been shown to be ASI. The term belongs in discussions of possible futures, risks and governance—not in a product description pretending the future has already arrived.

The relationship is:

- **specialised AI:** strong in a bounded area;
- **general-purpose AI:** useful across many kinds of current work;
- **AGI:** a disputed proposed threshold for broadly adaptable general intelligence;
- **ASI:** a hypothetical level far beyond human intellectual capability.

These are not four confirmed stops on a railway timetable. Two describe systems we have. One is disputed. One is hypothetical. Anyone drawing a smooth arrow from today’s chatbot to ASI has replaced evidence with stationery.

## AI did not begin with ChatGPT

The current AI boom can make the entire field look three years old. It is not. Many of the ideas are decades old; what changed at different times was the technical capability, the amount of computing power and data available, the scale, the public access or the label everyone suddenly started using.

### A short timeline of overlapping AI ideas

| Period | What was happening | What to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1950s–1960s** | Researchers established AI as a field and explored rule-based problem solving, search, early neural networks and machine interaction. | AI and neural-network ideas are not new inventions of the chatbot era. |
| **1970s–1980s** | Expert systems used large sets of human-written rules in specialised areas. Research on robots, planning and learning continued. | Rule-based AI was a major practical approach, not a primitive prelude everyone abandoned. |
| **1990s–2000s** | Machine-learning systems became increasingly useful for recognition, prediction, ranking, recommendations, search and speech. | Predictive AI was already shaping ordinary products long before the generative-AI boom. |
| **2010s** | Deep-learning advances, more data and much greater computing power dramatically improved image, speech and language systems. | Older ideas became far more capable at new scale. |
| **Late 2010s–2020s** | Large general-purpose and generative models made text, image, audio, code and multimodal capabilities widely accessible. | The consumer experience and scale are new even when the underlying research line is older. |
| **2020s** | “Agentic AI” became a popular product label for systems that combine models, tools, information and multi-step control. | Agents and automated planning have long research histories; the current label and product wave are newer. |
| **Still disputed or hypothetical** | AGI definitions and tests remain contested; ASI remains a future scenario. | A timeline of research does not prove an inevitable march toward either one. |

When someone calls an AI development “new,” the useful follow-up is: **which part is new?** The idea? The capability? The scale? The number of people who can use it? Or the name breeding in PowerPoint?

## Put the labels together on one real product

Return to the travel assistant. You upload a screenshot of wedding dates and ask it to find reasonable flights, build a weekend plan and stop before booking anything.

What can we truthfully say?

- It may use **machine learning** inside the broader field of **AI**.
- It performs several jobs: it may **recognise** dates in the screenshot, **estimate** travel times, **rank** flights, **recommend** options and **generate** the written plan.
- It is **multimodal** if it meaningfully connects the image with your written request.
- It is **agentic** if the complete system keeps hold of the goal, uses permitted tools, checks results and chooses next steps before stopping for your approval.
- It is not **embodied** merely because it took software actions. It has no physical body or controls in this example.
- The system may use a **general-purpose** model inside a **specialised** travel product.
- None of this makes it **AGI**, and it certainly does not make it **ASI**.

Notice what happened: the labels did not nest into one perfect family tree. They accumulated around one product because each answered a different question.

That is the whole chapter’s click:

> **Do not ask only, “What type of AI is it?” Ask, “What exactly is this label telling me about the system?”**

## Check that the labels now make sense

### Recognise it

A phone photo app finds every photograph containing your dog and creates a new birthday collage from the results. Which statement is the most useful?

1. It must be either predictive or generative, but not both.
2. Finding the dog photographs is a recognition job; creating the collage is a generative job.
3. It is agentic because it completed two jobs.
4. It is AGI because it understands dogs and birthdays.

**Answer: 2.** One product can combine a recognition job with a generative job. Completing more than one job does not by itself establish an agentic loop, and neither ability is evidence of AGI.

### Explain it

Without using the book’s exact wording, explain why **generative**, **multimodal** and **agentic** are not three levels on one ladder.

A strong explanation should include:

- generative tells us that the system produces new content;
- multimodal tells us that it works across more than one form of information;
- agentic tells us that the complete system works through a goal, results and next steps; and
- one product can have all three properties, or only one or two.

### Draw it

Write **AI** in the centre of a page. Around it, draw six branches:

1. How was it built?
2. What job does it do?
3. What forms of information does it use?
4. Does it work through goals and actions?
5. Does it sense or act in the physical world?
6. How broad are its abilities, and what evidence supports that claim?

Choose one AI product you use. Put it beside every branch that applies. Leave a question mark where you do not know. An honest unknown is better than a confident decorative label.

### Use it

Your company proposes an “agentic multimodal recruitment solution.” Before anyone approves it, what can those labels tell you—and what can they not tell you?

**Reasoned answer:** *Multimodal* suggests the system may work with more than one form of information, perhaps résumés, written answers, audio or video. *Agentic* suggests the complete system may work through steps, tools and results toward a recruitment goal. Neither label tells you which information is actually used, what jobs the system performs, whether it ranks or rejects candidates, which actions it may take, how accurate or fair it is, whether a person reviews the result, or what happens when it is wrong. Those are the next questions.

If your answer found both the information forms **and** the missing decisions, you have the organizing idea. You are not memorizing labels. You are learning how to interrogate a system.

## Key Definitions

- **Artificial intelligence (AI):** the broad field of making computer systems interpret information and produce useful results such as recognition, estimates, recommendations, content, plans or actions.
- **Rule-based AI:** an AI approach in which people write the conditions and responses the system follows.
- **Machine learning:** an AI approach in which a system improves at finding useful patterns from examples instead of relying only on a person writing every rule.
- **Predictive AI:** a loose public label for systems that estimate a likely category, value, outcome or order from available information.
- **Generative AI:** AI that produces new content for a request, such as text, images, audio, video or code.
- **Multimodal AI:** an AI model, system or product that works across more than one form of information, such as text and images.
- **Agentic AI:** a complete AI system arranged to work through a goal, next steps, tools or actions, results and stopping decisions.
- **Embodied AI:** AI connected to physical sensors, machines or controls so it can sense or act in the physical world.
- **Specialised AI:** AI designed for a particular task or bounded area of work.
- **General-purpose AI:** AI capabilities that can support many different kinds of tasks.
- **AGI:** a disputed proposed category for broadly adaptable artificial general intelligence across unfamiliar intellectual work; there is no universally accepted definition or test.
- **ASI:** a hypothetical artificial superintelligence far beyond the strongest human abilities across most or all important intellectual areas.
