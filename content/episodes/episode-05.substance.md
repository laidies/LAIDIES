## 1 · THE CAPABILITY — a verb phrase, or it didn't ship

**She can inspect an AI product and separate seven questions: the
product/operator, model developer/provider, public model label, documented
release, task mode, plan/access and external source/retrieval use—without
treating them as seven names for the same thing.**

The observable finish line: give her a product screen plus its current primary
documentation and she can answer:

- **What product am I using, and who operates it?**
- **Who developed or supplies the selected model?**
- **What public model label does the product show?**
- **Does the provider document a specific release/model ID?**
- **What does this product say its task mode changes?**
- **What does my plan let me access or use?**
- **Did this answer actually retrieve or use external sources, and what proves
  it?**

Valid answers include **shown**, **not shown**, **not disclosed**, **unknown**,
**not applicable**, **documented automatic routing** and **documented
multi-model workflow**. Hidden is not the same as routed.

This episode gives her the map. Episode 6 teaches her how to choose from it.

## 2 · THE PLAIN TEACHING — before any analogy

> **The company operating the app and the company supplying the model may be
> the same—or not. The product may show a public model label and a documented
> release, or it may hide/route them. A task mode changes whatever that product
> says it changes. A subscription controls access and limits. Search may bring
> newer material into this task; it does not retrain the model and it does not
> prove the answer is current or correct.**

### The Super Models thesis

In the 1990s, the public followed supermodels by name, watched the houses and
campaigns they appeared in, and treated a new arrival or major booking as news.
AI models now produce a similar kind of attention: a new name/release becomes
the It girl, everyone wants to know what changed, and launch coverage can feel
like the race to book the September issue.

That cultural parallel makes the names memorable, but the episode uses it to
decode the machinery:

- **store:** the product and the company operating it;
- **house:** the developer/provider supplying the selected model;
- **star:** the public model label;
- **campaign/season:** a documented release/model ID, when one is actually
  disclosed;
- **service:** the product’s documented task mode/workflow;
- **membership:** plan/access/limits;
- **research packet:** external source material actually retrieved or used for
  this answer.

The factual bend must be spoken: fashion houses did not manufacture or own
supermodels, and supermodels worked across houses. AI developers do build
model families; outside products may then carry or route to those models.

### Two racks, not seven things to memorize

**Rack A — What is this?**

1. product/operator;
2. model developer/provider;
3. public model label;
4. documented release/model ID.

**Rack B — What affected this use?**

5. task mode/workflow;
6. plan/access;
7. external source/retrieval use.

The technical relationships are not one nested hierarchy:

```text
model developer/provider → creates → model(s) / documented model IDs
product operator → runs → product/app → offers or routes to → model(s)/workflows

subscription plan → controls access to products/models/modes/tools/limits
task mode/workflow → changes whatever that product documents
external source retrieval → may supply material to this answer
```

Important: not every product exposes every layer. Sometimes the product routes
the request and **the store chooses for you**; sometimes it simply does not
disclose what happened. Claim automatic routing only when documentation proves
it.

### Decode each field once

| Receipt question | Fashion hook | Technical job | Honest limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product/operator | Store | The service/interface and company operating it | A product may carry another developer’s models |
| Model developer/provider | House | The company/team developing or supplying the selected model | May be hidden or one of several providers |
| Public model label | Star | The marketed name shown to the user | May be a family, tier, alias, routed label or workflow—not proof of a pinned model |
| Documented release/model ID | Campaign/season | A documented version or pinned model identity, where exposed | Numbers/names are provider-specific; not every label identifies a fixed checkpoint |
| Task mode/workflow | Service level | The current product control (for example depth, speed, thinking or workflow) | May change token/tool effort, route differently or combine several changes; check documentation |
| Subscription plan | Membership/access | Models, modes, tools, limits or usage the account can access | Paying may unlock a different model/tool, but does not magically improve one fixed model |
| External source/retrieval use | Research packet | Source material actually retrieved or used for this answer | Availability is not use; newer is not automatically current, relevant or correct |

### The training-photo concept

Training produces a model whose learned parameters do not rewrite themselves
each morning. The fashion photograph/poster is the memory handle for a pinned
model snapshot: after the shoot, that captured state stays fixed until a new
model/release is produced.

It is **not** a database or photograph of every training example, and it does
not freeze the whole product. Routing, tools, safety systems and serving
infrastructure around fixed model weights can still change.

Do not overstate this as one universal, perfectly clean “knowledge cutoff.”
Products may combine differently dated training, later tuning and current
external source retrieval. The durable distinction is:

- **new documented model release:** the model snapshot itself may have changed;
- **external search/retrieval:** material is placed in front of the model for
  this task; inspect the source and date;
- **product/infrastructure change:** behaviour around the model may change even
  when a documented pinned model ID does not.

Episode 3 bridge:

> **A confident wrong answer may be invented, or it may be working from
> material that is no longer current. Search can change the material available.
> The Receipts Pass still tells you whether the sources deserve your trust.**

## 3 · THE WORKED EXAMPLE — biggest block in the episode

### “What did I actually buy?”

Start with a complete **fictional composite receipt** so every field can be
taught honestly. It is labelled fictional on screen and in narration:

```text
STUDIO DESK                         FICTIONAL TEACHING COMPOSITE
Product operated by: Northstar Work
Selected model label: Muse
Model supplied by: Atelier AI
Documented model ID: muse-4-2
Task mode: Deep Review
Plan: Studio Plus
External sources used for this answer: No
```

The fictional product documentation says:

- `muse-4-2` is a pinned model ID supplied by Atelier AI;
- **Deep Review** uses more response/tool effort within that model;
- **Studio Plus** unlocks Deep Review and raises usage limits; and
- external search was available but was not used for this answer.

Her question:

> **“I pay for Studio Plus, I opened Studio Desk, it says Muse 4.2, and Deep
> Review is on. Which one is the AI?”**

Answer key:

| Receipt question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Product/operator | Studio Desk, operated by Northstar Work |
| Model developer/provider | Atelier AI |
| Public model label | Muse |
| Documented release/model ID | `muse-4-2`—documented as pinned in this fictional example |
| Task mode/workflow | Deep Review—documented to change response/tool effort |
| Subscription access | Studio Plus—unlocks the mode and raises limits |
| External source/retrieval use | None for this answer, even though search exists |

The key line:

> **You usually bought access to a service. You did not buy or own the model.**

Then change one field at a time and ask **what changed and what stayed the
same**:

1. Switch off Deep Review: product, provider, model ID and plan stay the same.
2. Select another documented model: product and plan stay the same; the model
   may change.
3. Use external search: the pinned model is not secretly retrained; material
   is supplied to this answer and still needs source/date checks.
4. Compare plan labels: access/limits may change. The plan does not magically
   improve one fixed model, although it may unlock a different model, mode or
   tool.

Only after that clean teaching composite, show one messy real product Ali uses.
Its labels and primary documentation must be captured and reverified on
recording day. For every question, record **shown / not shown / not disclosed /
unknown / not applicable / documented automatic routing / documented
multi-model workflow**. Do not force one screen to reveal what it does not.

No volatile version name enters evergreen narration merely because it was
current during drafting.

The worked example is successful only if the distinction becomes usable. It
cannot be a fashion glossary followed by a technical glossary.

## 4 · THE RULE SHE CAN USE

> **Do not ask “Which one of these names is the AI?” Read the receipt in two
> racks: what it is—product/operator, provider, public model label and
> documented release—then what affected this use—mode, plan/access and
> external source/retrieval use. Say “not disclosed” when the product does not
> tell you.**

This is an identification rule, not a model-selection rule. Episode 6 will
teach the selection question:

> **What is the occasion, and which model/mode fits it?**

## 5 · THE ANALOGY — and its strip test

The receipt table in Section 2 is the one mapping authority. Do not teach a

Open on the confusing receipt, introduce the 1990s Super Models thesis, and

Department stores and specialist shops receive one preview line each:

  providers/models/workflows;
- **specialist shop:** a product may focus on one medium/job, whether it built
  the underlying model or uses somebody else’s.

The full specialist tour stays in Episode 14.

### Where it bends

- A public model label is not always a pinned model ID. Use
  release/version/checkpoint language only when primary documentation supports
  it.
- A product is more than a shop window: it supplies tools, files, memory,
  settings and user experience.
- Provider taxonomies differ. Couture/ready-to-wear/resort cannot be presented
  as a universal official tier system.
- A task mode may change effort, tools, workflow or routing; use the product’s
  documented meaning.
- A plan controls access and limits; it is not intelligence.
- A department-store product may let the shopper choose, route automatically
  or do both.
- A specialist app may build its own model or wrap another provider.
- Models are made by teams. “Designer changes houses” is later industry
  context, not a one-designer/one-model claim.
- New It-girl/September-issue attention explains launch hype, not quality.

### Strip test

Delete every fashion word. The episode must still teach the seven-question
receipt, honest unknown/hidden states, and the difference between a new model
snapshot, a product/workflow change and external source/retrieval use. If it
does not, the analogy has become decoration.

## 6 · THE TRY-ON — “READ YOUR RECEIPT”

Open one AI product she already uses and record:

1. What app/product is open, and who operates it?
2. Who develops/supplies the selected model, if disclosed?
3. What public model label is displayed, if any?
4. Does primary documentation identify a release/model ID?
5. Is there a task mode/workflow control, and what does the product say it
   changes?
6. What does the current plan control—access, models, modes, tools or limits?
7. Can the product search or retrieve external sources?
8. For this specific answer, did it actually use retrieved sources? What
   source/date proves that?

For every answer, use one honest status:

> **shown · not shown · not disclosed · unknown · not applicable · documented
> automatic routing · documented multi-model workflow**

Do not turn “hidden” into “the store chooses for me.” Routing is a legitimate
discovery only when the product documents it.

Finish this sentence:

> **I’m using [product], operated by [company]. Its displayed model is
> [label/not shown], supplied by [provider/unknown]. Its documented release is
> [ID/not shown], its task mode is [mode/not offered/unknown], my plan controls
> [access/limits], and this answer’s retrieved external sources are
> [used/not used/unknown—with evidence].**

The exercise ends with two “what changed?” scenarios, not another prompt:

- If an answer may be stale, does raising task effort or changing the plan
  supply newer source material?
- If external search is available, what proves this answer actually used it
  and that the source is current enough?

Do **not** infer that two apps use the same underlying model because their
answers sound alike. Similar outputs are not identity evidence. Use an
explicit label or current primary product documentation.

Before Gate 1 can be approved, the draft must support this no-brand test:

excerpt. Without provider rankings or a prompt, she can:

1. identify the product/operator separately from the model provider;
2. classify the public model label without claiming it is a pinned model ID
   unless the documentation says so;
3. distinguish plan/access from task mode/workflow;
4. determine whether external source retrieval was available versus actually
   used;

   date/quality checks; and

   inventing the missing architecture.
