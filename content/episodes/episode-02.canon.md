## lesson
How to write specific, context-rich prompts that produce usable work output.
**One-liner:** AI can't read your mind — so tell it what you want.

## narrative
The beats the script (spoken) and the article (read) both render, in order:

1. **Cold open.** 9:15 on a Tuesday, losing a staring contest with a paragraph. She asked AI for
   meeting talking points and got word-salad ("leverage synergies," "drive alignment"). Yesterday the
   *same tool* wrote a director update she barely touched. Same app, same her. *"Why does AI read my
   mind some days, and completely ignore me on others?"*
2. **To town.** Corner table at the Blend & Snap. She sets the two outputs side by side. The variable
   that changed wasn't the tool — **it was the ask.** AI can't read your mind; what's in your head
   stays in your head until you type it out.
3. **The coffee-order analogy** *(→ concept: Context).* Your regular café knows your usual because you
   built that over a hundred Tuesdays. AI is the **brand-new café across town** — say "the usual" and
   you get a blank look. It has your words and nothing else: not your job, not your reader, not the
   meeting on your calendar. Spell it out and the guessing stops.
4. **How long until it knows your order?** Right now — it doesn't; every new chat you're a stranger
   again. But you can teach it to remember you. *That's a future episode (AI memory / context).* For
   today: assume it forgot, and tell it again.
5. **The Spice Girls** *(→ concept: Prompt).* *"Tell me what you want, what you really, really want"* —
   they wanted specifics, not a vibe. AI is the same. (Aside: *"decades ago… that is a jagged little
   pill to swallow, but here we are."*)
6. **David Rose / fold in the cheese.** Moira says "fold in the cheese," David screams "WHAT DOES THAT
   MEAN?!" — that's your AI every time you type "write me an email about the project." It's trying, but
   you handed it "fold in the cheese" when it needed "scrape the spatula along the bottom, lift, turn
   it over, turn the bowl, repeat."
7. **The fix — brief it like a new hire.** Stop typing *at* AI like a Google search; brief it like a
   smart new hire in her first week: who's this for? what do they care about? tone? length? what should
   it NOT include? any example to copy? *Prompting isn't technical — it's delegation.* Steal one move:
   **show it an example** ("match this").
8. **The demo** *(→ comparison block).* Vague vs specific policy summary (full text below).
9. **Iterate like a new hire.** If the first answer's off, you don't start over — you tell it what's
   wrong. *It's calling into the radio and asking for exactly what you want to hear (or shelling out
   twenty bucks at HMV for the CD) — not constantly changing the station.*
10. **Soft-skills reframe** *(→ facts).* At the LIBRAiRY she finds the proof: the Harvard/BCG study.
    The differentiator wasn't technical — it was knowing how to brief, hand over context, push back.
    *The skills filed under "soft" for a generation are the ones that now separate the winners — not
    instead of the hard skills, on top of them.* A sharp communicator with real judgment **who also
    knows her stuff** was never the soft skill set. That's the one that wins.

## concepts[]
*(→ article glossary · learn/glossary.html · LIBRAiRY · concept cards · SUNNYVAiLE High course)*

- **Prompt** — the ask you give an AI: the instructions for the work you want back.
  **Analogy (canon):** briefing a smart new hire — who it's for, what they care about, tone, length,
  what to leave out, an example to copy. Specific brief → usable work.
- **Context** — everything the AI can't see unless you say it: your job, your reader, the meeting, the
  goal. **Analogy (canon):** the coffee order. Your regular spot knows your usual; AI is the new café
  across town — spell it out every time and the guessing stops.
- **Token** — the small word-pieces AI reads and counts (roughly how length/cost is measured).
  *Listed in the Ep2 glossary but **not taught** in this episode's story — keep the definition light
  here; it gets its real treatment when an episode needs it. Do not over-explain it in Ep2 surfaces.*

## comparison
*(The generalized **flat way vs fluent way** block. Topic-agnostic pattern — this episode fills it with
vague-vs-specific; Ep3 will fill it with trusted-blindly vs verified, etc. → article prompt-lab block · FAiRY Godmother.)*

- **The task:** summarize a **dense** 12-page policy change (regulatory language, cross-refs, nested
  defined terms) before a 2 p.m. meeting — she pastes the whole PDF in. **Why AI matters here (Ali
  2026-07-07):** the value is NOT writing the summary (she could do that herself) — it's *reading*
  twelve pages under time pressure and surfacing what matters, including a buried catch she'd have
  missed. **Rule for every "why bother" example:** pick a task where AI does the genuinely hard/tedious
  part (digest a big/dense input, catch the buried thing) — never something so straightforward the
  reader thinks "I'd just do that myself."
- **The flat way (what you'd type without ladies):** *"summarize this policy change for my
  stakeholders."* → NOT four short vague sentences (that's unrealistic on a dense doc). A vague ask on
  12 dense pages yields a **long, over-detailed dump**: it faithfully summarizes everything — background,
  rationale, audit findings, every section, appendices, escalation procedures — because it doesn't know
  what matters to *you*, buries the action items, and prioritizes nothing. *Accurate, thorough, and
  useless in the two minutes you have; you still have to do the reading.* **RULE (Ali 2026-07-07): the
  vague-prompt failure mode is OVER-detail, not brevity.**
- **The fluent way (the brief, out loud):** Who's it for? Six senior managers with two minutes before
  a meeting. What do they need? What's changing, when it takes effect, what their teams do differently,
  whether it touches budget. What do we cut? The backstory. What shape? Bullets, ~150 words. And the
  move nobody makes: *"here's last quarter's summary that landed — match it."*
- **The result (approved 2026-07-07):** a realistic exec summary drafted **the way the prompt asked** —
  subject line "Compliance Reporting — Q4 Policy Update," then complete-sentence bullets: *What's
  changing* (dual sign-off, was just the lead) · *When it takes effect* (Oct 1; first dual-signed report
  due Dec 31) · *What your team does differently* (route through finance partner; +3 business days/cycle)
  · *Budget impact* · *Who's affected* (full-time teams only — contractors/vendor teams exempt until Q1)
  · *What we need from you* (name finance partner by Sep 15). ~145 words (the brief asked ~150), forwardable as-is; ninety
  seconds to write, forty-five to check. **DRAFT RULE:** complete professional sentences + a subject line
  + matches a recurring template — never telegraphic AI fragments; realistic enough to actually forward.
  **The "Who's affected" line is the "why AI" moment** (AI read all 12 pages and surfaced the carve-out) —
  phrase it as a clean brief caveat, never "buried on p.9" chatter; the "buried on page nine" story lives
  in the article's *narration*, never in the output.
  **RULES for this example (Ali 2026-07-07):** use **real months** (never bare "the 1st / 15th"), span a
  realistic multi-month rollout, and **never "Your move"** inside a professional output — that isn't
  brief-to-senior-managers language. The spoken script may condense; the article carries the full
  structured version.

## try_on
*(→ try-on.html · article · study pack at Blend & Snap. ~10 minutes, not homework.)*
Hand one real task to an AI tool **twice**. First the lazy way — three vague words, the way you'd
Google it. Then the David Rose way: who it's for, what they care about, the tone, the length, and what
to leave out. Put the two answers side by side. The difference isn't the tool getting smarter between
tries — it's you getting specific.
