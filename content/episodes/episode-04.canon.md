## lesson
AI was never one thing, and never new — it's a real science almost two hundred years old, built leap after leap by women. Every major advance (the algorithm, frequency-hopping, the compiler, search, computer vision) has a woman's name on it.
**One-liner:** You were never behind on AI — you were just never told it was yours.

## narrative
The beats, in order. This episode has **surface drift** between the spoken narration and the current written article (v4) — flagged inline. Reconcile before ship.

1. **Cold open — the slow Wednesday.** Three weeks of talking to this thing every day — briefing it, arguing with it, catching it lying to her face — and she couldn't tell you the first thing about where it came from, what it even is, who made it. *"It's like I moved in with someone and realized I'd never once asked to meet their family."* The question underneath: is this thing actually **new** — and who **built** it? *(Both surfaces.)*
2. **"Just use AI" isn't an instruction.** A coworker said "just use AI for that" — as if "AI" were a complete instruction and not a whole mall directory wearing one name tag. It's like being told to "just use internet." *Use it for what?* Nobody hands you the map. **⚠ NARRATION-ONLY** — cut from the v4 article (which jumps straight to "no new trick, this week's a flashback"). This beat seeds Episode 5's "which AI" question; keep or drop deliberately when reconciling.
3. **Up the hill to the LUMINAiRY.** SUNNYVAiLE's hall of heroes. Everyone knows the front room — the patron saints, the Chers and the Elles. Behind it, a quiet wing she'd never walked into: *"…Well. Almost no movie stars."* The women who actually built the thing this whole town is about. They call them the **MAiVENS**. Lights go soft, somewhere a harp — we're going back. *(Both surfaces.)*
4. **Ada Lovelace · 1843 · The Idea** *(→ concept: Algorithm).* Before the lightbulb, a young woman stares at a giant mechanical calculator everyone else saw as arithmetic — and sees that if a machine can follow precise-enough instructions, *numbers are only the beginning*; it could work with symbols, set them to music. She writes the instructions down: **the first algorithm.** And she tells us exactly what it can't be: *"it has no pretensions to originate anything. It only ever does what we know how to order it to do."* For a hundred years they hand the credit to the man whose machine it was. *(Both.)*
5. **Hedy Lamarr · 1942 · The Signal.** Billed as the most beautiful woman in the world — and inventing between takes. *"It is a very useful thing… to be underestimated — no one watches what you're actually doing."* With composer **George Antheil** she designs a system that *hops* frequency to frequency, too fast to jam: *"You cannot jam a signal you cannot find."* The Navy shelved it; the idea is in the family tree of Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. She never made a dime from it. *(Both.)*
6. **The ENIAC Six · 1945 · The First Program.** The Army in Philadelphia switches on a **thirty-ton, room-sized** machine, the **ENIAC**, built for artillery tables. With no manual and no programming language — *one didn't exist yet* — **six women (Jean, Betty, Kay, Marlyn, Ruth, and Frances)** program it by hand, cable by cable, switch by switch. *"They are, quite literally, the first programmers."* When it's shown to the press, the men get named; the six women, standing right there, are assumed to be **models**. It took about forty years to learn who they were. *(Both.)*
7. **Grace Hopper · 1952 · The Language** *(→ concept: Compiler).* Talking to a machine was agony — raw code, by hand. The Navy almost didn't take her (too old, at thirty-six). She found "a computer can't understand words" a failure of imagination: *why should a person have to think like a machine?* So she built a **compiler** — write in near-plain English, it converts to code. Every app sits on that idea. (The moth taped into the logbook — *"first actual case of a bug being found"* — is where *debugging* comes from.) *(Both.)*
8. **1956 · The naming — the men show up.** A handful of men get a room at Dartmouth for the summer and christen the dream: *"artificial intelligence."* Names on it as the founding fathers. They promise it'll be basically solved by the end of the summer. **It was *not* solved by the end of the summer** — not for decades. The funding dried up, the promises curdled, and "AI" became an embarrassing thing to say out loud: the **AI winters** (roughly two). But even in the cold, the work didn't stop. *(Both.)*
9. **Karen Spärck Jones · 1972 · The Finding.** A woman at Cambridge cracks how a machine finds the *right* thing: the common words ("the," "and," "is") tell you nothing — it's the **rare** words that carry the meaning, so she builds a way to weigh them. It's the arithmetic under every search box, and under the modern systems that look things up before they answer. No permanent post until 1993 (which she said plainly was because she was a woman). Her line, carved over the door of the episode: *"Computing is too important to be left to men."* *(Both.)*
10. **Fei-Fei Li · 2012 · The Sight** *(→ concept: Training data).* After almost everyone gave up — the thaw. A Stanford professor's heretical idea: the brain was never the problem; *we'd never given it enough to look at.* She builds millions upon millions of labeled images — the **training data** — and in **2012** a program trained on her pictures suddenly *sees.* That's the spark of the "AI boom." The world calls her the **Godmother of AI**. *"Godmother. Not godfather."* *(Both.)*
11. **And then it moves fast — the day it landed on your desk.** **2017**, a team at Google publishes a design that finally cracks language — the thing can really *write.* **November 2022**, someone wraps it in a chat box, puts it online free, and calls it **ChatGPT**. *(→ facts: the ~100M estimate + caveat.)* The day AI stopped being a lab thing and became a *you* thing — the very desk she sat at three weeks ago. *(Both.)*
12. **The checkers — the women who check the whole machine.** **Joy Buolamwini** (MIT): face-detection couldn't see her dark skin until she held up a white mask — she proves the big systems fail hardest on darker-skinned women. **Timnit Gebru** + linguist **Emily Bender**: these machines can sound brilliant while understanding nothing — Bender's name for it, the *stochastic parrot.* Gebru raised it inside Google and in 2020 was abruptly gone; thousands protested. **Kate Crawford** maps the mines, water, electricity, and underpaid hands behind the "magic": *"AI is neither artificial nor intelligent."* *"These are the women who check the whole machine."* *(Both.)*
13. **The lights come up.** Alone in the back of the LUMINAiRY, holding all of it — almost two hundred years, a room full of women who had to fight to be believed, one very confident chatbot. *Not magic. Not born last Tuesday.* **⚠ NARRATION adds** a line the v4 article doesn't: the newest chapter still being written — *agentic AI, the kind that doesn't just answer you, it goes and does the thing* — "the edge of the map right now." (Article-only heading: *"Not magic. Not born last Tuesday."*)
14. **Cocktail party.** *"Is this whole AI thing brand new, or what?"* — it's almost two hundred years old and about three years old at the same time. *(→ cocktail_party, full text below.)* *(Both.)*

## concepts[]
*(→ article glossary / "The Vocab" · learn/glossary.html · LIBRAiRY · concept cards · SUNNYVAiLE High. The four terms are locked in `issue-04.json → glossaryTerms`.)*

- **Algorithm** — a precise, repeatable set of steps for solving a problem: the recipe a machine follows, in order, every time. **Anchor (canon):** Ada Lovelace wrote the first one in 1843, for a machine that wouldn't be built for another century. Not magic, not thinking — very careful instructions, followed exactly.
- **Compiler** — a translator that turns instructions written in something close to plain language into the raw code a machine actually runs. **Anchor (canon):** Grace Hopper built one of the first, so people would stop having to think like the machine. Every app you tap sits on top of that one idea. *(Ledger guard: say "one of the first compilers" — the A-0 System.)*
- **AI winter** — one of the long stretches (roughly two) when AI's big promises outran what it could actually do, the funding froze, and "artificial intelligence" became an embarrassing thing to say out loud. The work didn't stop; it just went quiet, until the thaw.
- **Training data** — the examples a model learns from; the more it sees, the more it can do. **Anchor (canon):** Fei-Fei Li's insight was that the bottleneck was never the machine's "brain" — no one had shown it enough of the world. Her ImageNet (millions of labeled pictures) is why, in 2012, a machine finally learned to see.

Spell **Karen Spärck Jones** (ä, no hyphen). **Source:** Wikipedia; J. of Documentation 1972; ITNOW/Oxford Academic. · **Verified** 2026-07-09.

## comparison
*(The generalized **flat way vs fluent way** block. This is a **history episode with no vague-vs-specific task demo** — there is no prompt-lab in Ep4. The closest structural equivalent, and the episode's actual rhetorical pivot, is **the highlight reel vs the real record** — the men who *named* it vs the women who *built* it. Filled that way; if a future template needs a task demo here, mark it PENDING rather than inventing one.)*

- **The setup:** "Is this whole AI thing brand new, and was it built by a handful of famous founding fathers?"
- **The flat way (the highlight reel):** AI is a recent invention; a room of men at Dartmouth in 1956 named it, put their names on it as the founding fathers, and the story runs from there. Clean, familiar, and the version the textbook wrote down.
- **The fluent way (the real record):** the science is almost two hundred years old, and every real leap has a woman's name on it — **the idea** (Ada, 1843), **the signal** (Hedy, 1942), **the first program** (the ENIAC Six, 1945), **the language** (Grace, 1952), **the finding** (Karen, 1972), **the sight** (Fei-Fei, 2012) — plus the women who now **check the whole machine** (Joy, Timnit, Emily, Kate). The men named it; the women built it. Your access is three years old; the work is two centuries deep.
- **The payoff:** overwhelm becomes belonging. It's very hard to feel behind on something the moment you learn it took almost two hundred years and a hundred brilliant women to hand it to you.
