## lesson
How to check, challenge, source, and contain AI output when it sounds confident but may be wrong,
missing context, or not relevant to the real work.
**One-liner:** AI can sound right — and sounding right is not the same thing as being right; before
your name goes on it, know which kind of answer you're holding. *(Sign-off form: "AI can write like
Regina George. You still need to check like Elle Woods.")*

## narrative
The beats the script (spoken) and the article (read) both render, in order. *(Where a beat appears in
only one surface, it's flagged.)*

1. **Cold open — the tiny office miracle that lies.** Thursday afternoon; she asks an AI tool to turn
   four days of messy meeting notes into a clean client update. Nine seconds later: structured, calm,
   "per our discussion," two clicks from sending. Then one sentence catches her eye — the draft says the
   client *"approved"* a July rollout. But she was *in* that meeting; nobody approved anything. What was
   actually said: *"July could work, if procurement clears by Friday."* *"The machine took a maybe...
   and gave it a lanyard."*
2. **I couldn't help but wonder…** if a paragraph can sound that sure of itself — *"hair done, makeup
   done, 'per our discussion' and everything"* — how do you catch the one line that's quietly, completely
   wrong? *Chutney-before-the-perm-timeline energy: confident, composed, one tiny detail from collapse.*
3. **To town** *(script-primary — the article reworks this framing).* She takes it to Main Street — the
   **NewsStand on Main**, whose job is separating what happened from what just sounds like it did. The
   draft didn't need a better writer; it needed the news desk holding every confident line up to the
   light: *says who? based on what?* Fact-check the front page before it runs under your byline.
4. **The Burn Book Problem** *(→ concept: Hallucination).* The Burn Book didn't work because it was true
   — *none of it was, and nobody checked.* It worked because it had **social authority**: a rumor, a
   grudge, a wild guess, and something fully unhinged, all in the same handwriting, same devastating
   certainty. AI writes in that same hand — but it doesn't only write rumors. It'll take a real source,
   an old source, a similar-but-not-*this* source, and an assumption it made because the pattern looked
   familiar, and hand you one smooth paragraph like everyone in it belongs together. *An official court
   record and a Burn Book entry come out in the exact same handwriting — you can't tell which is which
   until you check.* **That is the Burn Book Problem: unsupported information can look just as finished as
   supported information.**
5. **Bethany Byrd — the tiny sourcing disaster.** The Burn Book claims Bethany must be lying about being
   a virgin *because she buys super-jumbo tampons.* One box → a verdict. Then the real explanation walks
   in, less scandalous and more specific: she's just got a heavy flow. *That was never evidence — it's a
   clue in a Claire's headband, sprinting straight to a conclusion. One data point. No context. Enormous
   conclusion.*
6. **Can I use AI? Yes.** *"We are not here to churn butter by candlelight."* The real question: which
   parts is it just drafting for you — and which parts are **claims that need receipts** before they
   borrow your name?
7. **She doesn't even go here** *(→ concept: Assumption / relevance check).* Some wrong answers are easy
   (the product that doesn't exist; the answer that argues with itself). The **fake citation** is dressed
   to pass — *"cited with the confidence of 'my boyfriend goes to another school'"* — until you click it
   and it goes nowhere. The sneakiest isn't fake at all: it's **real information in the wrong place** —
   a U.S. HR answer in a Canadian workplace; last year's pricing page wearing this year's lip gloss;
   *"we talked about it"* promoted to *"we decided"*; a policy answer technically true except the
   exception is the part that matters. That's when you stand up in the back, blue hoodie + oversized
   sunglasses, and yell: *she doesn't even go here.* Not just a line — a **quality-control standard.**
   The pre-use questions: is this about our *actual* company/customer/tool/policy/market/date/decision?
   Did AI say what I gave it, or quietly add what it inferred? What claim would make me look unprepared
   if wrong? What detail would make the story fall apart? Where's the receipt?
8. **Elle Woods would like to see the file** *(→ concept: Verification).* Chutney says she was in the
   shower right after getting a perm. Elle asks again; Chutney gives the same story; the room rolls its
   eyes. But Elle isn't checking whether Chutney can repeat herself — *she's waiting for the detail that
   doesn't fit.* You don't wash a fresh perm. The story collapses. The pattern: **Claim** (in the shower)
   · **Timeline** (right after a perm) · **Domain knowledge** (you don't wash a fresh perm) ·
   **Contradiction** (story collapses) · **Receipts** (Elle has the file and the timing). *"Do not be
   Chutney on the stand. Be Elle with the timeline."*
9. **Cher's closet — sort into three piles** *(→ comparison block; concepts: Draft / Claim / Receipt).*
   The closet knows the pieces; it doesn't know the situation you're walking into. Let AI draft the
   outline, turn notes into a first pass, make a checklist, give you questions. But sort before it
   leaves your laptop — **Draft:** wording, structure, brainstorm, summary, checklist (can be fast) ·
   **Claim:** names, dates, numbers, quotes, sources, legal/HR/privacy/security/finance, customer
   commitments, policy interpretation (needs checking) · **Receipt:** the thing you can open, name, date,
   quote, or point to. *"A draft is an outfit. A claim is an alibi. Dress accordingly."*
10. **You're Elle, the machine is the law clerk** *(script-only warm beat).* Elle didn't have a clerk who
    already did the reading; the machine spent the weekend in the library and drafted the brief. You still
    read it, still check the citations, still walk in and own it — *"the grunt work got done for you; the
    judgment stayed yours."*
11. **Chutney can say it thrice** *(→ facts).* Asking AI *"are you sure?"* is *"like asking Regina George
    whether the Burn Book is peer reviewed. Bold choice. Limited value."* Re-asking and getting the same
    answer back isn't verification — it's Chutney repeating the alibi. The tools *are* getting better
    (search, cite, read uploaded docs, sometimes flag uncertainty) — *but better is not solved.* Proof:
    the 2026 Nature paper, Stanford's 2026 AI Index (sycophancy), the Stanford legal-RAG study, and KPMG's
    pulled report (40 of 45 citations fabricated). *"Sources attached" ≠ "sources checked."* Big Four,
    tiny receipt drawer.
12. **David, meet Elle — Prompt Like Elle** *(→ try_on).* The serious guidance (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google,
    Stanford) all points the same way, and it's **three moves + one rule.** **Move one — give her the
    source:** paste the document / policy / this year's pricing; turn on search when freshness matters;
    then *"answer only from what I gave you."* **Move two — let her say "I don't know":** *"if it's not in
    there, say so; don't guess to be helpful; mark anything you inferred."* **Move three — make her show
    the line:** *"quote the exact sentence you relied on."* **The rule over all three — no invented
    receipts:** no made-up links, dates, quotes, numbers, policies, or certainty; mark a missing one
    `[needs receipt]`. Two more: ask for a **claim table** (claim, source, date, confidence, what would
    change the answer) and make the **second check independent** (don't let the same witness validate
    herself). *Then still check.* The full method lives on the **Verification Rulebook** shelf.
13. **The cocktail-party explanation** *(→ cocktail_party).* Why does it just make things up? *It's not
    lying — lying takes intent. It's guessing.* When it doesn't know, it reaches for the most
    plausible-sounding thing and says it with its whole chest — *your most confident friend.* The fix was
    never to catch her in a lie; it's to ask for the receipt before you repeat her in a meeting.
14. **The try-on / Receipts Pass.** Ten minutes, not homework. Take one real AI answer (a meeting recap, a
    summary of a public page, a draft reply) and verify three claims before it borrows your name.
15. **Sign-off.** *"Remember, lAIdies: AI can write like Regina George. You still need to check like Elle
    Woods."*

## concepts[]
*(→ article glossary · learn/glossary.html · LIBRAiRY / Verification Rulebook · concept cards · SUNNYVAiLE High.)*
**Load-bearing framework — the three piles** (article defines all three verbatim):
- **Draft** — useful wording, structure, brainstorm, summary, checklist. *"A draft is an outfit."* Can be fast.
- **Claim** — names, dates, numbers, quotes, sources, legal/HR/privacy/security/finance details, customer
  commitments, policy interpretation. *"A claim is an alibi."* Needs checking.
- **Receipt** — the thing you can open, name, date, quote, or point to if someone asks. *"Receipts are what
  keep you off the stand."*

**Glossary terms (data-declared for Ep3):** Hallucination · Source · Citation · Verification · Assumption.
- **Hallucination** — when AI fills a gap with plausible-sounding language instead of admitting it doesn't
  know. **Canon framing (cocktail line):** *it's not lying — lying takes intent — it's guessing; it reaches
  for the most plausible thing and says it with its whole chest.*
- **Verification** — checking a claim against something outside the same chat (a source, date, quote, number,
  domain rule, or human owner). **Anti-pattern (canon):** re-asking the model *"are you sure?"* — that's
  *"Chutney repeating the alibi,"* not verification.
- **Citation / Source** — the openable, nameable origin behind a claim. **Canon warning:** a **fake citation**
  can look exactly like a real source until you click it and it goes nowhere; *"sources attached" ≠ "sources
  checked."* ⏳ *The article uses "source" and "citation" but does not give a crisp one-line dictionary
  definition of each; definitions above are extracted from usage — tighten against learn/glossary.html when
  syncing, don't invent.*
- **Assumption** — what AI quietly *infers* and states as fact (e.g., *"we discussed it"* → *"approved"*;
  *"phase two"* → *"remove from scope"*). The relevance failure — *"she doesn't even go here"* — is the
  assumption check: right-sounding info that doesn't match your actual company/customer/date/decision.

## comparison
*(The generalized **flat way vs fluent way** block — Ep3 fills it with **trusted-blindly vs
sorted-and-checked** (Draft/Claim/Receipt). In the article this is the "Receipts Pass Study Montage" mini
example; in the **script it's the cold open.** → article demo block · Verification Rulebook · FAiRY Godmother.)*

- **The task:** turn messy meeting notes into a client update.
- **The input (notes):**
  ```text
  July could work if procurement clears by Friday.
  Training might move to phase two.
  Final approval: account owner to confirm.
  ```
- **The flat way (what AI hands back, tidy and wrong):**
  ```text
  The client approved a July rollout and asked us to remove the training module from scope.
  ```
  *"Tempting. Tidy. Wearing a lanyard."* Three assumptions smuggled in: "could work" → **approved**;
  "phase two" → **removed**; and the person with approval authority **wasn't on the call.**
- **The receipt check catches:** **Claims** = approved / July rollout / remove training · **Assumptions** =
  "we discussed it" became "approved"; "phase later" became "remove" · **Fragile detail** = who actually
  approved the timeline? · **Receipts** = the notes, transcript, and follow-up from the decision owner.
- **The fluent way (the usable version):**
  ```text
  We discussed a possible July rollout and a phased training approach. Approval is still pending. Confirm timeline and training scope with the account owner before updating the deck.
  ```
  *"Same useful structure. Less Chutney on the stand."*

## try_on
*(→ try-on.html · article "Receipts Pass Study Montage" · printable · Verification Rulebook. ~10 minutes, not homework.)*
Run **Prompt Like Elle** in your own AI tool on a low-risk task or source notes (a meeting-prep note, a
summary of a public page, a plain-language explanation, a draft reply, a comparison table, a list of
questions) — never confidential tea. Then verify **three claims** before it borrows your name:
1. one claim that leans on a name, date, number, quote, or link;
2. one claim that could be stale, jurisdiction-specific, or context-dependent;
3. one claim you'd be embarrassed to say out loud if someone asked *"where did that come from?"*
Quick source check — Stanford's three questions: **Who is behind this information? What is the evidence?
What do other sources say?** *This Week's Rule:* `I can use the draft. I still check the alibi.`
