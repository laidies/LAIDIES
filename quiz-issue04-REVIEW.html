Episode 04 Quiz — The Founding Mothers

Title: Almost two hundred years of women built this — pop quiz.

Intro: Answer ten Episode 4 questions from the quiet wing of the LUMINAiRY, then try the two bonus questions teasing next week's episode. A perfect 12/10 gets two stickers, because we are not monsters.

Q1

What did Ada Lovelace understand first, back in 1843?

  • ✓ If a machine can follow instructions, numbers are only the beginning
  • Machines would one day think for themselves
  • Computers needed better keyboards
  • Programming should be left to mathematicians

Why: The founding insight of the whole field: precise instructions, faithfully followed. Everything in your browser tab descends from it.

Q2

Why was "just use AI" never a real instruction?

  • ✓ AI isn't one thing. It's a whole field, built piece by piece over almost two hundred years
  • Because AI is too new for instructions to exist
  • Because only engineers can use it properly
  • Because the tools change too fast to learn

Why: Ada's math, Grace's translator, Karen's search, Fei-Fei's data: different pieces, different jobs. Nobody hands you a map when they say "just use it." Next week, we draw the map.

Q3

The episode says AI has had roughly two "winters." What's an AI winter?

  • ✓ A long stretch when the promises outran the results and the money froze
  • The gap between one model's release and the next
  • When a government pauses AI research on safety grounds
  • The early period before computers could handle language

Why: After Dartmouth's overpromise, the funding dried up and "artificial intelligence" became an embarrassing thing to say out loud. It happened roughly twice. The work didn't stop in the cold; the money did.

Q4

Billed at the time as the most beautiful woman in the world, Hedy Lamarr spent 1942 inventing. Radio-controlled torpedoes kept getting jammed. What was her and George Antheil's fix?

  • ✓ Their signal never stayed on one frequency long enough to be blocked
  • The signal was encrypted with an unbreakable code
  • It broadcast at a power no jammer could overwhelm
  • It switched from radio to sonar

Why: It jumped constantly between dozens of frequencies, and the receiver jumped in the same pattern. You can't jam what you can't find. The Navy shelved it as too bulky, but frequency hopping is in the family tree of Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Hedy Lamarr: world-famous actor, incredible inventor. Both, fully.

Q5

What did Grace Hopper's compiler change?

  • ✓ You could write near-plain English and the machine did the translating
  • It let machines finally understand human intent
  • It replaced programming languages entirely
  • It made computers dramatically faster at calculation

Why: Everyone said computers could only do numbers; she called that a failure of imagination. Every plain sentence you type at an AI lives in her world.

Q6

What did the men who named "artificial intelligence" promise?

  • ✓ That it would be basically solved by the end of the summer
  • That machines would think like humans within a generation
  • That the field would never need another naming
  • That industry, not universities, would finish the job

Why: It took decades, not a summer. The funding froze, the promises curdled, and the field kept the founding fathers' names anyway.

Q7

How did Karen Spärck Jones teach machines to find what matters?

  • ✓ Weigh the rare words, because the common ones tell you nothing
  • Count every word equally
  • Only read the first paragraph
  • Ask a librarian

Why: "The" and "and" are useless; the unusual words carry the meaning. Every search you've ever run leans on her.

Q8

Everyone was chasing smarter algorithms, and colleagues warned Fei-Fei Li she had taken her idea too far. What was the idea?

  • ✓ The brain was never the problem. The machine had just never seen enough examples
  • The machines needed to be bigger, not smarter
  • Language mattered more than images
  • AI needed better marketing

Why: "Pre-ImageNet, people did not believe in data," as she put it. She built the mountain of examples anyway, and in 2012 image recognition won by a landslide. That was the spark of the current boom.

Q9

Face-detection software couldn't see Joy Buolamwini's dark skin until she held up a white mask. What's the real lesson?

  • ✓ AI has to work for everyone. That takes representative data and diverse builders, not data soaked in decades of bias
  • Always test software before trusting it
  • Early face detection was simply low-resolution
  • Bias patches itself out as models improve

Why: The software learned from data shaped by years of who got photographed, hired, and counted, so it inherited those gaps. That doesn't fix itself. It takes better data and more kinds of people in the room deciding what gets built, which is why who builds AI matters as much as what it can do.

Q10

Why does two hundred years of this history matter right now?

  • ✓ AI's next chapter is being written right now. Women built this field, and it needs them back in the room
  • So the historical record can finally be corrected
  • It makes AI feel less intimidating to newcomers
  • It shows the field has always been fair underneath

Why: From Ada to Fei-Fei, it was never a boys' field. It just gets told that way, and the credit and the seats still skew one direction today. The newest chapter is being written right now. That's the part you can do something about.
