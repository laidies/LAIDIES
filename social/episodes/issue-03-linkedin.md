## Launch-Day Post

AI can be useful and wrong in the same answer.

That is one of the first real trust lessons at work, because the risky answer is not always obvious nonsense.

Sometimes it has structure.
Sometimes it has tone.
Sometimes it has a table, a source-looking link, and one tiny detail that cannot survive a timeline check.

This week in LAiDIES, Issue 03 is about The Burn Book Problem: confidence is not evidence.

The Burn Book had the same problem. Same marker, same layout, same social authority. A true thing, a stale thing, a rumor, and a completely unsupported conclusion could all sit on the page with the same certainty.

AI can do that with better punctuation.

The work move is not fear. It is verification.

Before AI output goes into a deck, email, client note, policy question, budget conversation, or anything with your name on it, ask:

1. What claim am I relying on?
2. What source, date, quote, number, policy, or domain rule checks it?
3. What did AI infer instead of know?
4. What detail would make the answer fall apart?
5. Do I have the receipt?

Chutney can say it thrice. Elle still checks the timeline.

## Shorter Alternate

AI can sound confident and still be wrong.

The problem is not that AI is useless. The problem is that polish can make a supported claim, stale fact, assumption, wrong-context answer, and made-up source look equally finished.

So before the output gets your name on it:

Use the draft.
Check the alibi.
Find the receipt.

## Founder-Style Version From Ali

One of the first real AI confidence moments is not when you get a perfect answer.

It is when you catch a confident wrong one and realize you know enough to question it.

That is the moment Issue 03 of LAiDIES is built around.

I keep coming back to Elle Woods and Chutney on the stand, because the lesson is so practical. Elle does not win because she asks the same question over and over and gets the same answer. She wins because one detail does not fit the timeline.

That is what good AI use requires too.

AI can help you turn messy notes into a draft, outline, summary, table, or list of questions. That is useful.

But if the answer includes names, dates, numbers, links, quotes, pricing, policies, legal-sounding language, HR implications, privacy issues, security decisions, or anything someone could reasonably ask you to defend, it needs receipts.

The LAiDIES line this week:

I can use the draft. I still check the alibi.

This is not about becoming afraid of AI. It is about becoming harder to embarrass while using it.
