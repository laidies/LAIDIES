### Learner promise

Show people how to properly set up, organize, use, control and verify a current
AI tool—the LAiDIES way. The learner brings one real safe project and leaves
with a tested working system, not a list of features.

### Masterclass lessons

1. **Know Where You Are** — personal versus company/school
   workspace/environment, ownership, policies and available tools.
2. **Set It Up Once** — personal instructions, memory, data controls and safe
   preferences.
3. **Choose Chat or Work/Cowork** — conversation for thinking and guidance
   versus agentic execution for longer multi-step deliverables.
4. **From Chatting to Working** — briefing, stages, finish/queue/steer/stop and
   when to open another chat.
5. **Give the Work a Home** — when to use a Project/Notebook, how several chats
   or Work/Cowork runs fit inside it and when to create a separate project.
6. **Give It the Right Stuff** — files, sources, connected apps, retrieval and
   permissions.
7. **Finish Like a Professional** — verification, durable artifacts, clean
   review, decisions, version/status and handoff.

### Chat, Work/Cowork, project or workspace?

- **Chat:** discuss, question, search, brainstorm, explain, discover and refine
  with tight conversational control.
- **Work/Cowork/agent mode:** execute a longer multi-step job across the
  relevant sources, files, tools or apps, with progress, questions, direction
  changes and approvals where supported.
- **Project/notebook:** one ongoing body of work with reusable sources,
  instructions and several purposeful Chat or Work/Cowork sessions.
- **Workspace/account environment:** the broader personal, company or school
  world governing people, ownership, admin policy, privacy, available tools and
  many projects/chats.

Warn that vendors use “workspace” inconsistently. Teach what the container
governs, not only what its button says. Work/Cowork is a mode for doing the job,
not a replacement for the Project that holds the job. The ability to act also
does not imply permission to send, publish, purchase, delete or disclose.

### Reader question

Should I keep using the same AI chat, or start a new one? Does starting over
make the AI lose everything? Can one chat get too long or confused? How should
I organize my chats when I use AI for several parts of my life or one ongoing
project?

Frame this as **chat/session working practice**, not as a literal choice
between one chat and ten and not only as a context-window lesson. Introduce the
vocabulary plainly: products may call the same working conversation a chat,
conversation, thread or session, and those terms do not always mean exactly the
same thing across products.

### Core teaching promise

The number of chats is not the organizing principle. A chat is a working
conversation; it is not automatically a project, database, source of truth or
always-running worker. Use the same chat while you are still doing one
coherent job and the earlier discussion helps. Start a new chat when the job
changes, you want a clean second opinion, the old conversation has become
mixed or unwieldy, or information should be kept separate. For ongoing
projects, keep the durable brief, decisions, current status and outputs outside
the chat so a new conversation can recover the same truth.

### Format direction — one masterclass pathway, seven short narrated lessons

This subject needs visuals and narration because the learner must see changing
conversation and project state, not only read rules about it. A LIBRAiRY book
could preserve a later reference checklist, but it should not be the primary
teaching experience.

#### Lesson 3 — Choose Chat or Work/Cowork

Show the same recognizable job moving from conversation into execution. Use
Chat to explore, ask questions and produce the current brief. Use Work/Cowork
when the AI must carry out a bounded multi-step task across sources, files,
tools or apps. Keep both inside the same Project when they belong to the same
ongoing body of work, and show progress, a clarifying question, a direction
change and an important-action approval.

The portable rule is:

> Chat helps you think through the job. Work/Cowork takes on a bounded job.
> The Project is where that ongoing job lives.

#### Lesson 4 — From Chatting to Working

Show why friend-style back-and-forth is useful for discovery but may be a weak
complete workflow. Teach the sequence: orient → discover → brief → execute in
bounded stages → review → checkpoint → deliberately continue or reset.

Include the live-work decision people are rarely taught:

- **let it finish** when the assignment remains correct;
- **queue it** when the thought belongs after the current result;
- **steer now** when a missing fact or constraint can still repair the active
  work; and
- **interrupt/stop** when the premise is wrong or continuing risks a wrong,
  costly, private, external or substantially throwaway result.

Questions follow the same rule. “Can you make slides afterward?” queues.
“Did you use the approved source?” steers now if the current result depends on
it. “Stop—the audience is customers, not employees” interrupts because the
governing assignment is wrong. Demonstrate the tool's actual controls where
they exist, but teach the decision independently of any one product's button.

Open with a short **2025 versus now** correction. The old shorthand—one chat
holds the context, a new chat starts from zero, start over when the thread is
long—is no longer defensible as a universal rule. Current tools increasingly
use cross-chat memory/search, Projects or Notebooks, automatic context
management, branches, editable artifacts and longer-running Work/Cowork
sessions. Teach learners to inspect and control those sources rather than
pretending the improvements do not exist.

Keep the 2025 multi-turn study only as historical evidence of early commitment
and failure-recovery risk. Do not use its 39% average as a July 2026 product
performance claim. The current class claim is that workflow structure still
matters even though the products have materially better continuity and control.

#### Lesson 5 — Give the Work a Home

Show when an ongoing job needs a Project-style workspace, what belongs there,
why several purposeful chats may live inside one project, when a separate
project is justified and what no project feature guarantees. The exact product
feature differs, but the scoped-workspace concept is shared across leading
tools.

#### LAiDIES delivery rule

Do not make these feature lectures or generic screen tours. The learner should
watch a real current tool being set up for a recognizable job, understand what
each choice changes, test the setup and leave with a reusable project brief,
project structure or handoff.

Each class moves through:

> recognizable frustration → real screen → plain-language mechanism →
> meaningful setup choice → controlled test → common failure → portable rule

Exact menus, plans and feature labels are dated product demonstrations. The
mental model and decision skill are the durable lesson. Use LAiDIES narration,
visual comparison and Rewind Era references to make the invisible structure
legible without making the learner feel foolish for following an interface
that looks like instant messaging.

Use the sequence to show:

- one conversation collecting useful context as a task develops;
- the model committing to an early interpretation while essential requirements
  are still arriving piecemeal;
- unrelated requests entering until the chat is doing several jobs at once;
- the difference between starting a new chat with no handoff and starting one
  with a concise project brief;
- two separate chats producing conflicting answers because neither has been
  named the owner or given the current decision;
- a clean reviewer chat returning findings to the main working chat; and
- a Project-style workspace holding shared instructions and sources while
  research, drafting and review chats open, close and change;
- the durable approved artifact remaining authoritative rather than the latest
  confident chat response.

Narration should explain the job of each layer at every beat: personal setup,
project, chat/session, turn and durable artifact. On-screen labels,
colour-coded context cards, animated message windows and a stable project
binder should make the invisible workflow visible.

### Proposed lesson spine

1. **What a chat actually carries:** messages, attached/current context and
   tool access—not perfect permanent memory of the whole project.
2. **When one chat helps:** one evolving objective, shared decisions and a
   sequence where each step depends on the last.
3. **When a new chat helps:** a genuinely separate project, a clean-room
   review, a different specialist job, sensitive-context separation or a
   thread that has become so mixed that its brief can no longer be stated
   clearly.
4. **Why too many chats can fail:** duplicated instructions, contradictory
   versions, lost decisions and several chats each acting as if they own the
   final truth.
5. **Why one giant chat can fail:** unrelated jobs compete for attention,
   earlier requirements become compressed and the conversation becomes the
   idea inbox, plan, workbench and completion record at once.
6. **The practical structure:** one project home/source of truth; one
   foreground chat per coherent objective; optional bounded specialist/review
   chats; a written handoff containing the brief, relevant decisions, exact
   files/inputs, definition of done and return point.
7. **Useful test:** if two chats can make conflicting decisions about the same
   output, the structure is missing one named owner and one source of truth.

The research-backed behavioural rule is:

> Chat naturally. Work deliberately.

The user does not need to stop typing like herself. She needs to recognize when
the conversation has produced enough understanding to create or refresh the
working brief.

### Possible Rewind Era framing

Chats are like open instant-message windows; the project record is the shared
folder and production binder. Opening another window can separate a
conversation, but it does not automatically copy the binder, reconcile edits
or appoint an editor. The analogy stops at real-time human awareness: AI chats
may receive selected project context or tools, but they do not inherently share
complete state with one another.

### Practical takeaway

Teach a reusable starter pattern:

- `PROJECT HOME` — purpose, current status and links;
- `BRIEF` — the exact job and definition of done;
- `DECISIONS` — what has been ruled and why;
- `OUTPUTS` — the real files/artifacts;
- `NEXT / HANDOFF` — the exact resume point.

The class should include three worked setups: one-off personal questions,
several unrelated areas such as work/travel/personal admin, and one ongoing
project using a main working chat plus an optional clean reviewer chat. It
should finish with a simple decision tree:

- Same goal and earlier context is useful? Stay in the chat.
- Different goal or information should remain separate? Start a new chat.
- Same project but you want an unbiased critique? Start a clean review chat,
  then bring the findings back to the main owner.
- Important work you will need later? Save the brief, decisions and outputs
  outside the chat.

It should also distinguish separate chats from autonomous agents: another chat
does not run, coordinate or report back unless the product/workflow explicitly
provides that machinery.

### Product promise

The existing SUNNYVAiLE Tour Guide becomes a small, optional Rewind-Era
desktop-companion character. She already tracks the Welcome Tour and is named
in the trailer/site copy; this feature gives that existing role a consistent
face, voice and useful life outside the formal tour. She helps visitors discover
what LAiDIES contains without turning the homepage into a directory or making
the town feel overwhelming.

It has three jobs:

1. **Wayfinding:** answer questions such as “Where is the latest episode?”,
   “Where is Girl Talk?” and “How do I find the quizzes?” with one direct answer
   and a working destination.
2. **Surprise and discovery:** offer dismissible “Did you know?” cards drawn
   from verified LAiDIES content—women-in-computing facts, useful AI lessons,
   hidden town experiences and timely suggestions.
3. **Warm handoff:** route substantive AI/content questions to the correct
   authority instead of pretending to know everything—for example, Miss Jeeves
   for sourced Library answers and the Visitor’s Centre for an orientation
   route.
4. **Responsive companionship:** notice a small set of meaningful on-site
   actions and respond with encouragement, recognition or the next useful
   discovery.

### Reaction model

The Tour Guide may react when a visitor:

- begins, pauses, wanders from or completes the Welcome Tour;
- opens her first episode or returns for the newest one;
- finishes an episode, quiz, study pack or other meaningful weekly action;
- earns a real stamp, charm, sticker or Butterfly Clip;
- discovers a hidden town experience;
- visits several buildings but appears not to know where the next part of the
  weekly route lives; or
- returns after time away and has a genuinely new episode or town feature to
  discover.

Her reactions should be short, specific and useful. Examples:

- “First tape off the shelf. Excellent choice.”
- “You passed the quiz. I’m legally required to call that permanent-record
  material.”
- “You’ve found three buildings without asking for directions. I’ll pretend
  not to be professionally threatened.”
- “The newest episode is waiting at The Chick Flicks. Want the direct route?”

The first implementation should react only to explicit project-owned events,
not infer emotions, identity, intent or private behaviour.

### Recommended product boundary

This is a **town guide, not a general-purpose chatbot**. Its first version should
use a deterministic site catalogue, verified fact bank and authored answer
patterns. Natural-language AI may later interpret a visitor's wording, but the
destination, fact, source and freshness date must come from project-owned
structured data.

Every response should have:

- one useful answer first;
- one clear action or destination;
- an optional “Why this?” or source receipt;
- an honest “I couldn't find that” state with a route to the Visitor’s Centre;
  and
- no invented pages, availability claims or historical facts.

### Experience rules

- Small and collapsible; never cover reading, captions, forms or navigation.
- Quiet by default. No constant bouncing, unsolicited audio or repetitive
  pop-ups.
- Context-aware suggestions are allowed, but the user controls whether it
  opens.
- Reactions are event-triggered, rate-limited and deduplicated. One meaningful
  action may produce one response; ordinary clicks and scrolling produce none.
- The visitor can mute reactions, dismiss the guide or leave the formal tour
  without losing access to site navigation.
- Avoid guilt language, streak anxiety, surveillance phrasing and commentary
  about how long someone spent on a page.
- Returning visitors should not receive the same fact repeatedly.
- Facts about real people require the existing facts-and-citations authority.
- Site-availability answers must come from the same catalogue/status truth used
  by navigation so parked or coming-soon features are described honestly.
- It should feel like a Tamagotchi/desktop pet that learned the SUNNYVAiLE map,
  not Clippy interrupting a spreadsheet.

### Possible later reward connection

Helpful discoveries could occasionally reveal a stamp, charm or Butterfly Clip,
but ordinary navigation must never require a reward action. The guide's value
is finding and learning, not manufacturing another chore.

## Learning that meets women where they are

  change the conclusion.

outcome and the next experience. Any saved preference is voluntary,
domain-specific, editable and private. Domain expertise must be treated as an
asset rather than erased by an AI skill label.
